One Theorem to Replace Four

The fundamental theorem of calculus, Green's theorem, the divergence theorem and Kelvin–Stokes are four statements of the same fact — once you have the right language.

That language is differential forms: the things you integrate. A \(k\)-form eats \(k\) vectors and returns the signed \(k\)-volume they span, so integration is built into the object.

Everything rests on Chapter 5's alternating multilinear forms, carried point by point across a manifold.

Layer 1 · The algebra

Exterior Algebra

Chapter 5's alternating forms, organised.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Alternating \(k\)-form — a multilinear map \(V^{k}\to\mathbb{R}\) vanishing when two arguments coincide.

    It measures the signed \(k\)-volume of the box spanned by its \(k\) inputs.

  • The space \(\Lambda^{k}V^{*}\) — all alternating \(k\)-forms on \(V\).

    \(\Lambda^{0}\) is the scalars, \(\Lambda^{1}\) is the dual space, \(\Lambda^{n}\) is a line — the determinants.

  • Wedge product \(\alpha\wedge\beta\) — the alternating product of forms.

    Combine a \(k\)-box measurer and an \(l\)-box measurer into a \((k+l)\)-box measurer.

  • Decomposable form \(\alpha_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\alpha_k\).

    Its value on \(k\) vectors is exactly \(\det(\alpha_i(v_j))\) — the determinant, back again.

  • Basis of \(\Lambda^{k}\) — \(dx^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{i_k}\) with \(i_1<\cdots<i_k\).

    Choose \(k\) coordinate directions out of \(n\). That's why the dimension is a binomial coefficient.

  • Exterior algebra \(\Lambda^{\bullet}V^{*} = \bigoplus_k\Lambda^{k}V^{*}\).

    All degrees at once, with \(\wedge\) as multiplication. Its total dimension is \(2^{n}\) — one for each subset of the coordinates.

  • Interior product \(\iota_v\omega = \omega(v,\cdot,\dots,\cdot)\).

    Feed in one vector and drop the degree by one. It converts flux forms into fluxes.

  • Pullback of a form \((f^{*}\omega)(v_1,\dots,v_k) = \omega(df\,v_1,\dots,df\,v_k)\).

    Push the vectors forward, then measure. Forms pull back where vectors push forward.

Theorems

  • \(\dim\Lambda^{k}V^{*} = \binom nk\), and \(\Lambda^{k} = 0\) for \(k > n\).

    You cannot span a 4-dimensional box with 3-dimensional room. Beyond degree \(n\) everything vanishes.

    An alternating form is determined by its values on increasing tuples of basis vectors, since permuting arguments only changes the sign and repeats give 0. There are \(\binom nk\) such tuples, and the corresponding decomposable forms are independent (evaluate each on its own tuple). For \(k > n\) every tuple repeats an index, so the form is 0. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Wedge is associative, bilinear, and graded-commutative: \(\alpha\wedge\beta = (-1)^{kl}\beta\wedge\alpha\).

    Two 1-forms anticommute: \(dx\wedge dy = -dy\wedge dx\). Even-degree forms commute with everything.

    On decomposables the wedge is a determinant-like sum over shuffles, and swapping a block of \(k\) arguments past a block of \(l\) requires \(kl\) transpositions, each contributing \(-1\) (Chapter 5, Layer 1). Associativity and bilinearity are checked on the basis, where both sides are the same signed sum. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Corollary: \(\alpha\wedge\alpha = 0\) for any \(\alpha\) of odd degree.

    \(dx\wedge dx = 0\). The single most-used simplification in the whole subject.

    Graded commutativity with \(k = l\) odd gives \(\alpha\wedge\alpha = -\alpha\wedge\alpha\), so \(2\alpha\wedge\alpha = 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Decomposables and determinants: \((\alpha_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\alpha_k)(v_1,\dots,v_k) = \det\bigl(\alpha_i(v_j)\bigr)\).

    The wedge is the determinant. Chapter 5's uniqueness theorem is the reason.

    Both sides are multilinear and alternating in the \(v_j\), so by Chapter 5, Layer 2 they agree up to a constant; evaluating on a dual basis fixes the constant at 1. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Independence test: \(v_1,\dots,v_k\) are linearly independent iff some decomposable \(k\)-form is non-zero on them.

    Non-zero \(k\)-volume means genuinely \(k\)-dimensional — the geometric reading of independence.

    If the vectors are dependent, every alternating form vanishes on them (Chapter 5, Layer 2). If independent, extend to a basis and take the corresponding dual decomposable, which evaluates to 1. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Top-degree transformation: for a linear \(T : V\to V\), \(T^{*}\omega = (\det T)\,\omega\) on \(\Lambda^{n}\).

    Where the change-of-variables factor comes from. It is not inserted by hand; it is what forms do.

    \(\Lambda^{n}V^{*}\) is one-dimensional (previous theorem), so \(T^{*}\omega = c\,\omega\) for a scalar \(c\). Evaluating on a basis gives \(c = \omega(Te_1,\dots,Te_n)/\omega(e_1,\dots,e_n) = \det T\) by Chapter 5. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Pullback is an algebra homomorphism: \(f^{*}(\alpha\wedge\beta) = f^{*}\alpha\wedge f^{*}\beta\), and \((g\circ f)^{*} = f^{*}\circ g^{*}\).

    Change coordinates and every wedge formula survives unchanged. This is why forms are the right objects.

    Both statements follow by evaluating on vectors: the pullback simply pre-composes each argument with \(df\), and the shuffle sum defining \(\wedge\) commutes with that substitution. The composition rule is the chain rule \(d(g\circ f) = dg\circ df\) (Chapter 15, Layer 2). \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Expand in the \(dx^{I}\) basis — with increasing indices only.

    Sort every term into increasing order, tracking the sign. That's the entire computational technique.

  • Kill repeats — any wedge with a repeated 1-form is zero.

    Most terms in an expansion vanish immediately.

  • Read a wedge as a determinant.

    Converts an unfamiliar expression into a familiar computation.

Layer 2 · Forms that vary

Differential Forms & \(d\)

One operator that contains grad, curl and div.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Differential \(k\)-form — a smooth choice of \(\omega_p\in\Lambda^{k}T_p^{*}M\).

    A \(k\)-volume measurer attached to every point, varying smoothly.

  • 0-forms and 1-forms — functions; and objects like \(df = \sum_i\partial_if\,dx^{i}\).

    A 1-form eats a direction and returns a number: exactly what a gradient does, without needing a metric.

  • Exterior derivative \(d\) — the unique degree-raising operator with \(d^{2}=0\) extending \(f\mapsto df\).

    One operator; in \(\mathbb{R}^{3}\) it is gradient, curl and divergence in degrees 0, 1 and 2.

  • Closed form — \(d\omega = 0\).

    Curl-free, or divergence-free, depending on the degree.

  • Exact form — \(\omega = d\eta\) for some \(\eta\).

    Has a potential. Every exact form is closed; the converse depends on the shape of the space.

  • de Rham cohomology \(H^{k} = \dfrac{\text{closed }k\text{-forms}}{\text{exact }k\text{-forms}}\).

    Measures the holes. Calculus detecting topology.

  • Contractible space — continuously shrinkable to a point.

    A ball is; a punctured plane is not — and that is why \(d\theta\) has no global potential.

  • Volume form — a nowhere-vanishing top-degree form.

    Exists exactly on orientable manifolds (Chapter 15, Layer 4). It is what you integrate to get volume.

Theorems

  • Existence and uniqueness of \(d\): there is exactly one operator that is linear, satisfies the graded Leibniz rule, agrees with \(f\mapsto df\) on functions, and has \(d^{2} = 0\).

    Not a definition chosen for convenience — the properties force the formula.

    In a chart, the four properties force \(d\bigl(\sum_Ia_I\,dx^{I}\bigr) = \sum_Ida_I\wedge dx^{I}\): Leibniz splits the product, \(d^{2}=0\) kills \(d(dx^{I})\), and linearity assembles the terms. That formula is then checked to satisfy all four properties, and chart-independence follows from uniqueness on overlaps. \(\blacksquare\)

  • \(d^{2} = 0\).

    "The boundary of a boundary is empty", in algebraic form. It is Clairaut's theorem wearing a new hat.

    For a function, \(d(df) = \sum_{i,j}\partial_j\partial_if\,dx^{j}\wedge dx^{i}\). The coefficients are symmetric in \(i,j\) (Clairaut, Chapter 10, Layer 1) while \(dx^{j}\wedge dx^{i}\) is antisymmetric, so the terms cancel in pairs. The general case follows from the Leibniz rule and this special case. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Corollary: exact \(\Rightarrow\) closed.

    Every gradient field is curl-free. The converse is where topology enters.

    If \(\omega = d\eta\) then \(d\omega = d^{2}\eta = 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Vector calculus dictionary: in \(\mathbb{R}^{3}\), \(d\) on 0-, 1- and 2-forms is \(\operatorname{grad}\), \(\operatorname{curl}\), \(\operatorname{div}\).

    And \(d^{2}=0\) becomes the two identities \(\operatorname{curl}\operatorname{grad} = 0\) and \(\operatorname{div}\operatorname{curl} = 0\).

    Identify a vector field \(F\) with the 1-form \(\omega_F = F_1dx+F_2dy+F_3dz\) and with the 2-form \(\eta_F = F_1dy\wedge dz + F_2dz\wedge dx + F_3dx\wedge dy\). Computing \(df\), \(d\omega_F\) and \(d\eta_F\) in coordinates reproduces exactly the gradient, curl and divergence formulas. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Pullback commutes with \(d\): \(f^{*}(d\omega) = d(f^{*}\omega)\).

    Differentiation is coordinate-free. Every formula you prove in one chart holds in all of them.

    For a function, \(f^{*}(dg) = d(g\circ f)\) is the chain rule. For a general form, write it in coordinates as a sum of \(a_I\,dx^{I}\), apply Layer 1's homomorphism property of \(f^{*}\), and use the function case on each coefficient. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Poincaré lemma: on a contractible open set, every closed form is exact.

    No holes, no obstruction: a curl-free field on a ball always has a potential.

    Sketch: let \(H(t,x)\) be a smooth contraction to a point. Define the homotopy operator \(K\omega = \int_0^{1}\iota_{\partial_t}H^{*}\omega\,dt\). A computation using \(d\) commuting with pullback and the Leibniz rule gives Cartan's homotopy formula \(dK\omega + Kd\omega = \omega\). If \(d\omega = 0\), then \(\omega = d(K\omega)\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Closed but not exact: \(\omega = \frac{-y\,dx + x\,dy}{x^{2}+y^{2}}\) on \(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}\).

    The angle form. It measures winding; the puncture is exactly what stops it having a global potential.

    A direct computation gives \(d\omega = 0\). If \(\omega = df\) then \(\oint_{S^{1}}\omega = f(\text{end})-f(\text{start}) = 0\); but parametrising the unit circle gives \(\oint\omega = 2\pi\). So no potential exists, and \(H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\})\ne0\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Homotopy invariance: homotopic maps induce the same map on de Rham cohomology.

    Cohomology cannot see continuous deformation — which is what makes it a topological invariant.

    The same homotopy operator: if \(f\simeq g\) via \(H\), then \(g^{*}\omega - f^{*}\omega = d(K\omega)+K(d\omega)\). On closed forms the second term vanishes, so the two pullbacks differ by an exact form and agree in cohomology. \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Compute \(d\) by differentiating coefficients and wedging.

    \(d(a\,dx\wedge dy) = da\wedge dx\wedge dy\); only the \(dz\) term survives in \(\mathbb{R}^{3}\).

  • Check closedness first — it is necessary for exactness and cheap to test.

    If \(d\omega \ne 0\), stop looking for a potential.

  • Integrate around a loop to detect a hole.

    A non-zero loop integral of a closed form proves the form is not exact.

  • Use the dictionary — translate vector calculus into forms and back.

    Identities that take a page in coordinates become one line in forms.

Layer 3 · What integration really is

Integration of Forms

Why the change-of-variables factor comes for free.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Integral of a \(k\)-form over a \(k\)-manifold \(\displaystyle\int_M\omega\).

    Degrees must match: you integrate a 2-form over a surface, not over a curve.

  • Chart computation — \(\int_{\varphi(U)}a(x)\,dx^{1}\cdots dx^{k}\) for \(\omega = a\,dx^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{k}\).

    Strip the wedges, integrate the coefficient with Chapter 12. That is the whole definition.

  • Orientation-compatible charts — required for the sign to be consistent.

    Reverse the orientation and every integral changes sign — unlike Chapter 12's unsigned measure.

  • Integration over a chain — a formal sum of parametrised pieces.

    Chop a surface into patches with signs, integrate over each, and add.

  • Volume form of a metric \(\sqrt{\det g}\;dx^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{k}\).

    Chapter 14's \(\sqrt{EG-F^{2}}\), now as a form. Chapter 17 supplies \(g\).

  • Flux — the integral of a 2-form over a surface in \(\mathbb{R}^{3}\).

    How much fluid crosses the surface per second. The 2-form does the "per unit area, in the normal direction" bookkeeping.

Theorems

  • The integral is well defined: independent of the oriented atlas and the partition of unity.

    Different charts, same answer — because the pullback of a top form already carries the Jacobian.

    On an overlap, \(\psi^{*}(a\,dx^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{k}) = (a\circ\psi)(\det D\psi)\,du^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge du^{k}\) by Layer 1's top-degree transformation rule. Since the atlas is oriented, \(\det D\psi > 0\), so this matches the factor \(|\det D\psi|\) in Chapter 12's change of variables formula exactly. Independence of the partition of unity is the same double-sum argument as in Chapter 15, Layer 4. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Change of variables is automatic: \(\displaystyle\int_{f(M)}\omega = \int_M f^{*}\omega\) for an orientation-preserving diffeomorphism.

    Chapter 12's \(|\det DT|\) is not a correction bolted on — it is what a top-degree form does under pullback.

    Work in charts: the left side is the coordinate integral of the coefficient of \(\omega\), and the right side that of the coefficient of \(f^{*}\omega\), which is \((a\circ f)\det Df\). Chapter 12, Layer 4 says these agree, the sign being handled by orientation preservation. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Orientation reversal flips the sign: \(\int_{-M}\omega = -\int_M\omega\).

    Traverse a curve backwards and the line integral changes sign. Chapter 11's measure could not express this.

    Reversing orientation means composing charts with a reflection, whose Jacobian determinant is \(-1\); the top-form transformation rule then multiplies every local contribution by \(-1\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Forms vs densities: \(\int_M f\,\mathrm{vol}_g\) reproduces Chapter 14's surface area.

    The two constructions agree wherever both apply — as they must.

    In a chart the volume form's coefficient is \(\sqrt{\det g} = \sqrt{\det(D\Phi^{\mathsf T}D\Phi)}\), which is exactly the Gram-determinant factor of Chapter 14, Layer 4. The two integrals therefore have identical integrands. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Line and surface integrals recovered: \(\int_\gamma\omega_F = \int F\cdot T\,ds\) and \(\int_S\eta_F = \int F\cdot N\,dA\).

    Work done along a path; flux through a surface. The classical formulas are pullbacks.

    Pull \(\omega_F\) back by \(\gamma\): \(\gamma^{*}\omega_F = \langle F(\gamma(t)),\gamma'(t)\rangle dt = \langle F,T\rangle\|\gamma'\|dt\), whose integral is the line integral. Similarly \(\Phi^{*}\eta_F = \langle F,\Phi_u\times\Phi_v\rangle du\wedge dv = \langle F,N\rangle\sqrt{EG-F^{2}}\,du\wedge dv\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Only top-degree forms integrate over \(M\); lower degrees integrate over submanifolds.

    Degree = dimension. It is the rule that makes every formula in this chapter type-check.

    In a \(k\)-dimensional chart, a form of degree \(< k\) has no \(dx^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{k}\) component to strip, and one of degree \(> k\) is identically zero (Layer 1). Restricting a \(j\)-form to a \(j\)-dimensional submanifold — that is, pulling it back along the inclusion — makes it top-degree there. \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Pull back to the parameter domain, then integrate as in Chapter 12.

    Substitute, expand the wedges, strip them, integrate. Always in that order.

  • Fix the orientation first.

    Half the sign errors in this subject come from doing this last.

  • Match degree to dimension — a quick sanity check.

    If they don't match, the expression is meaningless, not merely wrong.

  • Use the volume form for unsigned quantities.

    Area, mass and average values want a density, not a signed integral.

Layer 4 · The theorem

Stokes' Theorem

Everything in the interior is determined by the edge.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Compact support — the form vanishes outside a compact set.

    Keeps the integrals finite and stops mass escaping to infinity. The one hypothesis that cannot be dropped.

  • Induced boundary orientation — outward normal first.

    Chapter 15's convention. It is exactly what makes the theorem's sign come out positive.

  • Closed manifold — compact, with empty boundary.

    A sphere or a torus. On these, \(\int_Md\omega = 0\) always.

  • Conservative field — one whose line integral depends only on the endpoints.

    Gravity. The work done depends on the height climbed, not the route.

  • Winding number & degree — integrals of forms that count wrapping.

    Calculus counting topology, made possible by Stokes.

Theorems

  • Stokes' theorem: for a compactly supported \((k-1)\)-form \(\omega\) on an oriented \(k\)-manifold with boundary, \[\int_Md\omega = \int_{\partial M}\omega.\]

    Add up the local change over the whole region and only the edge survives. Everything inside cancels against its neighbour.

    Sketch. Using a partition of unity, reduce to \(\omega\) supported in a single chart, so the claim becomes a statement on \(\mathbb{R}^{k}\) or the half-space \(\{x_k\ge0\}\). Write \(\omega = \sum_i a_i\,dx^{1}\wedge\cdots\widehat{dx^{i}}\cdots\wedge dx^{k}\); then \(d\omega = \sum_i(-1)^{i-1}\partial_ia_i\,dx^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{k}\). Integrating each term with Fubini (Chapter 12, Layer 3) and applying the one-dimensional fundamental theorem of calculus in the \(i\)-th variable, every term vanishes by compact support except the one differentiating across the boundary face \(x_k = 0\) — which is precisely \(\int_{\partial M}\omega\), with the induced orientation supplying the sign. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Corollary — fundamental theorem of calculus: \(k=1\), \(M = [a,b]\).

    \(\int_a^bf' = f(b)-f(a)\). The boundary of an interval is two points, with opposite signs.

    Take \(\omega = f\), a 0-form; then \(d\omega = f'\,dx\) and \(\partial M = \{b\}-\{a\}\), so Stokes reads \(\int_a^bf'\,dx = f(b)-f(a)\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Corollary — Green's theorem: \(\displaystyle\oint_{\partial D}(P\,dx+Q\,dy) = \iint_D(Q_x - P_y)\,dx\,dy\).

    A planimeter measures the area of a shape by tracing its outline. This is why it works.

    Take \(\omega = P\,dx+Q\,dy\) on a plane region. Then \(d\omega = (Q_x-P_y)\,dx\wedge dy\), and Stokes gives the identity. Choosing \(P = -y/2\), \(Q = x/2\) makes the right side the area. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Corollary — Kelvin–Stokes: \(\displaystyle\oint_{\partial S}F\cdot dr = \iint_S(\operatorname{curl}F)\cdot N\,dA\).

    The circulation around the rim equals the total swirl inside.

    Take \(\omega = \omega_F\), the 1-form of \(F\). By Layer 2's dictionary \(d\omega_F = \eta_{\operatorname{curl}F}\), and Layer 3 identifies \(\int_S\eta_{\operatorname{curl}F}\) with the flux integral and \(\oint_{\partial S}\omega_F\) with the circulation. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Corollary — divergence theorem: \(\displaystyle\iiint_V\operatorname{div}F\,dV = \iint_{\partial V}F\cdot N\,dA\).

    What flows out through the skin equals what is created inside. Conservation of mass, as a theorem.

    Take \(\omega = \eta_F\), the 2-form of \(F\). Then \(d\eta_F = (\operatorname{div}F)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz\) by the dictionary, and Stokes gives the identity, with Layer 3 identifying the boundary integral as the flux. \(\blacksquare\)

  • On a closed manifold, \(\int_Md\omega = 0\).

    No boundary, no leakage. This is what makes de Rham cohomology in top degree meaningful.

    \(\partial M = \varnothing\), so the right side of Stokes is an integral over the empty set, which is 0. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Corollary — a volume form on a closed orientable manifold is never exact.

    So \(H^{k}(M)\ne0\): the total volume is a cohomological obstruction.

    If \(\mathrm{vol} = d\eta\), then \(\int_M\mathrm{vol} = \int_Md\eta = 0\) by the previous result. But \(\int_M\mathrm{vol} > 0\) since the form is nowhere zero and positively oriented. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Conservative fields: on a simply connected domain, curl-free implies path-independent implies gradient.

    Three equivalent statements — and the punctured plane shows why "simply connected" is required.

    Curl-free means \(d\omega_F = 0\); on a simply connected domain the Poincaré lemma (Layer 2) gives \(\omega_F = df\), i.e. \(F = \nabla f\). Then Stokes in the \(k=1\) case makes every line integral \(f(\text{end})-f(\text{start})\), which is path-independent. Conversely path-independence defines a potential by integration from a fixed base point. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Integration by parts on a manifold: \(\int_M d\alpha\wedge\beta = \int_{\partial M}\alpha\wedge\beta - (-1)^{\deg\alpha}\int_M\alpha\wedge d\beta\).

    Chapter 3's integration by parts, in full generality — the workhorse of every partial differential equation.

    Apply Stokes to \(\omega = \alpha\wedge\beta\) and expand \(d(\alpha\wedge\beta) = d\alpha\wedge\beta + (-1)^{\deg\alpha}\alpha\wedge d\beta\) by the graded Leibniz rule. \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Translate the problem into forms, apply Stokes, translate back.

    The four classical theorems become one application.

  • Swap a hard interior integral for an easy boundary one — or vice versa.

    Whichever side is simpler. That choice is the entire practical value of the theorem.

  • Check compact support and orientation.

    The two hypotheses people forget, and the two that produce wrong answers.

  • Use \(\int_Md\omega = 0\) on closed manifolds to derive obstructions.

    The quickest way to prove that something cannot exist.
