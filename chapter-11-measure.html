What Volume Actually Is

A box has volume "length \(\times\) width \(\times\) height". This chapter asks the real question: what is the volume of an arbitrary set in \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\)?

The answer is Lebesgue measure. It agrees with the obvious answer on boxes, adds up over countably many disjoint pieces, and doesn't move when you move the set.

The surprise is that no such notion can measure every set — and the Axiom of Choice is why.

Layer 1 · The obvious start

Boxes & Elementary Volume

Where the naive approach gets to, and where it stops.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Box \(Q = I_1\times\cdots\times I_n\) — a product of bounded intervals.

    A brick with faces parallel to the axes. The only shape whose volume is not in question.

  • Volume of a box \(|Q| = \prod_i(b_i-a_i)\).

    Length \(\times\) width \(\times\) height. Everything else in this chapter is built to extend this one formula.

  • Elementary set — a finite union of boxes.

    Anything you can build out of bricks. Its volume is unambiguous once you cut the bricks apart.

  • Jordan inner & outer content — best volume from boxes inside, and from finitely many boxes outside.

    Fill the shape with bricks; then cover it with bricks. If the two totals meet, you have a volume.

  • Jordan measurable — inner and outer content agree.

    The rigorous version of "the area under a Riemann-integrable function".

  • Null set (measure zero) — coverable by countably many boxes of arbitrarily small total volume.

    A line in the plane, a point, all of \(\mathbb{Q}\). "Countably" is what makes this notion powerful.

  • Almost everywhere (a.e.) — except on a null set.

    Statements in the rest of this book hold a.e. rather than everywhere — and nothing is lost.

Theorems

  • Volume of a box is well defined: partitioning a box into sub-boxes adds up correctly.

    Cut a brick and the pieces still total the brick. Not quite as trivial as it sounds.

    For a grid partition, splitting each side interval \(I_i\) into pieces of lengths \(\ell_{i,k}\) gives \(\sum_{k_1,\dots,k_n}\prod_i\ell_{i,k_i} = \prod_i\sum_{k}\ell_{i,k} = \prod_i|I_i|\) by expanding the product. Any partition into boxes can be refined to a common grid, and both sides of the identity are unchanged by refinement. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Elementary sets form an algebra: closed under finite unions, intersections and differences.

    Brickwork stays brickwork under the finite operations — but not under countable ones.

    An intersection of two boxes is a box. A difference of boxes is a finite union of boxes (cut along the faces). Unions are by definition. Finite combinations of these operations therefore stay inside the class. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Countable unions of null sets are null.

    Countably many negligible things stay negligible. This is exactly what Jordan content cannot deliver.

    Cover the \(k\)-th null set by boxes of total volume \(< \varepsilon2^{-k}\). The combined family is countable and has total volume \(< \sum_k\varepsilon2^{-k} = \varepsilon\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • \(\mathbb{Q}^{n}\) is null; so is every countable set, and every hyperplane.

    The rationals fill the line densely and still have length zero. Density and size are unrelated.

    A single point is contained in a box of volume \(<\varepsilon\), so it is null, and a countable union of null sets is null. A hyperplane \(\{x_i = c\}\) meets each bounded region in a slab of thickness \(\delta\) and volume \(\to0\); exhaust \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\) by countably many bounded regions. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Jordan content fails on \(\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]\): inner content 0, outer content 1.

    A countable set with "length 1" by the finite theory, and length 0 by the countable one. The finite theory has to go.

    The set contains no interval, so any finite union of boxes inside it is degenerate and the inner content is 0. Any finite union of closed boxes covering it covers a dense subset of \([0,1]\), so by connectedness it covers \([0,1]\), forcing outer content 1. \(\blacksquare\)

  • The Cantor set is uncountable and null.

    "Null" is far weaker than "countable" — the two notions of small come apart completely.

    At stage \(k\) the set is covered by \(2^{k}\) intervals of length \(3^{-k}\), total \((2/3)^{k}\to0\), so it is null. Uncountability was shown in Chapter 8, Layer 3. \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Approximate from outside by boxes — the definition of outer measure to come.

    Everything in Layer 3 is this idea, with "finitely many" replaced by "countably many".

  • Use \(\varepsilon2^{-k}\) — split a tolerance across a countable family.

    The single most-used trick in measure theory.

  • Ignore null sets — prove statements almost everywhere.

    It costs nothing and removes almost all the pathologies.

Layer 2 · The right domain

\(\sigma\)-Algebras & Measures

Which collections of sets can carry a volume, and what a volume must do.

Concepts & Definitions

  • \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{A}\) — contains \(\varnothing\), closed under complement and countable union.

    The list of sets you are allowed to measure. Countable operations must not take you off the list.

  • Generated \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\sigma(\mathcal{E})\) — the smallest one containing \(\mathcal E\).

    Start from the sets you care about and close up under the operations.

  • Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{B}\) — generated by the open sets.

    Every set you can describe by countably many unions, intersections and complements of open sets. That is a very large class.

  • Measure \(\mu\) — \(\mu(\varnothing)=0\), values in \([0,\infty]\), countably additive on disjoint sets.

    Cut a region into countably many disjoint pieces and the volumes add. Just that.

  • Measure space \((X,\mathcal A,\mu)\).

    A set, a list of measurable subsets, and the volume function.

  • Finite / \(\sigma\)-finite measure.

    \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\) has infinite volume but is a countable union of boxes — \(\sigma\)-finite, which is all most theorems need.

  • Complete measure — every subset of a null set is measurable.

    Lebesgue measure is complete; the Borel measure is not, which is the technical reason Lebesgue's \(\sigma\)-algebra is bigger.

  • Counting measure & Dirac measure.

    "How many points"; "is the point here". Both are measures — the theory is not only about volume.

Theorems

  • \(\sigma\)-algebras are closed under countable intersections and differences.

    The two axioms already give all the countable set operations.

    \(\bigcap_kA_k = \bigl(\bigcup_kA_k^{c}\bigr)^{c}\) by De Morgan (Chapter 2, Layer 3), and \(A\setminus B = A\cap B^{c}\). Both stay in \(\mathcal A\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Arbitrary intersections of \(\sigma\)-algebras are \(\sigma\)-algebras, so \(\sigma(\mathcal E)\) exists.

    The "smallest containing" construction, exactly as for spans and closures.

    If each \(\mathcal A_i\) contains \(\varnothing\) and is closed under complements and countable unions, so is \(\bigcap_i\mathcal A_i\), since each operation can be checked inside every \(\mathcal A_i\). The family of \(\sigma\)-algebras containing \(\mathcal E\) is non-empty (it contains \(\mathcal P(X)\)), so their intersection is the smallest. \(\blacksquare\)

  • \(\mathcal B(\mathbb{R}^{n})\) is generated by the open boxes, indeed by the boxes with rational corners.

    Countably many generators suffice for the entire Borel class.

    Every open set is a countable union of rational boxes (Chapter 8, Layer 1), so it lies in the \(\sigma\)-algebra generated by them; hence \(\mathcal B\) is contained in that one. Conversely boxes are open (or countable intersections of open sets), so the containment is an equality. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Monotonicity and countable subadditivity: \(A\subseteq B \Rightarrow \mu(A)\le\mu(B)\), and \(\mu(\bigcup_kA_k) \le \sum_k\mu(A_k)\).

    A part is never bigger than the whole, and overlaps only shrink the total.

    \(\mu(B) = \mu(A) + \mu(B\setminus A) \ge \mu(A)\). For subadditivity, disjointify: \(B_k = A_k\setminus\bigcup_{j<k}A_j\) are disjoint with the same union and \(B_k \subseteq A_k\), so \(\mu(\bigcup A_k) = \sum_k\mu(B_k) \le \sum_k\mu(A_k)\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Continuity from below: \(A_k\uparrow A\) implies \(\mu(A_k)\to\mu(A)\).

    Fill a region with a growing sequence of pieces and the volumes climb to the total.

    Set \(B_1 = A_1\) and \(B_k = A_k\setminus A_{k-1}\); these are disjoint with \(\bigcup_{j\le k}B_j = A_k\) and \(\bigcup_jB_j = A\). Countable additivity gives \(\mu(A) = \sum_j\mu(B_j) = \lim_k\sum_{j\le k}\mu(B_j) = \lim_k\mu(A_k)\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Continuity from above: \(A_k\downarrow A\) with \(\mu(A_1)<\infty\) implies \(\mu(A_k)\to\mu(A)\) — the finiteness is necessary.

    \([k,\infty)\) shrinks to \(\varnothing\), yet every one has infinite measure. Drop the hypothesis and the theorem is false.

    Apply continuity from below to \(A_1\setminus A_k \uparrow A_1\setminus A\): \(\mu(A_1)-\mu(A_k)\to\mu(A_1)-\mu(A)\), and finiteness of \(\mu(A_1)\) lets us cancel it. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Borel–Cantelli: if \(\sum_k\mu(A_k)<\infty\), then almost every point lies in only finitely many \(A_k\).

    If the total size of the warnings is finite, almost nothing gets warned infinitely often.

    Let \(A = \bigcap_N\bigcup_{k\ge N}A_k\) be the set of points in infinitely many \(A_k\). For every \(N\), \(\mu(A) \le \sum_{k\ge N}\mu(A_k)\), which tends to 0 as \(N\to\infty\) because the series converges. So \(\mu(A)=0\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Completion exists: every measure space has a smallest complete extension.

    Add all the subsets of null sets and declare them null. Lebesgue measure is the completion of Borel measure.

    Let \(\overline{\mathcal A} = \{A\cup Z : A\in\mathcal A,\ Z \subseteq N \text{ for some null } N\in\mathcal A\}\), and set \(\overline\mu(A\cup Z) = \mu(A)\). One checks \(\overline{\mathcal A}\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra and \(\overline\mu\) is well defined (two representations differ by a null set) and countably additive. It is contained in any complete extension. \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Disjointify — replace \(A_k\) by \(A_k\setminus\bigcup_{j<k}A_j\).

    Turns any countable union into a disjoint one, so additivity applies.

  • Generate from a convenient family — prove things on boxes, then extend.

    The standard route: check on a generating class closed under intersections, then invoke uniqueness.

  • Monotone limits — use continuity from below to pass to the limit.

    And always check finiteness before using continuity from above.

  • Borel–Cantelli — to prove something happens only finitely often.

    Sum the measures; if the series converges, you're done.

Layer 3 · The construction

Lebesgue Measure

Volume in \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\), built and characterised.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Lebesgue outer measure \(\lambda^{*}(A) = \inf\bigl\{\sum_k|Q_k| : A \subseteq \bigcup_kQ_k\bigr\}\) over countable box covers.

    The cheapest countable brick cover. Defined for every set — but not additive on all of them.

  • Carathéodory criterion — \(E\) is measurable if \(\lambda^{*}(A) = \lambda^{*}(A\cap E) + \lambda^{*}(A\setminus E)\) for every \(A\).

    A set is measurable when it cuts every other set cleanly, with no loss at the seam.

  • Lebesgue measurable sets \(\mathcal L\) — those passing the criterion.

    Contains every Borel set, plus every subset of a null set. Strictly bigger than \(\mathcal B\).

  • Lebesgue measure \(\lambda\) — \(\lambda^{*}\) restricted to \(\mathcal L\).

    The volume this whole book has been building towards.

  • Translation invariance — \(\lambda(E+v) = \lambda(E)\).

    Moving a shape doesn't change its size. Together with normalisation, this pins \(\lambda\) down uniquely.

  • Regularity — approximation from outside by open sets and inside by compact ones.

    Every measurable set is nearly open and nearly compact. This is what makes proofs possible.

  • \(G_\delta\) and \(F_\sigma\) sets — countable intersections of open sets; countable unions of closed sets.

    The first two floors above open and closed. Every measurable set differs from one of these by a null set.

Theorems

  • \(\lambda^{*}\) is an outer measure: monotone, countably subadditive, and zero on \(\varnothing\).

    These hold for all sets. Additivity is what has to be earned.

    Monotonicity: a cover of the bigger set covers the smaller. Subadditivity: cover \(A_k\) with total volume \(<\lambda^{*}(A_k)+\varepsilon2^{-k}\); the combined countable family covers \(\bigcup_kA_k\) with total \(<\sum_k\lambda^{*}(A_k)+\varepsilon\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • \(\lambda^{*}\) agrees with volume on boxes.

    The construction doesn't change the answer where the answer was already known. Less obvious than it looks.

    \(\le\) is immediate (cover \(Q\) by itself). For \(\ge\), take any countable cover by boxes and enlarge each \(Q_k\) to an open box of volume \(<|Q_k|+\varepsilon2^{-k}\). Compactness of \(\overline Q\) (Heine–Borel) reduces to a finite subcover, and for finitely many boxes the elementary additivity of Layer 1 gives \(|Q|\le\sum|Q_k|+\varepsilon\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Carathéodory's theorem: the measurable sets form a \(\sigma\)-algebra on which \(\lambda^{*}\) is countably additive and complete.

    One clean criterion manufactures the entire theory.

    Sketch. The criterion is symmetric in \(E, E^{c}\), so \(\mathcal L\) is closed under complements. For finite unions, apply the criterion twice and use subadditivity to recombine. For countable unions, apply the finite case to partial unions and pass to the limit using monotonicity and subadditivity; the same computation yields countable additivity on disjoint measurable sets. Completeness: if \(\lambda^{*}(N)=0\) and \(Z \subseteq N\), then \(\lambda^{*}(A\cap Z)=0\) and \(\lambda^{*}(A\setminus Z)\le\lambda^{*}(A)\), so the criterion holds trivially. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Every Borel set is Lebesgue measurable; \(\mathcal B \subsetneq \mathcal L\).

    There are strictly more Lebesgue sets: \(|\mathcal B| = \mathfrak c\) while \(|\mathcal L| = 2^{\mathfrak c}\), since every subset of the Cantor set is measurable.

    It suffices that half-spaces \(\{x_i \le c\}\) satisfy the criterion, which follows by covering \(A\) with boxes and splitting each box along the hyperplane, where elementary additivity applies. These generate \(\mathcal B\), and \(\mathcal L\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra. Strictness: the Cantor set is null, so all \(2^{\mathfrak c}\) of its subsets are in \(\mathcal L\), while \(\mathcal B\) has cardinality \(\mathfrak c\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Regularity: for measurable \(E\), \(\lambda(E) = \inf\{\lambda(U) : U \supseteq E \text{ open}\} = \sup\{\lambda(K) : K \subseteq E \text{ compact}\}\).

    Any measurable set can be squeezed between an open set and a compact one, to within \(\varepsilon\).

    Outer: take a box cover within \(\varepsilon\) of \(\lambda(E)\) and enlarge each box slightly to be open. Inner: for bounded \(E\) inside a box \(Q\), apply the outer result to \(Q\setminus E\) and take complements within \(Q\) to get a closed — hence compact — subset; for unbounded \(E\), exhaust by \(E\cap\overline{B}(0,k)\) and use continuity from below. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Structure: \(E\) is measurable iff \(E = G\setminus Z\) with \(G\) a \(G_\delta\) and \(Z\) null, iff \(E = F\cup Z'\) with \(F\) an \(F_\sigma\) and \(Z'\) null.

    Every measurable set is a Borel set spoiled by something invisible.

    Choose open \(U_k \supseteq E\) with \(\lambda(U_k\setminus E)<1/k\) (regularity) and put \(G = \bigcap_kU_k\), a \(G_\delta\) containing \(E\) with \(\lambda(G\setminus E)=0\). The \(F_\sigma\) statement is this applied to \(E^{c}\). Conversely such sets are measurable, being Borel up to a null set and \(\mathcal L\) being complete. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Translation invariance and scaling: \(\lambda(E+v) = \lambda(E)\) and \(\lambda(cE) = |c|^{n}\lambda(E)\).

    Double a solid in every direction and its volume grows \(2^{n}\)-fold. Dimension appears as the exponent.

    Both operations map boxes to boxes, multiplying volume by 1 and by \(|c|^{n}\) respectively, and they map covers to covers bijectively. So the infimum defining \(\lambda^{*}\) transforms in the same way, and the Carathéodory criterion is preserved. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Linear images: \(\lambda(T(E)) = |\det T|\,\lambda(E)\) for linear \(T\).

    Chapter 5's determinant, finally proved to be the volume factor for arbitrary sets, not just boxes.

    For invertible \(T\), the set function \(E\mapsto\lambda(T(E))\) is a translation-invariant measure (translations commute with \(T\) up to a translation), so by the uniqueness theorem below it equals \(c_T\lambda\) for a constant \(c_T\). Multiplicativity of \(T\mapsto c_T\) plus the values on elementary matrices — shears leave volume alone, scalings multiply by the diagonal product — identify \(c_T = |\det T|\). If \(T\) is singular, the image lies in a hyperplane and both sides are 0. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Uniqueness: a translation-invariant measure on \(\mathcal B(\mathbb{R}^{n})\), finite on boxes, is a constant multiple of \(\lambda\).

    There is essentially only one notion of volume. The unit cube's value picks the units.

    Let \(\mu\) be such a measure with \(c = \mu([0,1)^{n})\). Splitting the unit cube into \(k^{n}\) translates of \([0,1/k)^{n}\) and using translation invariance forces \(\mu\) on every dyadic cube to be \(c\lambda\). Dyadic cubes generate \(\mathcal B\) and are closed under intersections, so the two measures agree on a generating \(\pi\)-system and hence everywhere. \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Cover with boxes, sum, take the infimum — the definition, used directly.

    Usually only for showing a set is null; everything else uses the structure theorems.

  • Approximate by open or compact sets — regularity turns a measurable set into a manageable one.

    "Choose \(K\) compact with \(\lambda(E\setminus K)<\varepsilon\)" opens a great many proofs.

  • Prove it on boxes, then extend by uniqueness.

    The standard architecture of every measure-theoretic proof in this chapter.

  • Use \(|\det T|\) — for the measure of a linear image.

    Chapter 12 turns this into the change of variables formula for curved maps.

Layer 4 · The price

Sets That Have No Volume

Why "measurable" cannot be dropped from any statement in this book.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Non-measurable set — one failing the Carathéodory criterion.

    No number can be its volume without breaking additivity or translation invariance.

  • Vitali set — one representative from each coset of \(\mathbb{Q}\) in \(\mathbb{R}\).

    Built by choosing one point from each of uncountably many buckets. Only the Axiom of Choice can do that.

  • Paradoxical decomposition — cutting a set into pieces that reassemble into two copies.

    Possible only when the pieces have no volume to conserve.

  • Solovay's model — a model of ZF + dependent choice in which every set of reals is measurable.

    The pathology is optional: drop full Choice and it disappears. Ordinary analysis survives.

Theorems

  • Vitali's theorem: there is a non-Lebesgue-measurable subset of \([0,1]\).

    Translate it by every rational in \([-1,1]\): the copies are disjoint and their union is squeezed between two sets of finite positive measure. No value for its size can work.

    Define \(x\sim y\) iff \(x - y\in\mathbb{Q}\), an equivalence relation on \([0,1]\), and use the Axiom of Choice to pick a representative from each class, forming \(V\). Let \(q_1,q_2,\dots\) enumerate \(\mathbb{Q}\cap[-1,1]\). The translates \(V + q_k\) are pairwise disjoint (two points of \(V\) never differ by a rational) and satisfy \([0,1]\subseteq\bigcup_k(V+q_k)\subseteq[-1,2]\). If \(V\) were measurable with \(\lambda(V) = c\), countable additivity and translation invariance would give \(1 \le \sum_k c \le 3\). But \(\sum_k c\) is 0 if \(c=0\) and \(\infty\) if \(c > 0\). Contradiction. \(\blacksquare\)

  • No translation-invariant, countably additive measure defines a volume for every subset of \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\).

    You must give up one of: measuring everything, countable additivity, or translation invariance. Lebesgue gives up the first.

    The Vitali argument used only those three properties plus normalisation on intervals, and derived a contradiction. In \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\), apply it in the first coordinate. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Banach–Tarski paradox: for \(n\ge3\), a ball can be split into finitely many pieces and reassembled by rigid motions into two balls of the same size.

    Not a statement about oranges. The pieces are non-measurable, so there is no volume being created — there was never any volume to conserve.

    Sketch: the rotation group \(SO(3)\) contains a free subgroup on two generators, which admits a paradoxical decomposition of itself. Choice picks one point from each orbit of that group acting on the sphere; transferring the group's decomposition to the orbits decomposes the sphere into finitely many pieces that reassemble into two spheres. Coning to the centre extends this to the solid ball. In \(\mathbb{R}^{1}\) and \(\mathbb{R}^{2}\) it fails: those isometry groups are amenable, and a finitely additive invariant measure on all subsets exists (Banach). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Every set of positive outer measure contains a non-measurable subset.

    The pathology is everywhere, not a single freak example.

    Intersect the Vitali construction with the given set: running the same coset argument inside \(E\) produces a subset whose rational translates disjointly cover a portion of \(E\), and measurability would again force the impossible dichotomy \(0\) or \(\infty\) against \(0 < \lambda^{*}(E)<\infty\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Choice is to blame: ZF + dependent choice is consistent with "every set of reals is Lebesgue measurable" (Solovay, assuming an inaccessible cardinal).

    The Axiom of Choice buys you Zorn's lemma and bases for every vector space — and charges you these sets.

    Sketch: Solovay constructs, from a model with an inaccessible cardinal, an inner model of ZF + DC in which every set of reals is Lebesgue measurable, has the Baire property, and is either countable or contains a perfect set. Since all the constructions of this book use at most DC, none of them is lost. \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Always state measurability — every theorem from here on carries the hypothesis.

    Not pedantry: the counterexamples are real.

  • Look for the use of Choice — non-constructive pathologies always come from there.

    Anything you can actually write down is measurable.

  • Translate-and-count — the technique of the Vitali argument.

    Disjoint translates squeezed between two finite bounds force a contradiction.

Layer 5 · What can be integrated

Measurable Functions

The functions whose graphs the theory can see.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Measurable function — \(f^{-1}(U)\) is measurable for every open \(U\); equivalently \(\{f > c\}\) is measurable for every \(c\).

    Chapter 8's continuity criterion, with "open" replaced by "measurable" on the left.

  • Simple function — a finite combination \(\sum_i c_i\chi_{E_i}\) of indicators of measurable sets.

    A staircase with measurable steps. Every integral in Chapter 12 is defined on these first.

  • Extended-real-valued — allowed the values \(\pm\infty\).

    Necessary, since limits of measurable functions may blow up on a small set.

  • Essential supremum — the smallest bound that holds a.e.

    Ignores what happens on a null set. A single spike doesn't count.

  • Convergence almost everywhere.

    The natural notion once null sets are declared invisible.

  • Convergence in measure — \(\lambda(\{|f_k-f|>\varepsilon\})\to0\).

    The bad set shrinks, though it may wander. Weaker than a.e. convergence, and it forgets where the trouble was.

Theorems

  • Equivalent criteria: \(f\) is measurable iff \(\{f>c\}\) is measurable for all \(c\), iff for all rational \(c\).

    Countably many conditions suffice — the practical test.

    \(\{f>c\} = \bigcup_{q>c, q\in\mathbb{Q}}\{f>q\}\) gives the reduction to rational \(c\). Conversely the sets \(\{f>c\}\) generate the Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra of \(\mathbb{R}\) (they generate the half-lines, hence the open sets), so measurability of their preimages gives measurability of all Borel preimages. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Continuous functions are measurable, and measurable \(\circ\) continuous stays measurable.

    Everything you meet in practice is measurable. Producing a non-measurable function requires Choice.

    For continuous \(f\), \(f^{-1}(U)\) is open, hence Borel, hence measurable. If \(g\) is continuous and \(f\) measurable, \((g\circ f)^{-1}(U) = f^{-1}(g^{-1}(U))\) is the preimage of an open set. (The other order can fail, which is why the composition rule is stated this way.) \(\blacksquare\)

  • Measurability is preserved by sums, products, scalar multiples, \(\max\), \(\min\), \(|f|\), and countable \(\sup\), \(\inf\), \(\limsup\), \(\liminf\), and a.e. limits.

    Unlike Riemann integrability, this class is closed under limits — which is the whole reason Lebesgue's theory works.

    \(\{f+g > c\} = \bigcup_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}\bigl(\{f>q\}\cap\{g>c-q\}\bigr)\), a countable union of measurable sets. \(\{\sup_kf_k > c\} = \bigcup_k\{f_k>c\}\) and \(\{\inf_kf_k < c\} = \bigcup_k\{f_k<c\}\); then \(\limsup_kf_k = \inf_N\sup_{k\ge N}f_k\) is measurable, and an a.e. limit equals the \(\limsup\) off a null set, which is measurable by completeness. Products follow from \(fg = \tfrac14((f+g)^{2}-(f-g)^{2})\) and measurability of \(t\mapsto t^{2}\) composed with a measurable function. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Simple approximation: every non-negative measurable \(f\) is an increasing pointwise limit of simple functions.

    Chop the range into layers rather than the domain into strips. That one change is the whole of Lebesgue's idea.

    Set \(s_k = \sum_{j=0}^{k2^{k}-1}\frac{j}{2^{k}}\chi_{\{j/2^{k}\le f<(j+1)/2^{k}\}} + k\chi_{\{f\ge k\}}\). Each set involved is measurable, so \(s_k\) is simple; refining the range partition makes \(s_k\) increasing; and \(0 \le f - s_k \le 2^{-k}\) wherever \(f < k\), so \(s_k \to f\) pointwise, uniformly where \(f\) is bounded. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Egorov's theorem: on a set of finite measure, a.e. convergence is uniform off a set of arbitrarily small measure.

    "Almost uniform" convergence. Discard a sliver and the convergence becomes as good as you like.

    Let \(E_{k,N} = \bigcup_{j\ge N}\{|f_j - f| > 1/k\}\). For fixed \(k\) these decrease in \(N\) with intersection null (a.e. convergence), so by continuity from above — using finite measure — \(\lambda(E_{k,N_k})<\varepsilon2^{-k}\) for some \(N_k\). Remove \(\bigcup_kE_{k,N_k}\), of measure \(<\varepsilon\); on the remainder, \(|f_j-f|\le1/k\) for all \(j \ge N_k\), which is uniform convergence. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Lusin's theorem: a measurable function is continuous off a set of arbitrarily small measure.

    Measurable functions are "almost continuous" — Littlewood's second principle.

    Approximate \(f\) by simple functions (above); each simple function is continuous off a small set by regularity of the measure on the finitely many sets involved. Egorov makes the approximation uniform off another small set, and a uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous. Removing the countable union of small exceptional sets leaves a set of measure \(> \lambda(E)-\varepsilon\) on which \(f\) is continuous. \(\blacksquare\)

  • a.e. convergence \(\Rightarrow\) convergence in measure on a finite measure space; the converse fails, but a subsequence converges a.e.

    The "typewriter" sequence of indicators of sliding intervals converges in measure to 0 while converging at no point at all.

    The forward implication is immediate from Egorov. For the failure, take \(f_k = \chi_{I_k}\) with \(I_k\) marching across \([0,1]\) in blocks of shrinking length: \(\lambda(\{f_k > \varepsilon\})\to0\) but every point is hit infinitely often. For the subsequence, choose \(k_j\) with \(\lambda(\{|f_{k_j}-f|>2^{-j}\})<2^{-j}\); Borel–Cantelli (Layer 2) says a.e. point lies in only finitely many of those sets, giving a.e. convergence. \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Check \(\{f > c\}\) — the standard measurability test.

    Rational \(c\) suffices, so it is a countable check.

  • Approximate by simple functions — then prove the theorem for staircases.

    The architecture of every proof in Chapter 12.

  • Discard a small set — Egorov and Lusin trade a sliver of measure for uniformity or continuity.

    Littlewood's three principles in action.

  • Work almost everywhere — modify on a null set whenever it helps.

    Nothing measurable notices.
