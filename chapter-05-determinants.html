The First Volume

Chapter 4 gave direction but no size. The determinant is the first quantity in this book that measures how much space something occupies.

It is the unique way to attach a number to \(n\) vectors so that the number scales linearly in each and dies when two coincide. That forces it to be signed volume.

Everything that follows — the Jacobian in Chapter 10, the change of variables formula in Chapter 12, the volume form in Chapter 16 — is this one idea.

Layer 1 · Shuffling

Permutations & Sign

The bookkeeping that produces the minus signs.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Permutation \(\sigma\) — a bijection of \(\{1,\dots,n\}\) to itself.

    A reseating of \(n\) guests: everyone gets a chair, no chair gets two.

  • Symmetric group \(S_n\) — all \(n!\) permutations under composition.

    3 guests, 6 seatings. 10 guests, 3.6 million.

  • Transposition — a permutation swapping two elements and fixing the rest.

    Two guests trade chairs; nobody else moves.

  • Cycle \((a_1\,a_2\,\dots\,a_k)\) — each element passes to the next, the last back to the first.

    Everyone shifts one seat to the left around a round table.

  • Inversion — a pair \(i < j\) with \(\sigma(i) > \sigma(j)\).

    A pair standing in the wrong order. Counting them measures how scrambled the shuffle is.

  • Sign \(\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma) = (-1)^{\#\text{inversions}}\); even or odd.

    The single number that turns a sum over shuffles into a determinant.

  • Alternating group \(A_n\) — the even permutations.

    Exactly half of them, for \(n \ge 2\). These are the ones that preserve orientation.

Theorems

  • Cycle decomposition: every permutation is a product of disjoint cycles, uniquely up to order.

    Follow each guest to their new seat and you trace out closed loops. The loops are the permutation.

    Define \(x \sim y\) iff \(y = \sigma^{k}(x)\) for some \(k\); this is an equivalence relation (Chapter 2, Layer 4), so its classes partition \(\{1,\dots,n\}\). On each class the orbit \(x, \sigma(x),\sigma^2(x),\dots\) must return to \(x\) — the set is finite and \(\sigma\) is injective — giving a cycle. Distinct classes are disjoint, and the cycles commute, so the order is irrelevant. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Every permutation is a product of transpositions.

    Any seating can be reached by a sequence of two-person swaps.

    By cycle decomposition it suffices to handle a single cycle, and \((a_1\,a_2\,\dots\,a_k) = (a_1\,a_k)(a_1\,a_{k-1})\cdots(a_1\,a_2)\), which is checked by tracking where each \(a_i\) goes. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Sign is well defined: the parity of the number of transpositions does not depend on the decomposition.

    You may need more swaps than your neighbour, but never a different parity of swaps.

    Consider the polynomial \(\Delta = \prod_{i<j}(x_i - x_j)\) and let \(\sigma\) act by permuting indices. A transposition swaps two variables and changes the sign of \(\Delta\) exactly once: the factor between them flips, and the remaining factors pair up with sign changes cancelling. So \(\sigma\cdot\Delta = \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\Delta\) with \(\operatorname{sgn}\) equal to \((-1)^{t}\) for any decomposition into \(t\) transpositions — and since \(\Delta \ne 0\), the parity of \(t\) is forced. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Sign is multiplicative: \(\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\tau) = \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\operatorname{sgn}(\tau)\), and \(\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma^{-1}) = \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\).

    Two even shuffles make an even shuffle; even and odd make odd.

    Concatenating decompositions of \(\sigma\) and \(\tau\) into \(s\) and \(t\) transpositions gives one of \(\sigma\tau\) into \(s+t\), so \((-1)^{s+t} = (-1)^s(-1)^t\). Reversing a decomposition inverts the permutation with the same number of transpositions, giving the second claim. \(\blacksquare\)

  • A \(k\)-cycle has sign \((-1)^{k-1}\).

    A 3-cycle is even, a 2-cycle (a swap) is odd. Handy for computing signs at a glance.

    The decomposition above writes a \(k\)-cycle as \(k-1\) transpositions, so its sign is \((-1)^{k-1}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Decompose into cycles, then read the sign — multiply \((-1)^{k-1}\) over the cycles.

    Much faster than counting inversions by hand.

  • Count inversions — for small \(n\), just list the out-of-order pairs.

    \((2,3,1)\) has inversions \((1,3)\) and \((2,3)\): two, so even.

Layer 2 · The right requirement

Multilinear Alternating Forms

Three demands that leave exactly one function standing.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Multilinear form — a function of \(k\) vectors, linear in each slot with the others held fixed.

    Double one edge of a box and the volume doubles — whichever edge you pick.

  • Alternating — the value is \(0\) whenever two arguments are equal.

    A "box" with two identical edges is flat, so it holds nothing.

  • Antisymmetric — swapping two arguments flips the sign.

    Relabel two edges and the volume keeps its size but reverses its handedness.

  • Normalisation — the value \(1\) on the standard basis.

    The unit cube has volume 1. That single choice pins the whole function down.

  • Determinant function \(D(v_1,\dots,v_n)\) — the multilinear alternating normalised form on \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\).

    Feed it \(n\) arrows, get the signed volume of the box they span.

Theorems

  • Alternating \(\Rightarrow\) antisymmetric (and conversely, outside characteristic 2).

    "Flat when repeated" already forces "sign flips on a swap".

    Expand \(D(\dots,u+v,\dots,u+v,\dots) = 0\) by multilinearity into \(D(u,u)+D(u,v)+D(v,u)+D(v,v)\) in the two relevant slots. The first and last vanish because the arguments repeat, leaving \(D(u,v) = -D(v,u)\). Conversely, antisymmetry gives \(D(u,u) = -D(u,u)\), so \(2D(u,u)=0\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Adding a multiple of one argument to another does not change the value.

    Shearing a box — sliding the top face sideways — keeps its volume. Exactly why row operations are safe.

    \(D(\dots, v_i + cv_j, \dots, v_j,\dots) = D(\dots,v_i,\dots,v_j,\dots) + c\,D(\dots,v_j,\dots,v_j,\dots)\) by linearity in slot \(i\), and the second term vanishes because two arguments coincide. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Dependent arguments give zero.

    If the arrows don't reach into all \(n\) directions, the box is squashed flat.

    If \(v_k = \sum_{i\ne k}c_iv_i\), expand slot \(k\) by multilinearity into a sum of terms each having a repeated argument, hence each zero. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Uniqueness theorem: there is exactly one multilinear alternating normalised form on \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\), namely \[D(v_1,\dots,v_n) = \sum_{\sigma\in S_n}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^{n} a_{\sigma(i)\,i}.\]

    Volume isn't chosen — it's forced. Demand those three properties and the formula writes itself.

    Write each \(v_i = \sum_j a_{ji}e_j\) and expand by multilinearity into \(n^{n}\) terms \( \bigl(\prod_i a_{j_i i}\bigr) D(e_{j_1},\dots,e_{j_n})\). Every term with a repeated index vanishes (alternating), so only the \(n!\) terms where \(j\) is a permutation \(\sigma\) survive. Antisymmetry sorts \(D(e_{\sigma(1)},\dots,e_{\sigma(n)})\) into \(\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)D(e_1,\dots,e_n) = \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\) by normalisation. This both proves uniqueness and, since the resulting formula is checked to be multilinear and alternating, existence. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Corollary: every multilinear alternating form is a scalar multiple of \(\det\).

    There is only one notion of signed volume up to the choice of unit.

    Given such a form \(F\), let \(c = F(e_1,\dots,e_n)\). The expansion above shows \(F = c\,D\), since every step used only multilinearity and the alternating property. \(\blacksquare\)

  • The space of alternating \(n\)-forms on \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\) is one-dimensional.

    One number's worth of freedom — the unit of volume. Chapter 16 calls this line the top exterior power.

    Immediate from the corollary: the map \(F \mapsto F(e_1,\dots,e_n)\) is a linear bijection onto \(\mathbb{R}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Verify the three properties — to identify something as "the determinant, up to scale".

    Multilinear, alternating, and you're done; the value on the unit cube fixes the constant.

  • Expand by multilinearity — split each argument into basis pieces and discard repeats.

    The proof engine of this whole chapter.

Layer 3 · The number

The Determinant

Computing it, and the identities that make it useful.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Determinant of a matrix \(\det A\) — the determinant form applied to its columns.

    \(\det\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix} = ad-bc\). Everything else is that pattern in \(n\) dimensions.

  • Leibniz formula — the \(n!\)-term sum over permutations.

    Exact, and hopeless to compute: \(20!\) terms for a \(20\times20\) matrix. Use row reduction instead.

  • Minor \(M_{ij}\) — the determinant of \(A\) with row \(i\) and column \(j\) deleted.

    The volume of what's left after removing one direction and one constraint.

  • Cofactor \(C_{ij} = (-1)^{i+j}M_{ij}\).

    The minor with the checkerboard sign attached.

  • Adjugate \(\operatorname{adj}A\) — the transpose of the cofactor matrix.

    The matrix that multiplies \(A\) to give \(\det(A)\,I\) — an inverse waiting for a division.

  • Singular matrix — one with \(\det = 0\).

    It flattens space, so it cannot be undone. The seventh entry on Chapter 4's invertibility list.

  • Triangular matrix — all entries below (or above) the diagonal are zero.

    Its determinant is just the product of the diagonal — the reason elimination works.

Theorems

  • Row operations: swapping rows flips the sign, scaling a row by \(c\) scales \(\det\) by \(c\), adding a multiple of a row to another leaves \(\det\) unchanged.

    The three rules that make determinants computable in \(n^{3}\) steps rather than \(n!\).

    These are exactly antisymmetry, linearity in one slot, and the shear-invariance of Layer 2, applied to rows — legitimate because \(\det A^{\mathsf T} = \det A\) (below), or by running the same argument with rows in place of columns. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Triangular determinant: the product of the diagonal entries.

    Row-reduce to triangular, multiply the diagonal, adjust for the swaps and scalings you performed.

    In the Leibniz sum, a term \(\prod_i a_{\sigma(i)i}\) is non-zero only if \(\sigma(i)\le i\) for all \(i\) (upper triangular case). Summing the inequalities forces \(\sigma(i)=i\) for every \(i\), leaving the single term \(a_{11}a_{22}\cdots a_{nn}\) with sign \(+1\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • \(\det A \ne 0\) iff \(A\) is invertible.

    Zero volume means the map collapsed a direction, and collapsing cannot be undone.

    If the columns are dependent then \(\det A = 0\) (Layer 2). If they are independent they form a basis, so \(A\) is invertible (Chapter 4, Layer 4), and row reduction takes \(A\) to \(I\) by operations that only multiply the determinant by non-zero factors, so \(\det A \neq 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Multiplicativity: \(\det(AB) = \det A \cdot \det B\).

    Do one map then the other and the volume factors multiply. Beautiful, and not at all obvious from the formula.

    Fix \(A\) and consider \(F(v_1,\dots,v_n) = \det(Av_1,\dots,Av_n)\). It is multilinear (each \(v_i \mapsto Av_i\) is linear) and alternating (equal arguments give equal images). By Layer 2's corollary \(F = c\det\) with \(c = F(e_1,\dots,e_n) = \det A\). Applying this to the columns of \(B\) gives \(\det(AB) = \det A \det B\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Corollary: \(\det(A^{-1}) = 1/\det A\), and similar matrices have equal determinants.

    Undoing a map divides the volume back. So \(\det\) belongs to the map, not to the coordinates.

    \(\det A\det(A^{-1}) = \det(AA^{-1}) = \det I = 1\). Then \(\det(P^{-1}AP) = \det(P^{-1})\det A\det P = \det A\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Transpose invariance: \(\det A^{\mathsf T} = \det A\).

    Rows and columns tell the same volume story — which is why row and column operations are equally valid.

    In the Leibniz sum for \(A^{\mathsf T}\), the term for \(\sigma\) is \(\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\prod_i a_{i\sigma(i)}\). Re-index by \(j = \sigma(i)\): the product becomes \(\prod_j a_{\sigma^{-1}(j)j}\), and \(\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma^{-1}) = \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\) (Layer 1). As \(\sigma\) runs over \(S_n\) so does \(\sigma^{-1}\), so the two sums agree term for term. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Laplace (cofactor) expansion: \(\det A = \sum_{j} a_{ij}C_{ij}\) along any row, or any column.

    Peel off one row and reduce to smaller determinants — the practical hand method for \(3\times3\).

    Split row \(i\) as \(\sum_j a_{ij}e_j^{\mathsf T}\) and use linearity in that row: \(\det A = \sum_j a_{ij}\det A^{(j)}\), where \(A^{(j)}\) has row \(i\) replaced by \(e_j^{\mathsf T}\). Moving that row and column into position by \(i-1\) and \(j-1\) adjacent swaps contributes \((-1)^{i+j}\), and the Leibniz sum of what remains is precisely the minor \(M_{ij}\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Adjugate identity: \(A\operatorname{adj}A = \operatorname{adj}(A)\,A = (\det A) I\).

    A closed formula for the inverse: \(A^{-1} = \operatorname{adj}(A)/\det A\). Elegant, and far too slow to use.

    The \((i,i)\) entry of \(A\operatorname{adj}A\) is \(\sum_j a_{ij}C_{ij} = \det A\) by Laplace expansion. For \(i \ne k\), the \((i,k)\) entry is \(\sum_j a_{ij}C_{kj}\), which is the Laplace expansion of the matrix obtained by replacing row \(k\) with row \(i\) — a matrix with two equal rows, hence determinant 0. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Cramer's rule: for invertible \(A\), the solution of \(Ax=b\) has \(x_i = \det(A_i)/\det A\), where \(A_i\) is \(A\) with column \(i\) replaced by \(b\).

    Each unknown is a ratio of volumes. Theoretically perfect, practically hopeless beyond \(3\times3\).

    Write \(b = \sum_j x_j a^{(j)}\) with \(a^{(j)}\) the columns. Then by multilinearity and the alternating property, \(\det(A_i) = \det(a^{(1)},\dots,b,\dots,a^{(n)}) = \sum_j x_j\det(a^{(1)},\dots,a^{(j)},\dots,a^{(n)})\), and every term with \(j\ne i\) has a repeated column, leaving \(x_i \det A\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Block triangular determinant: \(\det\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\0&D\end{pmatrix} = \det A\cdot\det D\).

    If the map leaves a subspace alone, its volume factor splits into two independent factors.

    In the Leibniz sum, a non-zero term needs \(\sigma\) to map the last block of indices into itself (the lower-left block is zero), hence also the first block into itself. So \(\sigma\) splits as a pair \((\sigma_1,\sigma_2)\) with \(\operatorname{sgn}\sigma = \operatorname{sgn}\sigma_1\operatorname{sgn}\sigma_2\), and the sum factors into the two smaller Leibniz sums. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Vandermonde determinant: \(\det(x_i^{\,j-1}) = \prod_{i<j}(x_j-x_i)\).

    Non-zero exactly when the \(x_i\) are distinct — which is why \(n\) points determine one polynomial of degree \(< n\).

    As a polynomial in \(x_n\) the determinant has degree \(n-1\) and vanishes at \(x_1,\dots,x_{n-1}\) (two equal rows), so it equals \(c\prod_{i<n}(x_n-x_i)\). Comparing the coefficient of \(x_n^{n-1}\) identifies \(c\) as the Vandermonde determinant of the first \(n-1\) variables; induction closes the argument. \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Row-reduce to triangular — the only sane way to compute a determinant.

    Track the swaps (sign) and scalings (factors) as you go.

  • Cofactor expansion along a sparse row — pick the row or column with the most zeros.

    One well-chosen row can turn a \(4\times4\) into a single \(3\times3\).

  • Exploit block structure — split into blocks whenever a corner is zero.

    Common for Jacobians of maps that don't mix all the variables.

  • Use \(\det \ne 0\) as an invertibility test.

    One number answers solvability, independence and invertibility at once.

Layer 4 · What it means

Determinant as Volume

The geometric reading that the rest of the book depends on.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Parallelepiped \(P(v_1,\dots,v_n) = \{\sum t_iv_i : t_i \in [0,1]\}\).

    The box spanned by \(n\) arrows — a parallelogram in the plane, a slanted brick in space.

  • Signed volume — \(\det\) of the matrix whose columns are the edges.

    Size, plus a sign recording whether the edges are ordered right-handed or left-handed.

  • Orientation — the sign of the determinant of a basis, relative to the standard one.

    Right hand vs left hand. Chapters 15 and 16 need this to integrate over surfaces.

  • Volume scaling factor \(|\det T|\).

    Every region's volume is multiplied by this number, wherever it sits and whatever its shape.

  • Orientation-preserving map — \(\det > 0\).

    Rotations preserve; mirror reflections reverse. You cannot rotate a left glove into a right one.

  • Special linear group \(SL_n(\mathbb{R})\) — the maps with \(\det = 1\).

    Shears and volume-preserving distortions: they can smear a shape beyond recognition without changing its volume.

  • Cross product in \(\mathbb{R}^{3}\) — \(u\times v\), the vector whose length is the parallelogram's area.

    A determinant in disguise: \(u \times v\) is the formal expansion of a \(3\times3\) determinant with \(e_1,e_2,e_3\) in the top row.

  • Gram determinant \(\det(G)\) with \(G_{ij} = v_i\cdot v_j\).

    How to measure a \(k\)-dimensional box sitting inside \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\), where the matrix isn't square. Chapter 14 lives on this.

Theorems

  • Volume of a parallelepiped: \(\operatorname{vol}\,P(v_1,\dots,v_n) = |\det(v_1\,\cdots\,v_n)|\).

    The parallelogram on \((a,b)\) and \((c,d)\) has area \(|ad-bc|\) — the school formula, derived.

    Volume is translation-invariant, scales linearly in each edge, and vanishes when two edges coincide (the box is flat) — so as a function of the edges it is multilinear and alternating up to sign, hence a multiple of \(\det\) by Layer 2. Normalising with the unit cube, whose volume is 1, fixes the multiple, and the absolute value removes the sign. (Chapter 11 supplies the rigorous notion of volume this argument names.) \(\blacksquare\)

  • Uniform scaling: for measurable \(E\), \(\operatorname{vol}(T(E)) = |\det T|\operatorname{vol}(E)\).

    Not just boxes — every shape. This is the seed of the change of variables formula in Chapter 12.

    Sketch: the identity holds for boxes by the previous theorem. Since \(T\) is linear, the image of a grid of small cubes is a grid of congruent parallelepipeds, so the identity holds for finite unions of cubes; approximating an arbitrary measurable set from outside and inside by such unions (Chapter 11's regularity) transfers it in the limit. \(\blacksquare\)

  • \(\det(cA) = c^{n}\det A\).

    Double every edge of a 3-D box and the volume grows eightfold. Dimension appears as an exponent — a theme of Chapters 13 and 18.

    Scaling by \(c\) scales each of the \(n\) columns by \(c\); multilinearity pulls one factor of \(c\) out of each. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Orientation classes: the ordered bases of \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\) fall into exactly two classes.

    Right-handed and left-handed. There is no continuous path between them.

    Two bases are related by an invertible change matrix, whose determinant is non-zero; declare them equivalent when it is positive. This is an equivalence relation (determinants multiply), with exactly two classes since swapping two basis vectors changes the sign. Continuity of \(\det\) forbids a path from a positive to a negative value without passing through a singular — non-basis — matrix. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Cross product identities: \(u\times v\) is orthogonal to both, with \(|u\times v| = |u||v|\sin\theta\), and \(u\cdot(v\times w) = \det(u\,v\,w)\).

    The scalar triple product is the volume of the box on the three vectors.

    Define \(u\times v\) by \(\langle u\times v, w\rangle = \det(w\,u\,v)\) for all \(w\) — legitimate since the right side is a linear functional of \(w\) (Chapter 4, Layer 6). Taking \(w = u\) or \(v\) gives a determinant with a repeated column, hence 0: orthogonality. The length formula follows from \(|u\times v|^{2} = \det\) of the \(2\times2\) Gram matrix \(= |u|^2|v|^2 - (u\cdot v)^2\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Gram determinant formula: the \(k\)-volume of \(P(v_1,\dots,v_k)\) in \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\) is \(\sqrt{\det(A^{\mathsf T}A)}\), where \(A\) has the \(v_i\) as columns.

    A 2-D parallelogram floating in 5-D space still has an area, and this computes it.

    Apply Gram–Schmidt (Chapter 6) to get \(A = QR\) with \(Q\) having orthonormal columns and \(R\) upper triangular; then \(A^{\mathsf T}A = R^{\mathsf T}R\), so \(\sqrt{\det(A^{\mathsf T}A)} = |\det R|\). Since \(Q\) is an isometry onto the span, the \(k\)-volume equals that of the box on the columns of \(R\) inside \(\mathbb{R}^{k}\), which is \(|\det R|\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Corollary: \(\det(A^{\mathsf T}A) \ge 0\), with equality iff the columns are dependent.

    Volumes are never negative, and vanish exactly when the box is flat.

    \(\det(A^{\mathsf T}A) = (\text{$k$-volume})^{2}\ge0\) by the previous theorem. It is zero exactly when the parallelepiped is degenerate, i.e. when the columns are linearly dependent (Layer 2). \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Read \(|\det|\) as a volume factor — the standard way to think about any linear map.

    \(\det = 3\) means "triples every volume"; \(\det = -1\) means "keeps volume, flips handedness".

  • Use the Gram determinant for non-square problems.

    Whenever \(k < n\): area of a triangle in space, surface area in Chapter 14.

  • Track orientation by the sign.

    Chapters 15–16 need a consistent choice of sign to integrate anything over a surface.

Layer 5 · Preferred directions

Eigenvalues & Diagonalisation

The directions a map merely stretches — and the determinant that finds them.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Eigenvector & eigenvalue — \(Av = \lambda v\) with \(v \ne \mathbf{0}\).

    A direction the map doesn't turn, only stretches. The axis of a rotation is one.

  • Characteristic polynomial \(p(\lambda) = \det(A - \lambda I)\).

    Its roots are the eigenvalues. This is the determinant's most famous job.

  • Eigenspace \(E_\lambda = \ker(A-\lambda I)\).

    All vectors stretched by the same factor, together with \(\mathbf{0}\).

  • Algebraic & geometric multiplicity — order as a root; dimension of the eigenspace.

    Geometric never exceeds algebraic. When they differ, the matrix cannot be diagonalised.

  • Diagonalisable — similar to a diagonal matrix.

    Choose eigenvector axes and the map becomes "stretch each axis by its own factor".

  • Spectrum — the set of eigenvalues.

    The map's fingerprint. Chapter 6 shows symmetric matrices have an especially clean one.

  • Nilpotent matrix — \(N^{k} = 0\) for some \(k\).

    Every eigenvalue is 0, yet the matrix isn't. The standard obstruction to diagonalising.

Theorems

  • \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue iff \(\det(A-\lambda I) = 0\).

    Look for the stretch factors that make the map collapse a direction.

    \(Av = \lambda v\) for some \(v \ne \mathbf 0\) means \(\ker(A-\lambda I) \ne \{\mathbf 0\}\), i.e. \(A - \lambda I\) is not invertible, i.e. its determinant is 0 (Layer 3). \(\blacksquare\)

  • \(p(\lambda)\) has degree \(n\), with \(\det A = \prod\lambda_i\) and \(\operatorname{tr}A = \sum\lambda_i\) (counted with multiplicity, over \(\mathbb{C}\)).

    The determinant is the product of the stretch factors — of course it is: volume multiplies.

    The Leibniz expansion of \(\det(A-\lambda I)\) has leading term \((-\lambda)^{n}\) from the diagonal product, so the degree is \(n\). Setting \(\lambda = 0\) gives \(p(0) = \det A\), which equals the product of roots up to the sign absorbed by the leading coefficient. Comparing the coefficient of \(\lambda^{n-1}\), which arises only from the diagonal product, identifies the sum of the roots as \(\operatorname{tr}A\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are independent.

    Different stretch factors can't secretly be the same direction.

    Take a shortest non-trivial dependency \(\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_iv_i = \mathbf 0\) among eigenvectors with distinct \(\lambda_i\). Apply \(A - \lambda_k I\): the last term dies and the rest become \(c_i(\lambda_i - \lambda_k)v_i\), a shorter non-trivial dependency (the factors are non-zero) — contradiction. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Corollary: \(n\) distinct eigenvalues \(\Rightarrow\) diagonalisable.

    The generic case. Repeated eigenvalues are where the trouble hides.

    The \(n\) corresponding eigenvectors are independent, hence a basis (Chapter 4, Layer 2). In that basis the matrix acts by scaling each coordinate, so it is diagonal. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Diagonalisable iff eigenvectors span: equivalently, every geometric multiplicity equals its algebraic multiplicity.

    A shear \(\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\) has one eigenvalue, one eigenvector direction, and cannot be diagonalised.

    If a basis of eigenvectors exists, the matrix in that basis is diagonal, and each eigenspace has dimension equal to the number of times its eigenvalue appears — its algebraic multiplicity. Conversely, if the multiplicities all agree, the eigenspaces are independent (previous theorem) and their dimensions sum to \(n\), so together they span. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Similar matrices have the same characteristic polynomial, hence the same eigenvalues, trace and determinant.

    These numbers describe the map itself, not your choice of axes.

    \(\det(P^{-1}AP - \lambda I) = \det(P^{-1}(A-\lambda I)P) = \det(P)^{-1}\det(A-\lambda I)\det(P) = \det(A-\lambda I)\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Cayley–Hamilton: every matrix satisfies its own characteristic polynomial, \(p(A) = 0\).

    A matrix obeys the very equation built to find its eigenvalues. (Substituting \(\lambda = A\) into \(\det(A-\lambda I)\) is not a proof.)

    Apply the adjugate identity to \(B(\lambda) = A - \lambda I\): \(\operatorname{adj}(B)\,B = p(\lambda)I\). Both sides are matrices whose entries are polynomials in \(\lambda\); write \(\operatorname{adj}(B) = \sum_k \lambda^{k}C_k\) with constant matrices \(C_k\), expand, and compare coefficients of each power of \(\lambda\). Multiplying the \(k\)-th relation by \(A^{k}\) and summing telescopes the left side to 0 and leaves \(p(A)\) on the right. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Triangularisation: over \(\mathbb{C}\), every matrix is similar to an upper triangular one.

    You can't always diagonalise, but you can always get the eigenvalues onto the diagonal.

    Induct on \(n\). The characteristic polynomial has a root \(\lambda_1\) over \(\mathbb{C}\) (fundamental theorem of algebra), giving an eigenvector \(v_1\). Extend to a basis; in it the matrix has first column \((\lambda_1,0,\dots,0)^{\mathsf T}\), so it is block triangular with an \((n-1)\times(n-1)\) block, which the induction hypothesis triangularises. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Determinant of a diagonalisable map is the product of its stretch factors — and \(|\det|\) is the volume factor.

    Stretch by 2 along one axis and 3 along another: areas multiply by 6.

    If \(A = PDP^{-1}\) with \(D = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_i)\), then \(\det A = \det D = \prod\lambda_i\) by similarity invariance and the triangular formula. Layer 4 identifies \(|\det A|\) as the volume scaling factor. \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Solve \(\det(A-\lambda I) = 0\), then find each \(\ker(A-\lambda I)\).

    Eigenvalues first, eigenvectors second. Always in that order.

  • Diagonalise to take powers — \(A^{k} = PD^{k}P^{-1}\).

    Raising a diagonal matrix to a power is done entry by entry; everything hard is in the change of basis.

  • Check trace and determinant against the eigenvalues — a free error check.

    They must sum and multiply correctly, or something went wrong.

  • Cayley–Hamilton to reduce powers — express \(A^{n}\) in terms of lower powers.

    Turns an infinite family of matrices into an \(n\)-dimensional one.
