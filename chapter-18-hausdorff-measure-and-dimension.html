Hausdorff Measure & Dimension

❮ Chapter 17 — Riemannian Geometry Start Learning ❯

Dimension as a Number, Not a Count

Chapter 14 measured \(k\)-dimensional objects, but only smooth ones with a parametrisation. What is the "area" of a set too rough to parametrise?

Hausdorff's answer: cover it with small sets and add up \((\text{diameter})^{s}\). The exponent \(s\) at which the total jumps from \(\infty\) to \(0\) is the dimension — and it need not be a whole number.

This is the most general notion of size in the book: it measures curves, surfaces, fractals and Lebesgue volume with one formula.

Layer 1 · A ruler for every exponent

Hausdorff Measure

Chapter 11's construction, with boxes replaced by arbitrary small sets.

Concepts & Definitions

  • \(\delta\)-cover — a countable cover by sets of diameter at most \(\delta\).

    Chapter 11 used boxes; here any shape will do, provided it is small.

  • Pre-measure \(\mathcal{H}^{s}_\delta(A) = \inf\sum_i\alpha_s\bigl(\tfrac{\operatorname{diam}U_i}{2}\bigr)^{s}\) over \(\delta\)-covers.

    Add up "radius to the power \(s\)". The constant \(\alpha_s = \pi^{s/2}/\Gamma(\frac s2+1)\) is Chapter 13's ball volume, chosen so the answer normalises correctly.

  • Hausdorff measure \(\mathcal{H}^{s}(A) = \lim_{\delta\to0}\mathcal{H}^{s}_\delta(A)\).

    Force the covers to be fine, so they must follow the set's shape rather than bridging across it.

  • Counting measure \(\mathcal{H}^{0}\).

    At \(s=0\) every set contributes 1, so \(\mathcal H^{0}\) simply counts points.

  • Metric outer measure — additive on sets a positive distance apart.

    The property that makes all Borel sets measurable, with no further work.

  • Spherical measure — the same construction using balls only.

    Comparable to \(\mathcal H^{s}\), but not equal — the constants differ, which is why the isodiametric inequality matters.

  • Gauge function \(h(\operatorname{diam}U)\) — a general weight in place of \(t^{s}\).

    Lets you measure sets whose dimension is exactly critical, where \(\mathcal H^{s}\) is 0 or \(\infty\).

Theorems

  • \(\mathcal{H}^{s}\) is an outer measure, and the limit defining it exists in \([0,\infty]\).

    Shrinking \(\delta\) restricts the covers available, so the infimum can only rise.

    Monotonicity and countable subadditivity of each \(\mathcal H^{s}_\delta\) are proved exactly as for Lebesgue outer measure (Chapter 11, Layer 3), by combining covers with the \(\varepsilon2^{-k}\) trick. As \(\delta\) decreases the family of admissible covers shrinks, so \(\mathcal H^{s}_\delta\) is non-increasing in \(\delta\) and the limit exists, possibly infinite; subadditivity passes to the limit. \(\blacksquare\)

  • \(\mathcal{H}^{s}\) is a metric outer measure, so every Borel set is \(\mathcal H^{s}\)-measurable.

    Two pieces held apart are measured separately, with nothing lost in between.

    If \(d(A,B) = \rho > 0\), take \(\delta < \rho\): no set in a \(\delta\)-cover can meet both \(A\) and \(B\), so any cover splits into a cover of \(A\) and one of \(B\), giving \(\mathcal H^{s}_\delta(A\cup B) = \mathcal H^{s}_\delta(A)+\mathcal H^{s}_\delta(B)\). Carathéodory's criterion for metric outer measures then makes all Borel sets measurable. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Scaling: \(\mathcal{H}^{s}(cA) = c^{s}\mathcal{H}^{s}(A)\).

    The exponent \(s\) behaves exactly as a dimension should. Chapter 11's \(c^{n}\) is the case \(s=n\).

    Scaling by \(c\) maps \(\delta\)-covers of \(A\) bijectively to \(c\delta\)-covers of \(cA\) and multiplies every diameter by \(c\), hence every summand by \(c^{s}\). Taking infima and letting \(\delta\to0\) gives the identity. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Behaviour under Lipschitz maps: \(\mathcal{H}^{s}(f(A)) \le K^{s}\mathcal{H}^{s}(A)\) for \(K\)-Lipschitz \(f\); isometries preserve \(\mathcal H^{s}\).

    You cannot increase dimension by a Lipschitz map — the key fact behind everything in Layer 2.

    If \(\{U_i\}\) is a \(\delta\)-cover of \(A\), then \(\{f(U_i\cap A)\}\) is a \(K\delta\)-cover of \(f(A)\) with \(\operatorname{diam}f(U_i\cap A)\le K\operatorname{diam}U_i\). Summing the \(s\)-th powers gives the bound. For an isometry, \(K=1\) in both directions. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Translation and rotation invariance.

    Moving a set doesn't change its size — as any measure worth the name must satisfy.

    Both are isometries of \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\), so the previous theorem applies with \(K=1\) in each direction. \(\blacksquare\)

  • The dimension threshold: if \(\mathcal{H}^{s}(A)<\infty\) and \(t > s\) then \(\mathcal{H}^{t}(A) = 0\); if \(\mathcal H^{s}(A)>0\) and \(t < s\) then \(\mathcal H^{t}(A)=\infty\).

    There is exactly one exponent where the answer is interesting. Everywhere else it is 0 or \(\infty\).

    For a \(\delta\)-cover, \(\sum(\operatorname{diam}U_i)^{t} \le \delta^{\,t-s}\sum(\operatorname{diam}U_i)^{s}\). If \(\mathcal H^{s}(A)\) is finite, the right side tends to 0 as \(\delta\to0\), so \(\mathcal H^{t}(A)=0\). The second statement is the contrapositive with the roles of \(s\) and \(t\) exchanged. \(\blacksquare\)

  • \(\mathcal H^{0}\) is counting measure and \(\mathcal H^{1}\) on a rectifiable curve is its length.

    The new ruler reproduces the old answers, which is the least it must do.

    For \(s=0\) each covering set contributes \(\alpha_0 = 1\), so the infimum counts the points. For a curve, projecting onto the chord between two points is 1-Lipschitz, so \(\mathcal H^{1}\) is at least the distance between endpoints, hence at least every inscribed polygon's length; conversely covering the curve by short arcs gives the reverse inequality. Chapter 14, Layer 1 identified that supremum with the arc-length integral. \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Cover efficiently to bound \(\mathcal H^{s}\) above.

    Exhibit one good cover and you have an upper bound. This is always the easy direction.

  • Project or use a mass distribution to bound below.

    Lower bounds are the hard direction; Layer 3's mass distribution principle is the standard tool.

  • Use Lipschitz maps to transfer estimates.

    Dimension never increases under a Lipschitz map, and never changes under a bi-Lipschitz one.

Layer 2 · The critical exponent

Hausdorff Dimension

Where the measure jumps from infinite to zero.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Hausdorff dimension \(\dim_HA = \inf\{s : \mathcal{H}^{s}(A) = 0\} = \sup\{s : \mathcal H^{s}(A)=\infty\}\).

    The single number where the answer switches. At \(s = \dim_HA\) itself the measure may be \(0\), finite, or \(\infty\).

  • \(s\)-set — a set with \(0 < \mathcal{H}^{s}(A) < \infty\).

    The well-behaved case: the set has a genuine finite "\(s\)-dimensional size".

  • Bi-Lipschitz map — Lipschitz with a Lipschitz inverse.

    Stretches by a bounded factor in both directions. Dimension cannot notice.

  • Box-counting (Minkowski) dimension \(\dim_B A = \lim\frac{\log N(\varepsilon)}{\log(1/\varepsilon)}\).

    Count the \(\varepsilon\)-boxes needed to cover the set. Easier to compute, and sometimes wrong.

  • Topological dimension — defined inductively from the dimension of boundaries.

    Always an integer, and never larger than the Hausdorff dimension. Mandelbrot defined "fractal" as the gap between them.

  • Graph dimension — the dimension of a function's graph.

    A smooth graph has dimension 1; the Weierstrass nowhere-differentiable function's graph exceeds it.

Theorems

  • The dimension is well defined — the two descriptions agree.

    A single threshold, approached from both sides.

    By Layer 1's threshold theorem, \(\{s : \mathcal H^{s}(A)=0\}\) is an up-set and \(\{s : \mathcal H^{s}(A)=\infty\}\) a down-set, and every \(s\) lies in one or the other except possibly the single boundary value. So the infimum of the first equals the supremum of the second. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Monotonicity and countable stability: \(A\subseteq B \Rightarrow \dim_HA\le\dim_HB\), and \(\dim_H\bigcup_kA_k = \sup_k\dim_HA_k\).

    Countably many low-dimensional pieces cannot build a higher-dimensional whole — unlike box dimension, which fails this.

    Monotonicity is monotonicity of \(\mathcal H^{s}\). For the union, \(\ge\) follows from monotonicity. For \(\le\): if \(s > \sup_k\dim_HA_k\) then \(\mathcal H^{s}(A_k)=0\) for every \(k\), so countable subadditivity gives \(\mathcal H^{s}(\bigcup_kA_k)=0\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Corollary: countable sets have dimension 0.

    \(\mathbb{Q}\) is dense in \(\mathbb{R}\) and 0-dimensional. Density has nothing to do with size.

    A single point has \(\mathcal H^{s} = 0\) for every \(s > 0\), hence dimension 0; countable stability extends this to any countable union. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Lipschitz maps do not increase dimension; bi-Lipschitz maps preserve it.

    Dimension is a bi-Lipschitz invariant — the reason it is a genuine geometric quantity.

    Layer 1: \(\mathcal H^{s}(f(A)) \le K^{s}\mathcal H^{s}(A)\), so \(\mathcal H^{s}(A) = 0\) forces \(\mathcal H^{s}(f(A))=0\), giving \(\dim_Hf(A)\le\dim_HA\). Applying this to \(f^{-1}\) in the bi-Lipschitz case gives the reverse. \(\blacksquare\)

  • \(\mathcal{H}^{n} = \lambda_n\) on \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\); in particular \(\dim_H\mathbb{R}^{n} = n\).

    The new ruler agrees with Lebesgue measure exactly — normalising constant included. That is what \(\alpha_s\) was chosen for.

    Both are translation-invariant Borel measures finite on boxes, so by Chapter 11's uniqueness theorem \(\mathcal H^{n} = c\lambda_n\). Identifying \(c=1\) requires the isodiametric inequality (Layer 4): among sets of a given diameter the ball has the largest volume, which makes the normalising constant \(\alpha_n\) exactly right. \(\blacksquare\)

  • \(\dim_H\) of a smooth \(k\)-manifold is \(k\), with \(\mathcal H^{k}\) equal to its Chapter 14 area.

    Everything smooth gets the answer you already knew — the theory only adds new answers for rough sets.

    Locally the manifold is the bi-Lipschitz image of an open subset of \(\mathbb{R}^{k}\) (a chart with bounded derivative on a compact piece), so its dimension is \(k\); the area formula of Layer 4 identifies \(\mathcal H^{k}\) with the Gram-determinant integral. Countable stability handles the whole manifold. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Box dimension bounds: \(\dim_HA \le \underline{\dim}_BA \le \overline{\dim}_BA\), and the inequalities can be strict.

    \(\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]\) has Hausdorff dimension 0 and box dimension 1 — box counting cannot see that the set is countable.

    Covering by \(N(\varepsilon)\) sets of diameter \(\varepsilon\) gives \(\mathcal H^{s}_\varepsilon(A)\lesssim N(\varepsilon)\varepsilon^{s}\), which tends to 0 whenever \(s > \overline{\dim}_BA\); hence \(\dim_H \le \overline{\dim}_B\). For strictness, note that \(N(\varepsilon)\approx1/\varepsilon\) for \(\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]\) since its closure is the interval, giving box dimension 1, while countable stability gives Hausdorff dimension 0. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Dimension of a product: \(\dim_H(A\times B) \ge \dim_HA+\dim_HB\), with equality in the well-behaved cases.

    Dimensions add for products — usually. The inequality can be strict for pathological sets.

    Sketch: a mass distribution argument. If \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) satisfy \(\mu(B_r)\lesssim r^{s}\) and \(\nu(B_r)\lesssim r^{t}\), then \(\mu\times\nu(B_r)\lesssim r^{s+t}\), and the mass distribution principle (Layer 3) gives the lower bound. Upper bounds require covers of the product built from covers of the factors, which need not be efficient — hence the possible strictness. \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Bound above by covering — one good cover gives an upper bound on dimension.

    Usually built from the set's own construction stages.

  • Bound below with a mass distribution.

    Put a measure on the set and show it doesn't concentrate too much on small balls.

  • Use bi-Lipschitz equivalence to transfer known dimensions.

    If your set is a distorted copy of a known one, you're done.

  • Prefer Hausdorff over box dimension for unions.

    Countable stability is exactly what box dimension lacks.

Layer 3 · Non-integer dimension

Self-Similar Sets & Fractals

Sets that look the same at every scale, and the exponent that describes them.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Contracting similarity \(S_i\) with ratio \(r_i < 1\).

    Shrink by a fixed factor, then move. Composition of a scaling with a rigid motion.

  • Iterated function system (IFS) — a finite family \(\{S_1,\dots,S_m\}\).

    A recipe: replace each copy of the shape by \(m\) smaller copies, forever.

  • Attractor — the unique compact \(F\) with \(F = \bigcup_iS_i(F)\).

    The set the recipe converges to, whatever you start from.

  • Similarity dimension — the \(s\) solving \(\sum_ir_i^{s} = 1\).

    For \(m\) copies each of ratio \(r\): \(s = \log m/\log(1/r)\).

  • Open set condition — the copies overlap only at their edges.

    Without it the pieces double-count and the formula overestimates.

  • Cantor set — remove middle thirds; \(m=2\), \(r=1/3\).

    \(\dim_H = \log2/\log3 \approx 0.631\). Uncountable, of length zero, and dimension strictly between 0 and 1.

  • Koch curve — \(m=4\), \(r=1/3\).

    \(\dim_H = \log4/\log3\approx1.262\). Infinite length, zero area — genuinely between a curve and a surface.

  • Sierpiński triangle — \(m=3\), \(r=1/2\).

    \(\dim_H = \log3/\log2\approx1.585\).

  • Mass distribution — a finite measure supported on the set.

    Spread one kilogram over the fractal. How it distributes reveals the dimension.

  • Space-filling curve — a continuous surjection \([0,1]\to[0,1]^{2}\).

    Peano's curve. Continuous images can raise dimension — which is why Layer 1 insisted on Lipschitz.

Theorems

  • Existence and uniqueness of the attractor.

    Chapter 7's fixed point theorem, applied to the space of compact sets.

    Let \(\mathcal K\) be the compact subsets of \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\) with the Hausdorff metric \(d_H\); this is a complete metric space. The map \(\mathcal S(A) = \bigcup_iS_i(A)\) satisfies \(d_H(\mathcal S(A),\mathcal S(B))\le(\max_ir_i)\,d_H(A,B)\), a contraction. Banach's fixed point theorem (Chapter 7, Layer 4) gives a unique fixed point, reached from any starting compact set. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Mass distribution principle: if \(\mu(A)>0\) and \(\mu(U)\le c(\operatorname{diam}U)^{s}\) for all small \(U\), then \(\mathcal{H}^{s}(A)\ge\mu(A)/c > 0\), so \(\dim_HA\ge s\).

    If no small set can carry much mass, you need many small sets — which is exactly a lower bound on dimension.

    For any \(\delta\)-cover \(\{U_i\}\) of \(A\), \(\mu(A) \le \sum_i\mu(U_i) \le c\sum_i(\operatorname{diam}U_i)^{s}\). Taking the infimum over covers gives \(\mu(A)\le c\,\mathcal H^{s}_\delta(A)\), and letting \(\delta\to0\) gives the claim. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Moran–Hutchinson theorem: under the open set condition, \(\dim_HF = s\) where \(\sum_ir_i^{s}=1\), and \(0<\mathcal H^{s}(F)<\infty\).

    The similarity dimension is the real dimension. One equation answers every self-similar set.

    Upper bound: the \(k\)-th iterate covers \(F\) by \(m^{k}\) sets of diameters \(r_{i_1}\cdots r_{i_k}\operatorname{diam}F\), and \(\sum_{|i|=k}(r_{i_1}\cdots r_{i_k})^{s} = (\sum_ir_i^{s})^{k} = 1\), so \(\mathcal H^{s}(F)\le(\operatorname{diam}F)^{s}\). Lower bound: define \(\mu\) by assigning mass \(r_{i_1}^{s}\cdots r_{i_k}^{s}\) to each \(k\)-th level piece — a genuine measure since the weights sum to 1 at every level — and use the open set condition to show \(\mu(U)\lesssim(\operatorname{diam}U)^{s}\). The mass distribution principle finishes. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Cantor set: \(\dim_H = \log2/\log3\), with \(0 < \mathcal H^{s} < \infty\).

    A set of length zero and dimension \(0.631\ldots\) — the first honest example of fractional dimension.

    The two maps \(x\mapsto x/3\) and \(x\mapsto x/3+2/3\) satisfy the open set condition with \(U = (0,1)\), and \(2(1/3)^{s}=1\) gives \(s = \log2/\log3\). Moran–Hutchinson applies. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Koch curve: \(\dim_H = \log4/\log3\); it has infinite length and zero area.

    Too big to be a curve, too small to be a region. The word "fractal" was invented for this.

    Four maps of ratio \(1/3\) satisfying the open set condition give \(s = \log4/\log3\approx1.262\) by Moran–Hutchinson. Since \(s > 1\), \(\mathcal H^{1} = \infty\) (infinite length) and since \(s < 2\), \(\mathcal H^{2} = \lambda_2 = 0\) (zero area), by Layer 1's threshold theorem. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Box dimension of self-similar sets equals the Hausdorff dimension, under the open set condition.

    For nice fractals the easy computation gives the right answer.

    At level \(k\), the natural cover uses \(m^{k}\) pieces of size \(\approx r^{k}\), so \(N(\varepsilon)\approx\varepsilon^{-s}\) with \(s = \log m/\log(1/r)\), giving box dimension \(s\). Since \(\dim_H\le\dim_B\) always (Layer 2) and Moran–Hutchinson gives \(\dim_H = s\), the two coincide. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Continuous maps can raise dimension: a space-filling curve maps \([0,1]\) onto \([0,1]^{2}\).

    So "Lipschitz" in Layer 1 was not a technicality. Continuity alone controls nothing.

    Peano's construction: build \(f_k\) by iterating a pattern that visits each of \(4^{k}\) subsquares in order. The \(f_k\) converge uniformly (each stage moves points by at most \(2^{-k}\)) to a continuous \(f\), and every point of the square is a limit of visited points, so \(f\) is surjective. Such a map cannot be Lipschitz, since Lipschitz maps do not increase dimension. \(\blacksquare\)

  • The Weierstrass function is nowhere differentiable, and its graph has dimension \(>1\).

    Continuous everywhere, smooth nowhere. Baire's theorem (Chapter 7) says that is the typical case, not the exception.

    Sketch: for \(W(x) = \sum_k a^{k}\cos(b^{k}\pi x)\) with \(0<a<1\), \(ab > 1\), the M-test (Chapter 9, Layer 4) gives uniform convergence hence continuity. Comparing increments at scale \(b^{-k}\) shows \(|W(x+h)-W(x)|\gtrsim|h|^{\alpha}\) with \(\alpha = \log(1/a)/\log b < 1\) infinitely often, so no finite derivative can exist; the same Hölder exponent forces the graph's box dimension to be \(2-\alpha > 1\). \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Solve \(\sum r_i^{s} = 1\) — the fastest route to a fractal's dimension.

    Check the open set condition first, or the answer may be too large.

  • Build the natural measure — distribute mass equally among the copies.

    Then apply the mass distribution principle for the lower bound.

  • Count boxes at each construction stage.

    \(N(\varepsilon)\approx\varepsilon^{-s}\); read off \(s\) from a log-log plot.

  • Iterate a contraction on compact sets — the attractor is a fixed point.

    Start from any shape; after a dozen iterations you cannot tell the difference.

Layer 4 · Back to smoothness

Rectifiability & the Area Formula

Where Hausdorff measure and Chapter 14 agree.

Concepts & Definitions

  • \(k\)-rectifiable set — covered, up to \(\mathcal H^{k}\)-null, by countably many Lipschitz images of \(\mathbb{R}^{k}\).

    "Smooth apart from a negligible mess". The right class for a general theory of surface area.

  • Purely unrectifiable set — meets every Lipschitz \(k\)-image in an \(\mathcal H^{k}\)-null set.

    Fractal dust of the right dimension but no direction anywhere. The opposite extreme.

  • Approximate tangent plane — a plane the set looks like under blow-up at almost every point.

    Zoom in far enough on a rectifiable set and it flattens out — almost everywhere.

  • Area formula — counts multiplicity when the map is not injective.

    Chapter 12's change of variables, without the requirement of a bijection.

  • Coarea formula — Fubini along the level sets of a map.

    Slice a solid by a family of curved surfaces instead of parallel planes.

  • Isodiametric inequality — among sets of given diameter, the ball has the largest volume.

    The normalising fact that makes \(\mathcal H^{n} = \lambda_n\) exactly, not merely up to a constant.

  • Minkowski content — \(\lim\frac{\lambda(A_\varepsilon)}{\alpha_{n-k}\varepsilon^{\,n-k}}\), a "thickening" definition of area.

    Paint the surface with a thin coat and measure the paint. It agrees with \(\mathcal H^{k}\) on rectifiable sets.

Theorems

  • Isodiametric inequality: \(\lambda_n(A) \le \alpha_n\bigl(\tfrac{\operatorname{diam}A}{2}\bigr)^{n}\).

    A set of diameter 2 can have volume at most that of the unit ball. Obvious-looking, and not obvious — the set need not be contained in any such ball.

    Sketch: apply Steiner symmetrisation in each coordinate direction. Each step preserves volume, does not increase the diameter, and moves the set towards a symmetric one; the limit is a centrally symmetric set of the same volume and no larger diameter, which is contained in the ball of that radius. \(\blacksquare\)

  • \(\mathcal H^{n} = \lambda_n\) on \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\), with constant exactly 1.

    Chapter 11's volume is a special case of Hausdorff measure. One theory, not two.

    Both are translation-invariant Borel measures finite on boxes, so \(\mathcal H^{n} = c\lambda_n\) by Chapter 11's uniqueness theorem. The isodiametric inequality gives \(\lambda_n(U)\le\alpha_n(\operatorname{diam}U/2)^{n}\) for each covering set, so \(\lambda_n(A)\le\mathcal H^{n}(A)\), i.e. \(c\ge1\); covering a cube by small cubes gives the reverse. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Area formula: for Lipschitz \(f : \mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}\) (\(k\le n\)), \[\int_A J f\,dx = \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\#\bigl(A\cap f^{-1}(y)\bigr)\,d\mathcal{H}^{k}(y),\] with \(Jf = \sqrt{\det(Df^{\mathsf T}Df)}\).

    Chapter 14's surface area formula, freed from injectivity: overlapping sheets are simply counted twice.

    Sketch: Rademacher's theorem gives differentiability a.e. On a set where \(Df\) is nearly constant, \(f\) is nearly linear, and the linear case is Chapter 14, Layer 4's statement about \(\sqrt{\det(A^{\mathsf T}A)}\). Decompose the domain into countably many such pieces on which \(f\) is injective and bi-Lipschitz (using the rank theorem where \(Jf>0\), and noting \(\{Jf = 0\}\) contributes nothing), then sum. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Corollary: for an injective \(C^{1}\) parametrisation, \(\mathcal H^{k}(\text{image}) = \int\sqrt{\det G}\), reproducing Chapter 14.

    The two definitions of surface area coincide wherever both make sense.

    Injectivity makes the multiplicity function equal to 1 on the image and 0 elsewhere, so the right-hand side of the area formula is \(\mathcal H^{k}(\text{image})\), while the left is the Gram-determinant integral. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Coarea formula: for Lipschitz \(f : \mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}\) (\(m\le n\)), \[\int_A Jf\,dx = \int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\mathcal H^{\,n-m}\bigl(A\cap f^{-1}(y)\bigr)\,dy,\quad Jf = \sqrt{\det(Df\,Df^{\mathsf T})}.\]

    Fubini along curved slices. Taking \(f(x) = \|x\|\) gives Chapter 12's spherical coordinates.

    Sketch: as for the area formula, reduce by Rademacher and a decomposition to the case where \(f\) is linear, where the identity is the linear change of variables plus Fubini. The rank theorem straightens \(f\) locally into a projection, and the factor \(Jf\) records the distortion. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Rademacher's theorem: a Lipschitz function on \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\) is differentiable almost everywhere.

    A speed limit forces smoothness almost everywhere, even if the function is nowhere smooth in the classical sense.

    Sketch: for a fixed direction \(v\), the one-variable function \(t\mapsto f(x+tv)\) is Lipschitz, hence differentiable a.e. (a monotonicity/Lebesgue differentiation argument); Fubini makes \(D_vf\) exist a.e. in \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\). Taking a countable dense set of directions and using the uniform Lipschitz bound shows the resulting candidate linear map really is the derivative at a.e. point. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Besicovitch structure theorem (stated): every \(\mathcal H^{k}\)-finite set splits into a rectifiable part and a purely unrectifiable part.

    Every set of finite \(k\)-area is a smooth piece plus fractal dust, with nothing in between.

    Sketch: let \(R\) be the union of a maximal countable family of rectifiable subsets — a supremum argument makes this well defined up to null sets — and let \(P\) be the remainder. By maximality, \(P\) can contain no rectifiable subset of positive measure, i.e. it is purely unrectifiable. The deep half of Besicovitch's theorem shows \(P\) additionally projects to a null set in almost every direction. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Minkowski content agrees with \(\mathcal H^{k}\) on compact rectifiable sets.

    Painting the surface and measuring the paint really does give the area — for reasonable surfaces.

    Sketch: for a compact piece of a \(C^{1}\) manifold, the \(\varepsilon\)-neighbourhood is, to first order, the normal bundle of thickness \(\varepsilon\), whose volume is \(\alpha_{n-k}\varepsilon^{\,n-k}\mathcal H^{k}\) plus curvature corrections of higher order (Chapter 19's Steiner formula makes this exact for convex bodies). Dividing and letting \(\varepsilon\to0\) gives the identity; countable additivity extends it to rectifiable sets. \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Use the area formula for non-injective parametrisations.

    Count multiplicity instead of insisting on a bijection.

  • Slice with the coarea formula.

    Turns an \(n\)-dimensional integral into a family of \((n-m)\)-dimensional ones.

  • Check rectifiability before expecting good behaviour.

    Purely unrectifiable sets defy every geometric intuition.

  • Thicken and measure — Minkowski content as a practical substitute.

    Especially useful numerically, where covers are awkward.
