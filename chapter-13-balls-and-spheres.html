Balls & Spheres

❮ Chapter 12 — Integration Start Learning ❯

The Payoff

Twelve chapters of machinery, aimed at one concrete question: how much space does a ball in \(n\) dimensions occupy, and how much skin does it have?

The answers involve \(\pi\) and the Gamma function, and they behave in ways nobody expects. The unit ball's volume peaks at \(n=5\) and then collapses towards zero.

The last two layers explain why high-dimensional space is so counterintuitive — and why that matters for statistics, data and physics.

Layer 1 · The tool

The Gamma Function

Factorials for numbers that aren't whole.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Gamma function \(\Gamma(s) = \int_0^{\infty}t^{s-1}e^{-t}\,dt\), for \(s > 0\).

    A single integral that interpolates the factorials — and it appears the moment you ask for the volume of a ball.

  • Beta function \(B(a,b) = \int_0^{1}t^{a-1}(1-t)^{b-1}dt\).

    Gamma's companion. It computes the slices of a ball.

  • Double factorial \(n!! = n(n-2)(n-4)\cdots\).

    Skips every other term. The odd-dimensional ball formulas are cleanest written this way.

  • Stirling's approximation \(n! \sim \sqrt{2\pi n}\,(n/e)^{n}\).

    Factorials grow faster than any exponential. It is what makes the ball's volume collapse.

  • Asymptotic equivalence \(f\sim g\) — the ratio tends to 1.

    "Eventually the same size", even if the difference grows without bound.

Theorems

  • Convergence: the Gamma integral converges for every \(s > 0\).

    The two ends behave differently: \(t^{s-1}\) controls the origin, \(e^{-t}\) controls infinity.

    Near 0, \(t^{s-1}e^{-t}\le t^{s-1}\), integrable there exactly when \(s > 0\). Near \(\infty\), \(t^{s-1}e^{-t}\le C_se^{-t/2}\), which is integrable. Splitting the integral at 1 and adding gives convergence. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Functional equation: \(\Gamma(s+1) = s\,\Gamma(s)\).

    The recurrence that makes \(\Gamma\) a factorial in disguise.

    Integrate by parts: \(\Gamma(s+1) = \int_0^{\infty}t^{s}e^{-t}dt = \bigl[-t^{s}e^{-t}\bigr]_0^{\infty} + s\int_0^{\infty}t^{s-1}e^{-t}dt\). The boundary term vanishes at both ends for \(s > 0\), leaving \(s\Gamma(s)\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • \(\Gamma(n) = (n-1)!\) for positive integers.

    \(\Gamma(1)=1\), \(\Gamma(2)=1\), \(\Gamma(3)=2\), \(\Gamma(4)=6\). The factorials, shifted by one.

    \(\Gamma(1) = \int_0^{\infty}e^{-t}dt = 1\), and induction with the functional equation gives \(\Gamma(n+1) = n\Gamma(n) = n\cdot(n-1)! = n!\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • \(\Gamma(\tfrac12) = \sqrt\pi\), and \(\Gamma\bigl(n+\tfrac12\bigr) = \frac{(2n)!}{4^{n}n!}\sqrt\pi\).

    The \(\sqrt\pi\) is the Gaussian integral in disguise — which is exactly why \(\pi\) appears in every ball volume.

    Substitute \(t = u^{2}\): \(\Gamma(\tfrac12) = \int_0^{\infty}t^{-1/2}e^{-t}dt = 2\int_0^{\infty}e^{-u^{2}}du = \sqrt\pi\) by the Gaussian integral (Chapter 12, Layer 4). The half-integer values then follow by repeated use of the functional equation. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Beta–Gamma relation: \(B(a,b) = \frac{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)}{\Gamma(a+b)}\).

    Two integrals collapse into one identity — proved by a change of variables in the plane.

    By Tonelli, \(\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b) = \int_0^{\infty}\!\!\int_0^{\infty}x^{a-1}y^{b-1}e^{-(x+y)}dx\,dy\). Substitute \(x = st\), \(y = s(1-t)\) with \(s > 0\), \(t\in(0,1)\); the Jacobian is \(s\), and the integral factorises into \(\int_0^{\infty}s^{a+b-1}e^{-s}ds \cdot \int_0^{1}t^{a-1}(1-t)^{b-1}dt = \Gamma(a+b)B(a,b)\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Stirling's formula: \(\Gamma(s+1)\sim\sqrt{2\pi s}\,(s/e)^{s}\).

    Everything about high-dimensional volume follows from how fast this grows.

    Sketch (Laplace's method): write \(\Gamma(s+1) = \int_0^{\infty}e^{s\ln t - t}dt\). The exponent \(\varphi(t) = s\ln t - t\) is maximised at \(t = s\), with \(\varphi(s) = s\ln s - s\) and \(\varphi''(s) = -1/s\). Substituting \(t = s+u\sqrt s\) and expanding to second order gives \(\Gamma(s+1)\approx e^{s\ln s - s}\sqrt s\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-u^{2}/2}du = \sqrt{2\pi s}\,(s/e)^{s}\), and dominated convergence justifies the limit. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Log-convexity (Bohr–Mollerup): \(\Gamma\) is the unique log-convex function with \(\Gamma(1)=1\) satisfying \(\Gamma(s+1)=s\Gamma(s)\).

    Not just an extension of the factorial — the only sensible one.

    Log-convexity follows from Hölder's inequality applied to the integral. For uniqueness, the functional equation determines \(f\) on \((0,1]\) from its values elsewhere; log-convexity sandwiches \(\ln f(s)\) between the linear interpolations at consecutive integers, and letting the integer tend to infinity squeezes \(f\) to \(\Gamma\). \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Use the functional equation to shift arguments into a range you know.

    Every half-integer value reduces to \(\Gamma(\tfrac12)=\sqrt\pi\).

  • Recognise a Beta integral — \(\int_0^{1}t^{a-1}(1-t)^{b-1}\).

    It appears whenever you slice a ball perpendicular to an axis.

  • Stirling for asymptotics — replace factorials by their growth rate.

    The only practical way to see what a formula does as \(n\to\infty\).

Layer 2 · The formulas

Volume of the Ball, Area of the Sphere

Two closed forms, and the relation between them.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Unit ball \(B^{n} = \{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n} : \|x\|\le1\}\), of volume \(V_n\).

    \(V_1 = 2\), \(V_2 = \pi\), \(V_3 = \frac43\pi\). Then it starts to misbehave.

  • Unit sphere \(S^{n-1} = \{x : \|x\|=1\}\), of \((n-1)\)-dimensional area \(S_{n-1}\).

    The skin of the ball. In \(\mathbb{R}^{3}\) it is the familiar \(4\pi\).

  • Radius scaling — \(\operatorname{vol}(rB^{n}) = r^{n}V_n\).

    Chapter 11's scaling law. The exponent is the dimension — the idea Chapter 18 generalises.

  • Surface measure \(\sigma\) on \(S^{n-1}\).

    Defined by Chapter 12's spherical coordinates; Chapter 14 constructs it from first principles.

  • Solid angle — the fraction of \(S^{n-1}\) subtended by a cone.

    The \(n\)-dimensional version of "how wide does it look from here".

Theorems

  • Volume of the unit ball: \(V_n = \dfrac{\pi^{n/2}}{\Gamma\bigl(\frac n2+1\bigr)}\).

    \(V_4 = \pi^{2}/2 \approx 4.93\), \(V_5\approx5.26\), \(V_6\approx5.17\). The peak is at \(n=5\).

    Compute \(I = \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-\|x\|^{2}}dx\) in two ways. Cartesian: \(I = \pi^{n/2}\) (Chapter 12, Layer 4). Radially: by the layer-cake or spherical coordinates, \(I = \int_0^{\infty}e^{-r^{2}}\,d\bigl(V_nr^{n}\bigr) = V_n\!\int_0^{\infty}nr^{n-1}e^{-r^{2}}dr\). Substituting \(t = r^{2}\) turns the last integral into \(\tfrac n2\Gamma(\tfrac n2) = \Gamma(\tfrac n2+1)\). Equating gives the formula. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Surface area of the unit sphere: \(S_{n-1} = \dfrac{2\pi^{n/2}}{\Gamma\bigl(\frac n2\bigr)} = n\,V_n\).

    \(n=3\): \(S_2 = 3\cdot\frac43\pi = 4\pi\). The familiar answer, from the general formula.

    Differentiate the volume of the ball of radius \(r\): \(\frac{d}{dr}\bigl(V_nr^{n}\bigr) = nV_nr^{n-1}\), which is the surface area of the sphere of radius \(r\) — a thin shell of thickness \(dr\) has volume (area)\(\times dr\). At \(r=1\) this gives \(S_{n-1} = nV_n\), and substituting the volume formula with \(\Gamma(\tfrac n2+1) = \tfrac n2\Gamma(\tfrac n2)\) yields the closed form. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Recursion: \(V_n = \dfrac{2\pi}{n}V_{n-2}\), with \(V_1 = 2\), \(V_2 = \pi\).

    Each two extra dimensions multiply the volume by \(2\pi/n\) — a factor that drops below 1 once \(n > 6\).

    Immediate from the closed form: \(\frac{V_n}{V_{n-2}} = \pi\cdot\frac{\Gamma(\frac n2)}{\Gamma(\frac n2+1)} = \frac{\pi}{n/2} = \frac{2\pi}{n}\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Slicing recursion: \(V_n = V_{n-1}\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}(1-t^{2})^{\frac{n-1}{2}}dt\).

    Cut the ball into parallel discs. The cross-section at height \(t\) is a ball of radius \(\sqrt{1-t^{2}}\).

    By Fubini (Chapter 12, Layer 3), \(V_n = \int_{-1}^{1}\operatorname{vol}_{n-1}\bigl(\sqrt{1-t^{2}}\,B^{n-1}\bigr)dt = V_{n-1}\int_{-1}^{1}(1-t^{2})^{(n-1)/2}dt\), using the scaling law. Substituting \(u = t^{2}\) turns the integral into a Beta function, which reproduces the closed form via Layer 1. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Explicit forms: \(V_{2m} = \dfrac{\pi^{m}}{m!}\) and \(V_{2m+1} = \dfrac{2^{m+1}\pi^{m}}{(2m+1)!!}\).

    Even dimensions are the clean case: \(V_4 = \pi^{2}/2\), \(V_6 = \pi^{3}/6\).

    Put \(n = 2m\) in the closed form: \(\Gamma(m+1) = m!\), giving \(\pi^{m}/m!\). Put \(n = 2m+1\): \(\Gamma(m+\tfrac32) = \frac{(2m+1)!!}{2^{m+1}}\sqrt\pi\) by repeated use of the functional equation from \(\Gamma(\tfrac12)=\sqrt\pi\), and the \(\sqrt\pi\) cancels one factor of \(\pi^{1/2}\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Volume of a ball of radius \(r\): \(V_n r^{n}\); surface \(S_{n-1}r^{n-1}\).

    Double the radius in 10 dimensions and the volume grows by a factor of 1024.

    Chapter 11, Layer 3's scaling law \(\lambda(cE) = |c|^{n}\lambda(E)\), applied with \(c=r\); differentiating in \(r\) gives the surface statement as above. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Asymptotics: \(V_n \sim \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{n\pi}}\Bigl(\dfrac{2\pi e}{n}\Bigr)^{n/2} \to 0\) rapidly.

    \(V_{20}\approx0.026\); \(V_{100}\approx 10^{-40}\). The unit ball essentially vanishes inside its own bounding cube.

    Apply Stirling to \(\Gamma(\tfrac n2+1)\sim\sqrt{\pi n}\bigl(\tfrac{n}{2e}\bigr)^{n/2}\) and substitute into \(V_n = \pi^{n/2}/\Gamma(\tfrac n2+1)\). The ratio \((2\pi e/n)^{n/2}\) tends to 0 super-exponentially once \(n > 2\pi e \approx 17\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • The peak: \(V_n\) increases up to \(n=5\) and decreases thereafter; \(S_{n-1}\) peaks at \(n=7\).

    There is a "best" dimension for a unit ball to live in, and it is five. Nothing in the geometry suggests it in advance.

    From the recursion, \(V_n > V_{n-2}\) exactly when \(2\pi/n > 1\), i.e. \(n < 2\pi \approx 6.28\). Checking the two interleaved sequences (odd and even \(n\)) against the explicit values \(V_4\approx4.93\), \(V_5\approx5.26\), \(V_6\approx5.17\) identifies \(n=5\) as the maximum. The same computation for \(S_{n-1} = nV_n\) shifts the peak to \(n=7\). \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • The Gaussian trick — compute a radial integral two ways to extract \(V_n\).

    The cleanest derivation, and it generalises to other radial weights.

  • Slice with Fubini — reduce dimension \(n\) to dimension \(n-1\).

    Archimedes' method, with the bookkeeping made rigorous.

  • Differentiate volume to get surface — \(S = dV/dr\).

    One-line derivations of \(4\pi r^{2}\) from \(\frac43\pi r^{3}\), in any dimension.

  • Apply Stirling — to see what a formula does for large \(n\).

    Closed forms hide their behaviour; asymptotics reveal it.

Layer 3 · Where intuition breaks

High-Dimensional Geometry

Six facts that are false in three dimensions and true in three hundred.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Unit cube \([-1,1]^{n}\), of volume \(2^{n}\).

    The natural container for the unit ball. Their ratio is what goes wrong.

  • Diagonal of the cube — length \(2\sqrt n\).

    The corners run away from the centre while the faces stay put.

  • Annulus (shell) \(\{1-\varepsilon \le \|x\|\le1\}\).

    The skin of the ball. In high dimensions the skin is almost the whole fruit.

  • Equatorial band \(\{|x_1|\le t\}\cap S^{n-1}\).

    A strip around the equator. It contains nearly all the sphere's area for tiny \(t\).

  • Curse of dimensionality — sample size requirements grow exponentially with \(n\).

    Ten points cover an interval decently. Covering a 10-dimensional cube to the same resolution takes \(10^{10}\).

Theorems

  • The ball vanishes inside its cube: \(V_n/2^{n}\to0\) faster than exponentially.

    In 20 dimensions the inscribed ball occupies about \(2\times10^{-8}\) of the cube. Essentially all of the cube is in the corners.

    By Layer 2's asymptotics, \(V_n/2^{n} \sim \frac{1}{\sqrt{n\pi}}\bigl(\frac{\pi e}{2n}\bigr)^{n/2}\), which tends to 0 super-exponentially. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Corner escape: the cube's diagonal has length \(2\sqrt n\) while the inscribed ball has radius 1.

    In 100 dimensions the corners are 10 times further from the centre than the faces are.

    The corner \((\pm1,\dots,\pm1)\) has norm \(\sqrt{\sum_i1} = \sqrt n\), while the face centre \((1,0,\dots,0)\) has norm 1. The full diagonal is twice the corner distance. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Thin shell: for fixed \(\varepsilon > 0\), the fraction of the ball within radius \(1-\varepsilon\) is \((1-\varepsilon)^{n}\to0\).

    99% of a 1000-dimensional ball's volume lies in the outer 0.5% of its radius. A high-dimensional orange is all peel.

    The inner ball has volume \((1-\varepsilon)^{n}V_n\) by the scaling law, so the ratio is \((1-\varepsilon)^{n}\), which tends to 0 geometrically for any fixed \(\varepsilon > 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Equatorial concentration: for fixed \(t > 0\), the fraction of \(S^{n-1}\) with \(|x_1| > t\) is at most \(2e^{-nt^{2}/2}\).

    Almost all of a high-dimensional sphere lies within a hair's breadth of any chosen equator — and of every other equator simultaneously.

    Sketch: the density of the first coordinate of a uniform point on \(S^{n-1}\) is proportional to \((1-s^{2})^{(n-3)/2}\) (Layer 2's slicing). Then \(\Pr(|x_1|>t) = \frac{2\int_t^{1}(1-s^{2})^{(n-3)/2}ds}{\int_{-1}^{1}(1-s^{2})^{(n-3)/2}ds}\), and the bound \((1-s^{2})^{(n-3)/2}\le e^{-(n-3)s^{2}/2}\) with a comparison of the two Gaussian-type integrals gives the stated exponential estimate. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Near-orthogonality: two independent uniform points on \(S^{n-1}\) have \(\langle x,y\rangle\) of typical size \(1/\sqrt n\).

    Random directions in high dimensions are almost always nearly perpendicular. This is why random projections preserve so much structure.

    By rotational invariance, fix \(y = e_1\); then \(\langle x,y\rangle = x_1\), whose distribution is the one above, with mean 0 and variance \(1/n\) (the coordinates are exchangeable and \(\sum_ix_i^{2}=1\), so \(\mathbb{E}[x_i^{2}]=1/n\)). Chebyshev then bounds deviations beyond a few multiples of \(1/\sqrt n\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Volume of the intersection with a half-space: a slab of fixed width around the centre contains almost all of the ball.

    Project a high-dimensional ball onto any line and nearly everything lands near 0 — even though the ball has radius 1.

    The marginal density of \(x_1\) for a uniform point in \(B^{n}\) is proportional to \((1-s^{2})^{(n-1)/2}\), which concentrates at 0 with standard deviation of order \(1/\sqrt n\) by the same estimate as above. So \(\Pr(|x_1| > t)\) decays like \(e^{-nt^{2}/2}\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Nearest-neighbour collapse: in a uniform sample from a high-dimensional cube, the ratio of farthest to nearest distance tends to 1.

    "Nearest neighbour" stops meaning anything. This is the practical face of the curse of dimensionality.

    Sketch: \(\|x-y\|^{2} = \sum_i(x_i-y_i)^{2}\) is a sum of \(n\) independent bounded terms, so by the law of large numbers it concentrates around \(n\mathbb{E}[(x_1-y_1)^{2}]\) with fluctuations of order \(\sqrt n\). Hence all pairwise distances are \(\sqrt n\bigl(c + O(n^{-1/2})\bigr)\), and their ratio tends to 1. \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Compute the ratio, not the quantity — volumes alone say little; fractions say everything.

    \(V_n\to0\) sounds dramatic until you notice \(2^{n}\to\infty\); the ratio is the real story.

  • Look at one coordinate — rotational symmetry makes the first coordinate representative.

    Turns an \(n\)-dimensional question into a one-dimensional density.

  • Use \((1-\varepsilon)^{n}\) — the standard high-dimensional decay factor.

    Anything short of the full radius is negligible once \(n\) is large.

  • Test your intuition against \(n=100\), not \(n=3\).

    Three-dimensional pictures are actively misleading here.

Layer 4 · The general principle

Concentration of Measure

In high dimensions, well-behaved functions are almost constant.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Concentration of measure — a Lipschitz function on a high-dimensional sphere is close to its median with overwhelming probability.

    Measure any reasonable quantity on a random high-dimensional point and you nearly always get the same answer.

  • Median \(M_f\) — a value with \(\sigma(f \le M_f)\ge\tfrac12\) and \(\sigma(f\ge M_f)\ge\tfrac12\).

    Used instead of the mean because it behaves better under the isoperimetric argument.

  • \(\varepsilon\)-neighbourhood \(A_\varepsilon = \{x : d(x,A)\le\varepsilon\}\).

    Fatten the set slightly. On a high-dimensional sphere, fattening a half is enough to capture almost everything.

  • Spherical isoperimetric inequality — among sets of given measure, caps have the smallest neighbourhoods.

    Caps are extremal, exactly as balls are in Euclidean space (Chapter 19).

  • Johnson–Lindenstrauss projection — a random linear map to \(O(\varepsilon^{-2}\log m)\) dimensions preserving all pairwise distances.

    You can squash a million points from a million dimensions into a few hundred and barely disturb their distances.

Theorems

  • Lévy's concentration inequality: for 1-Lipschitz \(f\) on \(S^{n-1}\), \(\sigma\bigl(|f - M_f| > t\bigr) \le 2e^{-nt^{2}/2}\).

    Any measurement that doesn't amplify small changes is essentially constant on a high-dimensional sphere.

    Sketch: let \(A = \{f\le M_f\}\), so \(\sigma(A)\ge\tfrac12\). The spherical isoperimetric inequality says its \(t\)-neighbourhood is at least as large as that of a half-sphere, which by the equatorial estimate of Layer 3 has measure at least \(1 - e^{-nt^{2}/2}\). Since \(f\) is 1-Lipschitz, \(f \le M_f + t\) on \(A_t\), giving \(\sigma(f > M_f+t)\le e^{-nt^{2}/2}\); applying the same to \(-f\) gives the other tail. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Corollary — sharp equatorial concentration: the coordinate function \(x\mapsto x_1\) is 1-Lipschitz, recovering Layer 3's estimate.

    The specific fact is a special case of the general principle.

    \(|x_1 - y_1| \le \|x-y\|\), so \(f(x)=x_1\) is 1-Lipschitz, and its median is 0 by symmetry. Lévy's inequality gives \(\sigma(|x_1|>t)\le2e^{-nt^{2}/2}\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Gaussian concentration: for 1-Lipschitz \(f\) and a standard Gaussian vector \(X\) in \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\), \(\Pr(|f(X)-\mathbb{E}f|>t)\le2e^{-t^{2}/2}\) — with no \(n\) in the bound.

    Dimension-free. This is why Gaussian models are so well behaved in high dimensions.

    Sketch: the Gaussian measure satisfies a logarithmic Sobolev inequality, from which the Herbst argument bounds the moment generating function \(\mathbb{E}e^{s(f-\mathbb{E}f)}\le e^{s^{2}/2}\). Markov's inequality applied to that exponential, optimised over \(s\), yields the stated tail. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Norm concentration: a standard Gaussian vector in \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\) has \(\|X\|\approx\sqrt n\), with fluctuations of order 1.

    High-dimensional Gaussian noise lives on a thin spherical shell of radius \(\sqrt n\), not near the origin where the density is highest.

    \(\mathbb{E}\|X\|^{2} = n\), and \(x\mapsto\|x\|\) is 1-Lipschitz, so Gaussian concentration gives \(\Pr(|\|X\| - \mathbb{E}\|X\||>t)\le2e^{-t^{2}/2}\) with \(\mathbb{E}\|X\| = \sqrt n(1+O(1/n))\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma: \(m\) points in \(\mathbb{R}^{N}\) can be mapped linearly into \(\mathbb{R}^{k}\), \(k = O(\varepsilon^{-2}\log m)\), distorting all pairwise distances by at most \(1\pm\varepsilon\).

    The target dimension doesn't depend on the original one at all — only on how many points there are.

    Sketch: take \(P\) a random \(k\times N\) Gaussian matrix scaled by \(1/\sqrt k\). For a fixed vector \(v\), \(\|Pv\|^{2}\) concentrates around \(\|v\|^{2}\) with failure probability \(\le2e^{-ck\varepsilon^{2}}\), by norm concentration. Applying this to the \(\binom m2\) difference vectors and taking a union bound, the failure probability is under 1 as soon as \(k > C\varepsilon^{-2}\log m\), so some \(P\) works. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Dvoretzky's theorem (stated): every high-dimensional convex body has nearly spherical sections of dimension \(c\log n\).

    Slice any convex body the right way and it looks like a ball. Concentration, at its most striking.

    Sketch: the norm \(x\mapsto\|x\|_K\) is Lipschitz on the Euclidean sphere, so by Lévy it is nearly constant on most of \(S^{n-1}\). A net argument shows a random subspace of dimension \(c\varepsilon^{2}\log n\) has the whole of its unit sphere in that near-constant region, making the section nearly Euclidean. \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Check the Lipschitz constant — then quote concentration.

    Almost every natural quantity (norms, distances, coordinates) is 1-Lipschitz.

  • Union bound over a net — to upgrade a pointwise estimate to a uniform one.

    Exponential tails beat polynomially many events, which is what makes the argument work.

  • Project randomly — reduce dimension while preserving geometry.

    The practical use of everything in this chapter.

  • Expect near-constancy — in high dimensions, ask for the typical value, not the range.

    The range is huge; the typical value is sharp.
