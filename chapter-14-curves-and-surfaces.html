Measuring the Skin

Chapter 11 measured \(n\)-dimensional sets in \(n\)-dimensional space. But a surface in \(\mathbb{R}^{3}\) has volume zero — and yet obviously has an area.

The fix is to measure a \(k\)-dimensional object with a \(k\)-dimensional ruler, carried in from a flat parameter domain. The exchange rate is the Gram determinant, straight from Chapter 5.

Along the way: arc length, curvature, the first and second fundamental forms, and Gauss's Theorema Egregium.

Layer 1 · One dimension, curved

Curves & Arc Length

Length as the limit of straight-line approximations.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Parametrised curve \(\gamma : [a,b]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}\), continuous.

    A walk described by where you are at each moment.

  • Regular curve — \(\gamma\) is \(C^{1}\) with \(\gamma'(t)\ne\mathbf 0\).

    Never stop. Stopping lets the trace form a corner even though the formula is smooth.

  • Trace — the image \(\gamma([a,b])\), as opposed to the parametrisation.

    The path on the ground, not the schedule of the walk. Different walks, same path.

  • Arc length \(L(\gamma) = \int_a^b\|\gamma'(t)\|\,dt\).

    Speed integrated over time is distance travelled. Exactly as in Chapter 3, now with a vector speed.

  • Rectifiable curve — finite supremum of inscribed polygon lengths.

    Some continuous curves have infinite length. Chapter 18 measures those instead.

  • Arc-length parametrisation — \(\|\gamma'(s)\|\equiv1\).

    Walk at unit speed. Then time and distance are the same number, and every formula simplifies.

  • Curvature \(\kappa = \|T'(s)\|\), with \(T = \gamma'\) the unit tangent.

    How fast you turn the steering wheel. A straight line has \(\kappa=0\); a circle of radius \(r\) has \(\kappa = 1/r\).

  • Frenet frame \((T,N,B)\) — tangent, normal, binormal.

    Forward, sideways, up — carried along by the curve itself.

  • Torsion \(\tau\) — the rate at which the curve leaves its own plane.

    A helix twists out of every plane; a circle never does.

  • Line integral \(\int_\gamma f\,ds = \int_a^bf(\gamma(t))\|\gamma'(t)\|dt\).

    Total mass of a wire from its density. The \(\|\gamma'\|\) is the exchange rate between parameter and length.

Theorems

  • Arc length is the supremum of inscribed polygon lengths.

    The definition everyone starts with, and the integral formula agrees with it.

    For a partition \(a=t_0<\cdots<t_N=b\), the mean value inequality (Chapter 10, Layer 2) bounds \(\|\gamma(t_i)-\gamma(t_{i-1})\|\) above by \(\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_i}\|\gamma'\|\), so every inscribed polygon is at most the integral. Conversely, uniform continuity of \(\gamma'\) makes \(\|\gamma(t_i)-\gamma(t_{i-1})\| \ge (\|\gamma'(t_i)\|-\varepsilon)\Delta t_i\) for fine partitions, so the supremum is at least the integral minus \(\varepsilon(b-a)\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Arc length is invariant under reparametrisation.

    Walk faster and the path is no shorter. Length belongs to the trace, not the schedule.

    Let \(\varphi\) be a \(C^{1}\) bijection with \(\varphi' > 0\) and \(\tilde\gamma = \gamma\circ\varphi\). The chain rule gives \(\|\tilde\gamma'(u)\| = \|\gamma'(\varphi(u))\|\varphi'(u)\), and the substitution rule (Chapter 12, Layer 4) turns \(\int\|\tilde\gamma'\|du\) into \(\int\|\gamma'\|dt\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Every regular curve can be parametrised by arc length.

    You may always assume unit speed — and doing so removes most of the algebra.

    Let \(s(t) = \int_a^t\|\gamma'\|\), which is \(C^{1}\) with \(s'(t) = \|\gamma'(t)\| > 0\), hence strictly increasing with a \(C^{1}\) inverse (Chapter 10's inverse function theorem). Setting \(\tilde\gamma = \gamma\circ s^{-1}\) gives \(\|\tilde\gamma'\| = \|\gamma'\|/s' = 1\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Straight lines are shortest.

    The triangle inequality, integrated. Chapter 17 asks the same question on curved spaces and gets geodesics.

    For any curve from \(p\) to \(q\), and \(u\) the unit vector along \(q-p\): \(\|q-p\| = \langle q-p,u\rangle = \int_a^b\langle\gamma'(t),u\rangle dt \le \int_a^b\|\gamma'(t)\|dt = L(\gamma)\), by Cauchy–Schwarz. Equality forces \(\gamma'\) parallel to \(u\) throughout, i.e. a straight path. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Frenet–Serret formulas: \(T' = \kappa N\), \(N' = -\kappa T + \tau B\), \(B' = -\tau N\) (unit speed).

    Three equations describing how the frame tumbles along the curve — the complete local description.

    Differentiating \(\langle T,T\rangle = 1\) gives \(T'\perp T\), so \(T' = \kappa N\) defines \(\kappa\ge0\) and the unit normal \(N\). With \(B = T\times N\), differentiating the three orthonormality relations shows the derivative matrix of \((T,N,B)\) is antisymmetric; the entries not yet named are \(\langle N',B\rangle =: \tau\) and its negative, which gives the stated system. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Fundamental theorem of curves: \(\kappa > 0\) and \(\tau\) determine a space curve up to rigid motion.

    Two numbers at each point fix the shape completely. Where it sits and which way it faces is the only freedom left.

    The Frenet system is a linear ODE for the frame with continuous coefficients, so by Picard–Lindelöf (Chapter 7, Layer 4) it has a unique solution given an initial orthonormal frame; integrating \(T\) recovers \(\gamma\) up to a translation. Different initial frames differ by a rotation, so the curves differ by a rigid motion. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Curvature of a plane curve: \(\kappa = \dfrac{|x'y''-y'x''|}{\bigl((x')^{2}+(y')^{2}\bigr)^{3/2}}\).

    The formula you actually compute with, since the curve is rarely given at unit speed.

    Reparametrise by arc length: \(\frac{d}{dt} = \|\gamma'\|\frac{d}{ds}\). Expressing \(T' \) in terms of \(t\)-derivatives and taking the norm gives \(\kappa = \|\gamma'\times\gamma''\|/\|\gamma'\|^{3}\), whose planar case is the stated determinant expression. \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Parametrise, then integrate \(\|\gamma'\|\).

    Almost all of the work is choosing a parametrisation whose speed is easy to integrate.

  • Switch to arc length before doing theory.

    Unit speed kills every \(\|\gamma'\|\) factor in sight.

  • Use the cross-product formula for curvature when unit speed is inconvenient.

    \(\kappa = \|\gamma'\times\gamma''\|/\|\gamma'\|^{3}\) works with any parametrisation.

Layer 2 · Two dimensions, curved

Surfaces & Area

The first fundamental form, and what it measures.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Parametrised surface \(\Phi : U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}\).

    A flat map sheet bent into space. Latitude and longitude are exactly this for the globe.

  • Regular surface — \(\Phi_u\times\Phi_v \ne \mathbf 0\).

    The two parameter directions must span a genuine plane, or the "surface" collapses.

  • Tangent plane \(T_pS = \operatorname{span}(\Phi_u,\Phi_v)\).

    The flat sheet touching the surface at \(p\). Chapter 15 makes this the definition of a tangent space.

  • Unit normal \(N = \dfrac{\Phi_u\times\Phi_v}{\|\Phi_u\times\Phi_v\|}\).

    Straight up out of the surface. Choosing it consistently is what "orientable" means.

  • First fundamental form \(I = \begin{pmatrix}E&F\\F&G\end{pmatrix}\), with \(E = \langle\Phi_u,\Phi_u\rangle\), \(F = \langle\Phi_u,\Phi_v\rangle\), \(G = \langle\Phi_v,\Phi_v\rangle\).

    The dictionary that converts parameter distances into real distances. This is the Gram matrix of Chapter 5.

  • Surface area \(A = \displaystyle\iint_U\|\Phi_u\times\Phi_v\|\,du\,dv = \iint_U\sqrt{EG-F^{2}}\,du\,dv\).

    The area of the little parallelogram spanned by the two parameter directions, added up.

  • Surface integral \(\iint_Sf\,dA\).

    Total mass of a curved sheet from its density.

  • Intrinsic geometry — everything computable from \(I\) alone.

    What an ant confined to the surface could measure, with no view of the surrounding space.

  • Isometry of surfaces — a map preserving the first fundamental form.

    Rolling a flat sheet into a cylinder is an isometry. Flattening a sphere is not — hence every world map lies.

Theorems

  • \(\|\Phi_u\times\Phi_v\|^{2} = EG - F^{2}\), and it is positive for a regular surface.

    Two ways of writing the same area element; the second generalises to any dimension.

    Lagrange's identity: \(\|a\times b\|^{2} = \|a\|^{2}\|b\|^{2}-\langle a,b\rangle^{2}\), which is exactly \(EG-F^{2}\) — the determinant of the Gram matrix (Chapter 5, Layer 4). Positivity is the independence of \(\Phi_u,\Phi_v\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Surface area is independent of the parametrisation.

    Area belongs to the surface, not to the map projection used to describe it.

    If \(\tilde\Phi = \Phi\circ\psi\) for a diffeomorphism \(\psi\) of parameter domains, the chain rule gives \(\tilde\Phi_u\times\tilde\Phi_v = (\det D\psi)\,(\Phi_u\times\Phi_v)\circ\psi\). Taking norms and applying the change of variables formula (Chapter 12, Layer 4), the factor \(|\det D\psi|\) cancels exactly. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Area of a graph: for \(z = f(x,y)\), \(A = \iint\sqrt{1+f_x^{2}+f_y^{2}}\,dx\,dy\).

    The steeper the sheet, the more area it hides above a given footprint.

    Parametrise by \(\Phi(x,y) = (x,y,f(x,y))\). Then \(\Phi_x = (1,0,f_x)\), \(\Phi_y = (0,1,f_y)\), so \(E = 1+f_x^{2}\), \(G = 1+f_y^{2}\), \(F = f_xf_y\), and \(EG-F^{2} = 1+f_x^{2}+f_y^{2}\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Area of a surface of revolution: \(A = 2\pi\displaystyle\int_a^b r(t)\sqrt{r'(t)^{2}+z'(t)^{2}}\,dt\).

    Pappus's rule: length of the generating curve times the distance travelled by its centroid.

    Parametrise by \(\Phi(t,\theta) = (r(t)\cos\theta, r(t)\sin\theta, z(t))\). Then \(E = r'^{2}+z'^{2}\), \(F=0\), \(G = r^{2}\), so \(\sqrt{EG-F^{2}} = r\sqrt{r'^{2}+z'^{2}}\). Integrating \(\theta\) over \([0,2\pi]\) gives the factor \(2\pi\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Area of the sphere of radius \(R\) is \(4\pi R^{2}\).

    Chapter 13 got this from \(dV/dr\); here it comes straight from the definition of area.

    With \(\Phi(\varphi,\theta) = R(\sin\varphi\cos\theta,\sin\varphi\sin\theta,\cos\varphi)\), we get \(E = R^{2}\), \(F = 0\), \(G = R^{2}\sin^{2}\varphi\), so \(\sqrt{EG-F^{2}} = R^{2}\sin\varphi\). Then \(A = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi}R^{2}\sin\varphi\,d\varphi\,d\theta = 4\pi R^{2}\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Archimedes' hat-box theorem: the sphere's area between two parallel planes equals that of the enclosing cylinder's band.

    Every horizontal slice of the globe has area proportional to its thickness alone — latitude doesn't matter.

    From the computation above, the area between heights \(z = R\cos\varphi\) is \(\int 2\pi R^{2}\sin\varphi\,d\varphi = -2\pi R\int dz\), i.e. \(2\pi R\,\Delta z\), which is exactly the lateral area of the cylinder band of the same height. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Lengths and angles on the surface are determined by \(I\).

    An ant with a tape measure can compute everything intrinsic without ever leaving the surface.

    A curve on the surface is \(\Phi(u(t),v(t))\), and the chain rule gives \(\|\gamma'\|^{2} = Eu'^{2}+2Fu'v'+Gv'^{2}\) — a formula involving only \(I\). Angles come from the same expression via polarisation, and area from \(\sqrt{\det I}\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Schwarz's lantern: inscribed triangulations of a cylinder can have area tending to \(\infty\).

    The naive analogue of "inscribed polygons" fails for area. This is why surface area needs the derivative, not just the points.

    Divide a unit cylinder into \(m\) rings and \(k\) sectors, and inscribe the standard zig-zag triangulation. The triangles' total area is \(\approx 2\pi\sqrt{1+\frac{\pi^{4}m^{2}}{4k^{4}}}\). Letting \(m\) grow much faster than \(k^{2}\) sends this to infinity even though the vertices converge to the cylinder. \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Compute \(E, F, G\), then integrate \(\sqrt{EG-F^{2}}\).

    The whole of surface-area calculation, in one line.

  • Choose a parametrisation with \(F = 0\) — orthogonal coordinates.

    Then \(\sqrt{EG-F^{2}} = \sqrt{EG}\), and everything simplifies.

  • Use symmetry — revolution, spheres, graphs each have a standard form.

    Recognising the type saves recomputing the fundamental form.

  • Never approximate area by inscribed polygons.

    Schwarz's lantern is the standing warning.

Layer 3 · How it bends

Curvature of Surfaces

The second fundamental form, and Gauss's remarkable theorem.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Second fundamental form \(II = \begin{pmatrix}L&M\\M&N\end{pmatrix}\), with \(L = \langle\Phi_{uu},N\rangle\) and so on.

    How fast the surface pulls away from its own tangent plane.

  • Shape (Weingarten) operator \(S = I^{-1}II\).

    The derivative of the unit normal. Its eigenvalues are the principal curvatures.

  • Normal curvature — the curvature of the curve cut by a normal plane.

    Slice the surface vertically and measure how the cross-section bends.

  • Principal curvatures \(\kappa_1,\kappa_2\) — the extreme normal curvatures.

    On a cylinder: \(1/r\) around, and 0 along. Two very different directions.

  • Gaussian curvature \(K = \kappa_1\kappa_2 = \dfrac{LN-M^{2}}{EG-F^{2}}\).

    Sphere: \(K = 1/R^{2} > 0\). Plane and cylinder: \(K=0\). Saddle: \(K < 0\).

  • Mean curvature \(H = \tfrac12(\kappa_1+\kappa_2)\).

    Soap films minimise area, and they are exactly the surfaces with \(H = 0\).

  • Elliptic, parabolic, hyperbolic points — \(K>0\), \(K=0\), \(K<0\).

    Dome, cylinder, saddle. The three local shapes a surface can have.

  • Geodesic — a curve with zero tangential acceleration.

    Walk without steering. On a sphere these are the great circles — the flight paths airlines use.

  • Minimal surface — \(H\equiv0\).

    The catenoid and the helicoid. Dip a wire loop in soapy water and you get one.

Theorems

  • Euler's formula: the normal curvature in the direction at angle \(\theta\) to the first principal direction is \(\kappa_1\cos^{2}\theta+\kappa_2\sin^{2}\theta\).

    All the bending in every direction is determined by two numbers.

    The normal curvature in a unit tangent direction \(w\) equals \(II(w,w) = \langle Sw,w\rangle\). The shape operator is self-adjoint with respect to \(I\), so by the spectral theorem (Chapter 6, Layer 3) it has an orthonormal eigenbasis with eigenvalues \(\kappa_1,\kappa_2\); expanding \(w = \cos\theta\,e_1+\sin\theta\,e_2\) gives the formula. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Principal directions are orthogonal, and \(\kappa_1,\kappa_2\) are the extreme normal curvatures.

    The steepest and flattest slices meet at right angles — always.

    Self-adjointness of \(S\) gives orthogonal eigenvectors (Chapter 6, Layer 3), and the Rayleigh quotient identifies the eigenvalues as the extreme values of \(\langle Sw,w\rangle\) over unit \(w\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • \(K = \det S\) and \(H = \tfrac12\operatorname{tr}S\).

    The determinant and trace of Chapter 5, doing geometry.

    The eigenvalues of \(S\) are \(\kappa_1,\kappa_2\); the determinant is their product and half the trace their average. In coordinates, \(\det S = \det(I^{-1}II) = \frac{LN-M^{2}}{EG-F^{2}}\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Theorema Egregium: \(K\) depends only on the first fundamental form.

    Gauss's "remarkable theorem". The ant on the surface can measure \(K\) without ever leaving it — and that is why no flat map of the Earth can be accurate.

    Sketch: express the second derivatives \(\Phi_{uu},\Phi_{uv},\Phi_{vv}\) in the frame \((\Phi_u,\Phi_v,N)\); the tangential coefficients are the Christoffel symbols, computable from \(E,F,G\) and their derivatives alone. Equating the mixed third derivatives \(\Phi_{uuv} = \Phi_{uvu}\) (Clairaut, Chapter 10) yields the Gauss equation, whose normal component reads \(LN - M^{2} = (EG-F^{2})\times(\text{an expression in }E,F,G\text{ and derivatives})\). Dividing gives \(K\) intrinsically. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Corollary — no isometric flat map of the sphere.

    Every world map distorts distance, area or angle. The sphere has \(K = 1/R^{2}\); the plane has \(K = 0\); an isometry would have to match them.

    An isometry preserves the first fundamental form, hence — by the Theorema Egregium — preserves \(K\). Since \(K\) is \(1/R^{2}\) on the sphere and 0 on the plane, no such map exists, even locally. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Corollary — the cylinder is intrinsically flat.

    Roll a sheet of paper into a tube and nothing measured on the paper changes. It bends in space but not intrinsically.

    For the cylinder, \(\kappa_1 = 1/r\) and \(\kappa_2 = 0\), so \(K = 0\) — the same as the plane. The explicit isometry \((u,v)\mapsto(r\cos(u/r), r\sin(u/r), v)\) preserves \(E=G=1\), \(F=0\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Minimal surfaces are area-critical: \(H \equiv 0\) iff the surface is a critical point of area under compactly supported normal variations.

    Soap films solve a calculus of variations problem — physically, by minimising energy.

    Vary the surface by \(\Phi_t = \Phi + t\varphi N\) with \(\varphi\) compactly supported. Differentiating the area integral \(\iint\sqrt{\det I_t}\) at \(t=0\) gives the first variation \(-2\iint\varphi H\,dA\). This vanishes for every \(\varphi\) exactly when \(H\equiv0\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Gauss–Bonnet theorem: \(\displaystyle\iint_S K\,dA + \oint_{\partial S}\kappa_g\,ds = 2\pi\chi(S)\).

    Total curvature is a topological invariant. Deform a sphere however you like — the integral is always \(4\pi\).

    Sketch: for a geodesic triangle, the angle excess equals \(\iint K\,dA\) (proved by comparing the frame's rotation with the boundary's turning). Triangulate \(S\), sum the excesses over all triangles, and collect terms: the interior angles at each vertex sum to \(2\pi\), and the count of faces, edges and vertices assembles into \(2\pi(V-E+F) = 2\pi\chi(S)\). \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Compute \(I\) and \(II\), then \(S = I^{-1}II\).

    Eigenvalues give the principal curvatures; determinant and trace give \(K\) and \(H\).

  • Use \(K\) as an isometry invariant.

    Two surfaces with different Gaussian curvature can never be isometric.

  • Recognise the sign of \(K\) from the local shape.

    Dome, tube, saddle — positive, zero, negative.

  • Vary and differentiate — the calculus-of-variations route to minimal surfaces and geodesics.

    Set the first variation to zero and read off the equation.

Layer 4 · Any dimension, any codimension

\(k\)-Dimensional Area in \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\)

The general formula, and why \(\sqrt{\det(A^{\mathsf T}A)}\) is the only possible answer.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Parametrised \(k\)-patch \(\Phi : U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}\), with \(D\Phi\) of rank \(k\).

    A curved \(k\)-dimensional sheet in \(n\)-dimensional space. \(k=1\) is a curve, \(k=2\) a surface.

  • Gram matrix \(G = D\Phi^{\mathsf T}D\Phi\), with entries \(\langle\partial_i\Phi,\partial_j\Phi\rangle\).

    The first fundamental form, in general. It is \(k\times k\) whatever \(n\) is.

  • \(k\)-dimensional area \(\displaystyle\int_U\sqrt{\det G}\,du\).

    The volume of the little \(k\)-box spanned by the parameter directions, added up.

  • Jacobian of a non-square map \(J\Phi = \sqrt{\det(D\Phi^{\mathsf T}D\Phi)}\).

    The right replacement for \(|\det|\) when the matrix isn't square.

  • Atlas of patches — covering a surface by overlapping parametrisations.

    No single flat map covers a sphere, so you use several. Chapter 15 makes this the definition.

  • Coarea factor \(\sqrt{\det(DF\,DF^{\mathsf T})}\) for a map \(F : \mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}\), \(m\le n\).

    The other non-square Jacobian: it appears when you slice a region by the level sets of \(F\).

Theorems

  • \(\sqrt{\det G}\) is the \(k\)-volume of the parallelepiped spanned by \(\partial_1\Phi,\dots,\partial_k\Phi\).

    The same Gram determinant Chapter 5 derived. Nothing new is being assumed.

    Chapter 5, Layer 4: for a matrix \(A\) with independent columns, the \(k\)-volume of the box on its columns is \(\sqrt{\det(A^{\mathsf T}A)}\), proved via the \(QR\) factorisation. Apply this to \(A = D\Phi\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Consistency: for \(k = n\) the formula reduces to \(|\det D\Phi|\), and for \(k=1\) to \(\|\gamma'\|\).

    Chapters 12 and this chapter's Layer 1 are both special cases.

    For \(k = n\), \(\det(D\Phi^{\mathsf T}D\Phi) = (\det D\Phi)^{2}\) by multiplicativity, so the square root is \(|\det D\Phi|\). For \(k=1\), \(G\) is the \(1\times1\) matrix \(\|\gamma'\|^{2}\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Reparametrisation invariance: the \(k\)-area does not depend on the chosen parametrisation.

    Area is a property of the set, not of the coordinates used to describe it.

    If \(\tilde\Phi = \Phi\circ\psi\), then \(D\tilde\Phi = (D\Phi\circ\psi)D\psi\), so \(\tilde G = D\psi^{\mathsf T}(G\circ\psi)D\psi\) and \(\sqrt{\det\tilde G} = |\det D\psi|\sqrt{\det G\circ\psi}\). The change of variables formula (Chapter 12, Layer 4) absorbs the factor \(|\det D\psi|\) exactly. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Well defined on overlaps: areas computed in different patches agree, so the total area of a surface is unambiguous.

    Chart the globe with two maps and the total surface area comes out the same.

    On an overlap the two parametrisations differ by a diffeomorphism of parameter domains (their composition is smooth by the inverse function theorem), so the previous theorem applies. Summing over a partition of unity subordinate to the atlas (Chapter 15) gives a well-defined total. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Graphs: the \(k\)-area of the graph of \(f : U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{n-k}\) is \(\int_U\sqrt{\det(I + Df^{\mathsf T}Df)}\).

    Generalises \(\sqrt{1+f_x^{2}+f_y^{2}}\) to any dimension and codimension.

    Parametrise by \(\Phi(x) = (x,f(x))\), so \(D\Phi = \binom{I}{Df}\) and \(G = D\Phi^{\mathsf T}D\Phi = I + Df^{\mathsf T}Df\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Area of a linear image: for a linear \(A\) of rank \(k\) and \(E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}\), the \(k\)-area of \(A(E)\) is \(\sqrt{\det(A^{\mathsf T}A)}\,\lambda_k(E)\).

    Chapter 11's \(|\det T|\) law, in the non-square case.

    \(A\) maps \(\mathbb{R}^{k}\) isometrically onto its image after composing with a suitable orthogonal map, and the \(QR\) factorisation identifies the stretching factor as \(\sqrt{\det(A^{\mathsf T}A)}\). Chapter 11's scaling law then transfers to the image. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Surface measure of the sphere: \(\sigma(S^{n-1}) = nV_n = \frac{2\pi^{n/2}}{\Gamma(n/2)}\), recovered from the general formula.

    Chapter 13's answer, now derived from a definition of area rather than by differentiating volume.

    Parametrise the upper hemisphere as the graph of \(f(x) = \sqrt{1-\|x\|^{2}}\) over the unit ball of \(\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\). Then \(\sqrt{\det(I+\nabla f\nabla f^{\mathsf T})} = \sqrt{1+\|\nabla f\|^{2}} = (1-\|x\|^{2})^{-1/2}\), and integrating in polar coordinates gives \(\tfrac12 nV_n\); doubling covers both hemispheres. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Coarea formula (stated): \(\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}} g\,\sqrt{\det(DF\,DF^{\mathsf T})}\;dx = \int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\!\!\left(\int_{F^{-1}(y)}g\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-m}\right)dy\).

    Fubini for curved slices: integrate over the level sets, then over the levels. Chapter 18 supplies the measure \(\mathcal H\).

    Sketch: for a linear \(F\) the identity is the linear change of variables plus Fubini. In general, the rank theorem (Chapter 10, Layer 4) makes \(F\) locally a projection in suitable coordinates, and the Jacobian factor \(\sqrt{\det(DF\,DF^{\mathsf T})}\) records the discrepancy. Patching with a partition of unity and passing to the limit over finer decompositions gives the general case. Taking \(F(x)=\|x\|\) recovers spherical coordinates. \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Build the Gram matrix, take \(\sqrt{\det}\).

    One recipe for length, area and every higher-dimensional volume.

  • Parametrise as a graph when possible.

    The Gram determinant becomes \(\det(I+Df^{\mathsf T}Df)\), which is often easy.

  • Split into patches — no single chart need cover everything.

    Overlaps are harmless: reparametrisation invariance guarantees agreement.

  • Slice with the coarea formula — integrate over level sets.

    The curved generalisation of Fubini, and the tool behind spherical coordinates.
