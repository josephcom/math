Spaces That Are Flat Up Close

A sphere is not \(\mathbb{R}^{2}\), but every small patch of it is. That is the entire idea of a manifold: a space that looks like \(\mathbb{R}^{k}\) locally, though not globally.

Chapter 14 measured curved objects sitting inside \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\). This chapter frees them from the ambient space — the surface exists in its own right, and the charts are the only access to it.

The payoff is Chapter 16: on a manifold you can differentiate and integrate, and Stokes' theorem holds.

Layer 1 · Local coordinates

Charts, Atlases & Smooth Structure

Describing a curved space by overlapping flat maps.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Topological manifold — a Hausdorff, second countable space in which every point has a neighbourhood homeomorphic to \(\mathbb{R}^{k}\).

    The Earth's surface: locally a flat map, globally not a plane.

  • Chart \((U,\varphi)\) — an open set and a homeomorphism \(\varphi : U\to\varphi(U)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}\).

    One page of the atlas: coordinates for one region.

  • Atlas — a collection of charts covering the manifold.

    The whole book of maps. No single page can cover a sphere.

  • Transition map \(\varphi_\beta\circ\varphi_\alpha^{-1}\).

    How two overlapping pages disagree about coordinates. Requiring these to be smooth is the whole definition of a smooth manifold.

  • Smooth structure — a maximal atlas whose transition maps are all \(C^{\infty}\).

    Agree on what "smooth" means, consistently across every overlap.

  • Dimension \(k\) — the \(k\) in \(\mathbb{R}^{k}\), constant on each connected component.

    Well defined by Chapter 8's invariance of dimension. A surface is 2-dimensional however you chart it.

  • Embedded submanifold of \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\) — locally the graph of a smooth map, or a regular level set.

    The sphere, the torus, the graph of any smooth function. Chapter 10's regular value theorem produces these wholesale.

  • Manifold with boundary — charts onto the half-space \(\mathbb{H}^{k} = \{x_k\ge0\}\).

    A disc has a circle for a boundary; a sphere has none. Stokes' theorem lives on this distinction.

  • Product manifold \(M\times N\).

    The torus is \(S^{1}\times S^{1}\) — a circle of circles.

  • Quotient manifold — glue points together and check the result is still locally flat.

    Glue opposite edges of a square: a torus. Glue with a flip: a Klein bottle, which cannot fit in \(\mathbb{R}^{3}\).

Theorems

  • Dimension is well defined: a non-empty manifold cannot have charts to \(\mathbb{R}^{k}\) and \(\mathbb{R}^{m}\) with \(k\ne m\).

    You cannot chart a surface with three coordinates and a solid with two.

    Overlapping charts would produce a homeomorphism between an open subset of \(\mathbb{R}^{k}\) and one of \(\mathbb{R}^{m}\), contradicting Brouwer's invariance of domain (Chapter 8, Layer 2). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Regular value theorem: if \(c\) is a regular value of a smooth \(F : \mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}\), then \(F^{-1}(c)\) is a smooth \((n-m)\)-manifold.

    The sphere, the torus, \(SL_n\), \(O(n)\) — all manufactured by this single theorem.

    Chapter 10, Layer 4: the implicit function theorem writes the level set locally as a graph over \(n-m\) of the variables. Those graph parametrisations are charts, and their transitions are smooth because they are compositions of smooth graph maps and projections. \(\blacksquare\)

  • \(S^{n-1}\) is a smooth \((n-1)\)-manifold, chartable by two stereographic projections.

    Two flat maps suffice for any sphere — one from each pole.

    \(1\) is a regular value of \(F(x) = \|x\|^{2}\), since \(\nabla F = 2x \ne \mathbf 0\) on the sphere. For the explicit atlas, stereographic projection from the north pole is a homeomorphism of \(S^{n-1}\setminus\{N\}\) onto \(\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\), and similarly from the south pole; the transition map is \(y\mapsto y/\|y\|^{2}\), which is smooth away from the origin. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Equivalent descriptions of an embedded submanifold: local graph, local level set, local parametrisation, and locally straightenable.

    Four ways to say the same thing. Use whichever is easiest to verify in the case at hand.

    All four are the rank theorem (Chapter 10, Layer 4) read in different directions: a full-rank map of constant rank \(k\) can be straightened at both ends, and the straightened picture is simultaneously a graph, a level set \(\{x_{k+1}=\cdots=x_n=0\}\), and a parametrisation by the first \(k\) coordinates. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Manifolds are locally compact, locally connected and metrisable.

    All the good behaviour of \(\mathbb{R}^{k}\) survives locally — which is why analysis works on manifolds.

    Each point has a chart neighbourhood homeomorphic to \(\mathbb{R}^{k}\), which is locally compact and locally connected; these are local properties, so they transfer. Metrisability follows from Urysohn's theorem, since the space is Hausdorff, second countable and regular (the last from local compactness). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Charts are diffeomorphisms onto their images, once a smooth structure is fixed.

    Working in coordinates is legitimate: nothing smooth is lost or created by choosing a chart.

    By definition a smooth map \(M\to\mathbb{R}^{k}\) is one whose composition with chart inverses is smooth; for \(\varphi\) itself that composition is a transition map, which is smooth by hypothesis, and likewise for \(\varphi^{-1}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Check a regular value — the fastest way to prove something is a manifold.

    Verify \(DF\) has full rank at every point of the level set, not merely at one.

  • Work in a chart — pull everything back to \(\mathbb{R}^{k}\).

    Then apply Chapters 9–12 verbatim.

  • Check independence of the chart — every definition must survive a transition map.

    The standing obligation of this chapter. If a quantity changes with the chart, it wasn't geometry.

  • Build from products and quotients.

    Torus, cylinder, projective space — all assembled from simpler pieces.

Layer 2 · Calculus without an ambient space

Smooth Maps & Tangent Spaces

What "the derivative" means when there is no surrounding \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\).

Concepts & Definitions

  • Smooth map \(f : M \to N\) — smooth in every pair of charts.

    Check it in coordinates; the transition maps guarantee the answer doesn't depend on which coordinates.

  • Diffeomorphism — a smooth bijection with smooth inverse.

    The manifolds are the same object. A sphere and an ellipsoid are diffeomorphic; a sphere and a torus are not.

  • Tangent vector, three ways — a velocity of a curve; a derivation on functions; a coordinate tuple that transforms by the Jacobian.

    Three costumes, one actor. The derivation definition is the one that needs no ambient space.

  • Tangent space \(T_pM\) — the \(k\)-dimensional vector space of tangent vectors at \(p\).

    The tangent plane of Chapter 14, defined without ever leaving the surface.

  • Coordinate basis \(\partial/\partial x^{1},\dots,\partial/\partial x^{k}\).

    The directions "increase this coordinate". A chart gives a basis of every tangent space in its domain.

  • Differential (pushforward) \(df_p : T_pM\to T_{f(p)}N\).

    Chapter 10's total derivative, now as a map between tangent spaces. In coordinates it is still the Jacobian.

  • Immersion, submersion, embedding — \(df\) injective, surjective, and an immersion that is a homeomorphism onto its image.

    A figure-eight is an immersed circle but not embedded — the crossing point ruins it.

  • Cotangent space \(T_p^{*}M\) — the dual of \(T_pM\), with basis \(dx^{1},\dots,dx^{k}\).

    Chapter 4's dual space, attached at each point. Chapter 16's forms live here.

  • Tangent bundle \(TM = \bigsqcup_pT_pM\) — itself a \(2k\)-manifold.

    All positions together with all velocities: exactly the phase space of mechanics.

Theorems

  • The three definitions of a tangent vector agree.

    Velocity of a curve, derivation, coordinate tuple — one object, three descriptions.

    A curve \(\gamma\) with \(\gamma(0)=p\) acts on functions by \(f\mapsto(f\circ\gamma)'(0)\), which is a derivation. Conversely, in a chart every derivation is determined by its values on the coordinate functions — a Taylor expansion \(f = f(p)+\sum_i a_i(x^{i}-x^{i}(p))+\text{higher order}\) shows the higher-order terms are killed by the Leibniz rule — so derivations form a \(k\)-dimensional space with basis \(\partial/\partial x^{i}\), and each is realised by a straight coordinate curve. The transformation rule under a change of chart is the transition map's Jacobian, which is the third definition. \(\blacksquare\)

  • \(\dim T_pM = \dim M\).

    As many independent directions to move in as the manifold has dimensions.

    Immediate from the previous proof: the coordinate derivations \(\partial/\partial x^{i}\), \(i = 1,\dots,k\), form a basis. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Chain rule: \(d(g\circ f)_p = dg_{f(p)}\circ df_p\).

    Chapter 10's chain rule, unchanged. In coordinates it is still matrix multiplication.

    Apply both sides to a derivation \(v\) acting on a test function \(h\): \(d(g\circ f)(v)(h) = v(h\circ g\circ f) = df(v)(h\circ g) = dg(df(v))(h)\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Inverse function theorem on manifolds: \(df_p\) invertible implies \(f\) is a local diffeomorphism at \(p\).

    The whole of Chapter 10, Layer 4, transported by charts.

    Read \(f\) in charts around \(p\) and \(f(p)\), giving a smooth map between open subsets of \(\mathbb{R}^{k}\) whose derivative is invertible. Chapter 10's theorem produces a local inverse, and composing with the chart maps turns it into a local inverse of \(f\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Tangent space of a level set: for \(M = F^{-1}(c)\), \(T_pM = \ker dF_p\).

    The tangent plane to a sphere is exactly the vectors perpendicular to the radius — Chapter 10's "gradient ⟂ level set", restated.

    Any curve in \(M\) satisfies \(F(\gamma(t)) = c\); differentiating gives \(dF_p(\gamma'(0)) = 0\), so \(T_pM \subseteq \ker dF_p\). Both spaces have dimension \(n-m\) (rank–nullity, and the regular value theorem), so they are equal. \(\blacksquare\)

  • \(TM\) is a smooth \(2k\)-manifold, with charts \((x,\xi)\).

    Position and velocity together, and the whole thing is again a manifold you can do calculus on.

    Each chart \((U,\varphi)\) gives a bijection \(TU \to \varphi(U)\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\) sending a tangent vector to its base point and its coordinate components. The transitions are \((x,\xi)\mapsto(\psi(x), D\psi(x)\xi)\), smooth because \(\psi\) is smooth and \(D\psi\) depends smoothly on \(x\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Whitney embedding theorem (stated): every smooth \(k\)-manifold embeds in \(\mathbb{R}^{2k}\).

    Abstract manifolds are not more general than submanifolds of \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\) — but the chart-based definition is far more usable.

    Sketch: for compact \(M\), a finite atlas with a partition of unity gives an embedding into some large \(\mathbb{R}^{N}\) by assembling the chart maps. Then project along a generic direction: by Sard's theorem the directions that destroy injectivity or immersivity form a null set, so projections reduce \(N\) as long as \(N > 2k+1\). A more delicate argument sharpens \(2k+1\) to \(2k\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Sard's theorem (stated): the set of critical values of a smooth map has measure zero.

    Almost every value is a regular value — so almost every level set is a manifold.

    Sketch: cover the critical set by small cubes. On each cube, Taylor's theorem shows the image lies in a thin slab whose thickness is higher order in the cube's side, because the derivative is degenerate there. Summing the volumes of these slabs and letting the cubes shrink gives measure zero; the smoothness order needed grows with the dimension gap. \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Compute in a chart, check chart-independence.

    The standard discipline of the entire subject.

  • Use curves to produce tangent vectors.

    Differentiate a curve through the point: the fastest way to identify a tangent space.

  • Identify \(T_pM = \ker dF_p\) for level sets.

    Turns a geometric question into linear algebra.

  • Quote Sard — to get a regular value whenever you need one.

    "For almost every \(c\), the level set is a manifold."

Layer 3 · Motion on a manifold

Vector Fields & Flows

Assigning a direction at every point, and following it.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Vector field \(X\) — a smooth choice of \(X_p\in T_pM\) at each point.

    The wind at every point of the globe. A section of the tangent bundle.

  • Integral curve — a curve with \(\gamma'(t) = X_{\gamma(t)}\).

    The path of a balloon released into the wind.

  • Flow \(\Phi_t\) — the map sending each point along its integral curve for time \(t\).

    A one-parameter family of diffeomorphisms, with \(\Phi_{s+t} = \Phi_s\circ\Phi_t\).

  • Complete vector field — one whose flow is defined for all time.

    On a compact manifold every field is complete; on \(\mathbb{R}\), \(X = x^{2}\partial_x\) blows up in finite time.

  • Lie bracket \([X,Y] = XY - YX\).

    The failure of two flows to commute. Go east then north, or north then east — the difference is the bracket.

  • Frame — a set of vector fields forming a basis of each tangent space.

    A consistent choice of axes across the whole manifold. Most manifolds don't admit one.

  • Parallelisable manifold — one admitting a global frame.

    The torus is; the 2-sphere is not, by the hairy ball theorem.

  • Lie derivative \(\mathcal{L}_XY\) — the rate of change of \(Y\) along the flow of \(X\).

    Drift downstream and watch how the neighbouring current changes relative to you.

Theorems

  • Existence and uniqueness of integral curves.

    Release a balloon and its path is determined — a fact borrowed straight from Chapter 7.

    In a chart the condition \(\gamma' = X(\gamma)\) is an ODE with smooth — hence locally Lipschitz — right-hand side, so Picard–Lindelöf (Chapter 7, Layer 4) gives a unique local solution. Uniqueness lets the local solutions be glued along overlaps into a maximal integral curve. \(\blacksquare\)

  • The flow is a local one-parameter group: \(\Phi_0 = \mathrm{id}\) and \(\Phi_{s+t} = \Phi_s\circ\Phi_t\) where defined.

    Drifting for 3 hours then 2 is the same as drifting for 5.

    Both \(t\mapsto\Phi_{s+t}(p)\) and \(t\mapsto\Phi_t(\Phi_s(p))\) are integral curves of \(X\) starting at \(\Phi_s(p)\); uniqueness forces them to coincide. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Each \(\Phi_t\) is a diffeomorphism onto its image, with inverse \(\Phi_{-t}\).

    The wind can be run backwards.

    The group law gives \(\Phi_t\circ\Phi_{-t} = \Phi_0 = \mathrm{id}\), so \(\Phi_t\) is bijective with inverse \(\Phi_{-t}\); smooth dependence of ODE solutions on initial conditions makes both smooth. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Vector fields on a compact manifold are complete.

    Nothing can escape to infinity if there is no infinity to escape to.

    By compactness a uniform \(\varepsilon > 0\) exists such that every integral curve is defined on \((-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\) (take a finite subcover of chart neighbourhoods with local existence times). Repeatedly applying the group law extends any curve by \(\varepsilon\) at a time, indefinitely. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Straightening theorem: near a point where \(X\ne0\), some chart makes \(X = \partial/\partial x^{1}\).

    Any non-vanishing wind looks like a uniform breeze, in the right coordinates.

    Take a small hypersurface \(S\) through \(p\) transverse to \(X\), with coordinates \((x^{2},\dots,x^{k})\), and define \(\Psi(t,y) = \Phi_t(y)\) for \(y \in S\). Its differential at \((0,p)\) is invertible — it sends \(\partial_t\) to \(X_p\) and the \(S\)-directions to \(T_pS\) — so by the inverse function theorem \(\Psi\) is a local diffeomorphism, and in these coordinates the flow is translation in \(t\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Lie bracket properties: \([X,Y]\) is a vector field, bilinear, antisymmetric, and satisfies the Jacobi identity.

    Vector fields form a Lie algebra — the algebraic shadow of the group of diffeomorphisms.

    The second-order terms of \(XY\) and \(YX\) agree by Clairaut's theorem (Chapter 10, Layer 1), so their difference is a first-order operator, i.e. a derivation, i.e. a vector field. Bilinearity and antisymmetry are immediate, and the Jacobi identity follows by expanding all six terms of \([[X,Y],Z]+\text{cyclic}\), which cancel in pairs. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Flows commute iff the bracket vanishes: \([X,Y]=0 \iff \Phi^{X}_s\circ\Phi^{Y}_t = \Phi^{Y}_t\circ\Phi^{X}_s\).

    The bracket is precisely the obstruction to "east then north = north then east".

    Sketch: \(\mathcal L_XY = [X,Y]\) measures the derivative of \((\Phi^{X}_{-t})_{*}Y\) at \(t=0\). If the bracket vanishes, \(Y\) is invariant under the flow of \(X\), so \(\Phi^{X}\) carries integral curves of \(Y\) to integral curves of \(Y\), which is exactly commutation. Conversely, commuting flows make \((\Phi^{X}_{-t})_{*}Y\) constant, so its derivative — the bracket — is zero. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Hairy ball theorem (stated): every smooth vector field on \(S^{2n}\) vanishes somewhere.

    You cannot comb a coconut flat. There is always a cowlick — or, on Earth, always a point of zero wind.

    Sketch: a nowhere-zero field on \(S^{n}\) could be normalised and used to build a homotopy from the identity to the antipodal map. Those two maps have degrees 1 and \((-1)^{n+1}\), which differ when \(n\) is even — a contradiction. Equivalently, the Euler characteristic \(\chi(S^{2n}) = 2 \ne 0\) obstructs a non-vanishing field, by the Poincaré–Hopf theorem. \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Solve the ODE in a chart — then glue.

    All existence questions about flows reduce to Chapter 7's fixed point theorem.

  • Straighten the field — choose coordinates in which it is constant.

    Turns a differential equation into a triviality, locally.

  • Compute the bracket — to test whether two flows commute.

    In coordinates, \([X,Y]^{i} = X^{j}\partial_jY^{i} - Y^{j}\partial_jX^{i}\).

  • Use compactness for completeness.

    On a compact manifold you never have to worry about the flow escaping.

Layer 4 · Preparing to integrate

Orientation, Boundary & Partitions of Unity

The three technical ingredients Chapter 16 needs.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Orientation — a consistent choice of "positive" ordered basis in every tangent space.

    A consistent right-hand rule across the whole manifold. Some manifolds refuse to allow one.

  • Oriented atlas — all transition maps have positive Jacobian determinant.

    Chapter 5's orientation classes, glued together across charts.

  • Non-orientable manifold — one admitting no such atlas.

    The Möbius band and the Klein bottle. Walk around and you come back mirror-reversed.

  • Boundary \(\partial M\) — the points mapping to \(\{x_k = 0\}\) in a half-space chart.

    The rim of a disc; the two circles of a cylinder. \(\partial(\partial M) = \varnothing\), always.

  • Induced orientation on the boundary — "outward normal first".

    The convention that makes Stokes' theorem come out with a plus sign.

  • Partition of unity — smooth \(\rho_\alpha \ge 0\) with locally finite supports summing to 1.

    A way of chopping a global object into chart-sized pieces and reassembling it without seams.

  • Bump function — smooth, 1 on a ball, 0 outside a bigger one.

    Built from \(e^{-1/t}\). The existence of these is exactly what distinguishes smooth from analytic.

  • Density (volume element) — a positive quantity transforming by \(|\det|\).

    Lets you integrate on a non-orientable manifold, where forms cannot be consistently signed.

  • Riemannian volume — the density \(\sqrt{\det g}\) from a metric.

    Chapter 14's \(\sqrt{EG-F^{2}}\), in general. Chapter 17 supplies the metric.

Theorems

  • Existence of bump functions: for any \(p\) and neighbourhood \(U\) there is a smooth \(\rho\) that is 1 near \(p\) and 0 outside \(U\).

    Smooth functions can be switched off entirely — something no non-zero analytic function can do.

    The function \(h(t) = e^{-1/t}\) for \(t > 0\) and 0 otherwise is smooth, with all derivatives vanishing at 0 (each derivative is a rational function times \(e^{-1/t}\), which tends to 0). Then \(g(t) = \frac{h(t)}{h(t)+h(1-t)}\) is a smooth step from 0 to 1, and \(\rho(x) = g\bigl(\frac{b-\|x\|}{b-a}\bigr)\) in a chart gives the bump. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Existence of partitions of unity subordinate to any open cover of a manifold.

    The single most-used technical tool in the subject: it globalises anything that is easy locally.

    Second countability and local compactness give a countable, locally finite refinement of the cover by chart neighbourhoods with compact closures (an exhaustion argument). Place a bump function \(\psi_\alpha\) supported in each, positive on the corresponding smaller set. Local finiteness makes \(\Psi = \sum_\alpha\psi_\alpha\) a well-defined positive smooth function, and \(\rho_\alpha = \psi_\alpha/\Psi\) is the required partition. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Corollary — every manifold carries a Riemannian metric.

    Distance can always be installed. Chapter 17 explores what follows.

    In each chart take the pulled-back Euclidean inner product \(g_\alpha\), and set \(g = \sum_\alpha\rho_\alpha g_\alpha\). Each term is symmetric and positive semidefinite; at every point at least one \(\rho_\alpha\) is positive with \(g_\alpha\) positive definite, so the sum is positive definite. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Orientability criteria: \(M\) is orientable iff it has an oriented atlas, iff it carries a nowhere-vanishing top-degree form.

    Chapter 16's volume form exists exactly when the manifold is orientable.

    Given an oriented atlas, glue the local forms \(dx^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{k}\) with a partition of unity; positivity of the transition determinants keeps the sum nowhere zero. Conversely, a nowhere-zero top form \(\omega\) singles out the charts in which \(\omega\) is a positive multiple of \(dx^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{k}\), and their transitions have positive determinant. \(\blacksquare\)

  • The Möbius band is non-orientable.

    Slide a right-handed frame once around and it comes back left-handed.

    Parametrise the core circle and carry a frame around it. The gluing identification of the band reverses the transverse direction, so the transition map on the overlap has negative Jacobian determinant, and no relabelling of charts can fix it — a consistent choice would have to be both positive and negative on the single overlap. \(\blacksquare\)

  • \(\partial M\) is a \((k-1)\)-manifold without boundary, and inherits an orientation from \(M\).

    The edge of a disc is a circle, and the circle has no edge.

    Restricting half-space charts to \(\{x_k = 0\}\) gives charts of \(\partial M\) onto open subsets of \(\mathbb{R}^{k-1}\), whose transitions are restrictions of the original ones, hence smooth. No point of \(\partial M\) has a half-space chart in \(\partial M\), so \(\partial(\partial M) = \varnothing\). The induced orientation is defined by declaring a basis of \(T_p\partial M\) positive when preceded by an outward normal it forms a positive basis of \(T_pM\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Integration of a density is well defined — chart-independent, thanks to partitions of unity.

    Total mass of a curved sheet, computed chart by chart and summed without double counting.

    Define \(\int_M\mu = \sum_\alpha\int_{\varphi_\alpha(U_\alpha)}\rho_\alpha\mu\) in coordinates. Under a change of chart the coordinate expression of a density picks up \(|\det D\psi|\), which the change of variables formula (Chapter 12, Layer 4) cancels. Two different partitions of unity give the same total, since \(\sum_\alpha\rho_\alpha\sum_\beta\sigma_\beta\) can be summed in either order (all sums are locally finite). \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Do it locally, glue with a partition of unity.

    The standard three-line proof of most existence theorems on manifolds.

  • Check transition determinants — to decide orientability.

    All positive, and you can orient; one stubborn negative, and you cannot.

  • Use bump functions to localise.

    Multiply by a bump and any global problem becomes a chart problem.

  • Fix the boundary orientation convention early.

    Outward normal first. Get it wrong and Stokes' theorem acquires a spurious minus sign.
