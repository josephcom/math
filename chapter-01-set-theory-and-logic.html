Layer 1 · Ground floor

Propositional Logic

Statements, and the rules for combining them.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Proposition — a statement that is either true (T) or false (F), never both.

    "It is raining" is a proposition. "Close the door!" is not — it can't be true or false.

  • Negation \(\neg p\) — "not p".

    p = "I'm hungry" → \(\neg p\) = "I'm not hungry."

  • Conjunction \(p \land q\) — "p and q".

    "It's cold and wet" — true only if both parts are true.

  • Disjunction \(p \lor q\) — "p or q" (inclusive).

    "I'll have tea or coffee" — true if you have either one, or both.

  • Implication \(p \to q\) — "if p then q".

    "If it rains, the ground gets wet." Only false in one case: it rains, but the ground stays dry.

  • Biconditional \(p \leftrightarrow q\) — "p if and only if q" (iff).

    "You pass iff you score ≥ 50." Both happen together, or neither does.

  • Truth table — a table listing truth values for every combination of inputs.

    For \(p \land q\): TT→T, TF→F, FT→F, FF→F. Four rows, no case forgotten.

  • Tautology — always true.

    "Tomorrow it will rain or it won't." Guaranteed true — and tells you nothing.

  • Contradiction — always false.

    "I'm taller than you and you're taller than me." Can't both hold.

  • Logical equivalence \(p \equiv q\) — same truth value in every case.

    "It's not the case that it's not raining" ≡ "It's raining." Different words, same claim.

  • Converse & contrapositive — of \(p \to q\): converse is \(q \to p\), contrapositive is \(\neg q \to \neg p\).

    "If it's a dog, it's an animal." Converse: "if animal, then dog" — false! Contrapositive: "if not an animal, not a dog" — true, always.

Axioms

  • A1: \(p \to (q \to p)\) — a truth stays true, whatever else you assume.

    "2 + 2 = 4" is true. So "if pigs fly, then 2 + 2 = 4" is also true — adding assumptions can't break a truth.

  • A2: \((p \to (q \to r)) \to ((p \to q) \to (p \to r))\) — lets you re-wire chained if-thens.

    The bookkeeping rule for nested "if…then"s: it lets a proof distribute one assumption across a whole chain.

  • A3: \((\neg q \to \neg p) \to (p \to q)\) — contrapositive reasoning is legal.

    Prove "no smoke → no fire", and you've proven "fire → smoke."

The One Inference Rule

  • Modus ponens — from \(p\) and \(p \to q\), conclude \(q\).

    "If it rains, streets get wet. It's raining. Therefore: streets are wet."

Theorems — The Laws of Logic

  • Double negation: \(\neg\neg p \equiv p\).

    "I'm not not going" = "I'm going."

    Truth table: if \(p\) is T, then \(\neg p\) is F, so \(\neg\neg p\) is T; if \(p\) is F, then \(\neg\neg p\) is F. The columns for \(p\) and \(\neg\neg p\) agree in both rows, so \(\neg\neg p \equiv p\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • De Morgan's laws: \(\neg(p \land q) \equiv \neg p \lor \neg q\) and \(\neg(p \lor q) \equiv \neg p \land \neg q\).

    "He's not rich-and-famous" = "he's not rich, or not famous (or neither)."

    \(\neg(p \land q)\) is F exactly when \(p \land q\) is T, i.e. when \(p\) and \(q\) are both T. And \(\neg p \lor \neg q\) is F exactly when \(\neg p\) and \(\neg q\) are both F — again when \(p\) and \(q\) are both T. Same single F-row out of four, so the columns agree. The second law follows by applying the first to \(\neg p, \neg q\) and cancelling double negations. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Contrapositive law: \((p \to q) \equiv (\neg q \to \neg p)\).

    "Dog → animal" says exactly the same as "not animal → not dog."

    \(p \to q\) is F only in the row \(p = \text{T},\ q = \text{F}\). And \(\neg q \to \neg p\) is F only when \(\neg q\) is T and \(\neg p\) is F — the very same row. One shared failing row, agreement everywhere else: identical columns. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Implication as disjunction: \((p \to q) \equiv (\neg p \lor q)\).

    "If it rains, I stay home" = "It doesn't rain, or I stay home."

    Both sides are F in exactly one of the four rows: \(p \to q\) fails only at \(p = \text{T},\ q = \text{F}\); and \(\neg p \lor q\) fails only when \(\neg p\) and \(q\) are both F — i.e. the same row. Columns match. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Commutativity: \(p \land q \equiv q \land p\); same for \(\lor\).

    "Cold and wet" = "wet and cold."

    \(p \land q\) is T exactly when both \(p\) and \(q\) are T — a condition symmetric in the two letters — so its column equals that of \(q \land p\) in every row. Likewise \(p \lor q\) ("at least one is T") is symmetric. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Associativity: \((p \land q) \land r \equiv p \land (q \land r)\).

    "(Cold and wet) and windy" = "cold and (wet and windy)" — grouping doesn't matter.

    Each side is T exactly when all three of \(p, q, r\) are T, and F otherwise — the grouping never enters that condition. (Dually for \(\lor\): T iff at least one is T.) The 8 truth-table rows confirm it mechanically. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Distributivity: \(p \land (q \lor r) \equiv (p \land q) \lor (p \land r)\).

    "Coffee, with cake or pie" = "coffee-and-cake, or coffee-and-pie."

    The left side is T iff \(p\) is T and at least one of \(q, r\) is T. The right side is T iff \(p\) is T together with \(q\), or \(p\) is T together with \(r\) — the same condition. All 8 rows agree. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Idempotence: \(p \land p \equiv p\).

    "It's raining and it's raining" — saying it twice adds nothing.

    Row check: \(p = \text{T}\) gives \(p \land p = \text{T}\); \(p = \text{F}\) gives F. The column is identical to \(p\)'s. (Same two rows for \(p \lor p\).) \(\blacksquare\)

  • Absorption: \(p \lor (p \land q) \equiv p\).

    "It's raining, or it's raining-and-cold" boils down to just "it's raining."

    If \(p\) is T, the first disjunct makes the left side T — matching \(p\). If \(p\) is F, then \(p \land q\) is F too, so \(p \lor (p \land q)\) is F — again matching \(p\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Excluded middle: \(p \lor \neg p\) is a tautology.

    "The light is on, or it isn't." No third option.

    Only two rows exist: \(p = \text{T}\) makes the first disjunct T; \(p = \text{F}\) makes \(\neg p\) T. Either way \(p \lor \neg p\) is T — a tautology. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Non-contradiction: \(\neg(p \land \neg p)\) is a tautology.

    The light can't be on and off at the same moment.

    In both rows one of the conjuncts \(p, \neg p\) is F, so \(p \land \neg p\) is always F. Its negation is therefore T in every row — a tautology. \(\blacksquare\)

Derived Inference Rules

  • Modus tollens — from \(p \to q\) and \(\neg q\), conclude \(\neg p\).

    "If it rained, the ground would be wet. The ground is dry. So it didn't rain."

    By the contrapositive law, \(p \to q \equiv \neg q \to \neg p\). From \(\neg q\) and \(\neg q \to \neg p\), modus ponens gives \(\neg p\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Hypothetical syllogism — from \(p \to q\) and \(q \to r\), conclude \(p \to r\).

    "Rain → wet roads. Wet roads → slippery. Therefore rain → slippery."

    Assume \(p\). Modus ponens with \(p \to q\) gives \(q\); modus ponens again with \(q \to r\) gives \(r\). So assuming \(p\) always yields \(r\): \(p \to r\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Disjunctive syllogism — from \(p \lor q\) and \(\neg p\), conclude \(q\).

    "My keys are in my coat or my bag. Not in the coat. So: in the bag."

    By implication-as-disjunction (read right-to-left, with double negation), \(p \lor q \equiv \neg p \to q\). From \(\neg p\) and \(\neg p \to q\), modus ponens gives \(q\). \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Truth-table method — mechanically check any equivalence (\(2^n\) rows for \(n\) variables).

    To verify \(p \to q \equiv \neg p \lor q\), write out all 4 rows — both columns match. Done, no cleverness needed.

  • Algebraic simplification — rewrite using the laws above.

    Like school algebra, but with sentences: simplify \(\neg(\neg p \land \neg q)\) to \(p \lor q\) via De Morgan.

  • Direct proof — assume p, march to q.

    Even + even is even: \(2a + 2b = 2(a+b)\). Straight line, done.

  • Proof by contrapositive — prove \(\neg q \to \neg p\) instead.

    To show "\(n^2\) odd → \(n\) odd", it's easier to prove "\(n\) even → \(n^2\) even."

  • Proof by contradiction — assume the opposite, derive an absurdity.

    \(\sqrt{2}\) irrational: assume it's a fraction \(a/b\) in lowest terms → both \(a\) and \(b\) turn out even → contradiction.

  • Proof by cases — split into scenarios, prove each.

    \(|x| \ge 0\): check the case \(x \ge 0\), then the case \(x < 0\). Together they cover everything.

Layer 2 · Adding variables

Predicate (First-Order) Logic

Statements about "all" and "some" things.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Predicate \(P(x)\) — a statement whose truth depends on \(x\).

    "\(x > 3\)": true for \(x = 5\), false for \(x = 1\). Not true or false until \(x\) is filled in.

  • Domain — the universe of allowed values for the variables.

    "Someone is a doctor" may be false in this room but true across all humans. Domain changes truth.

  • Universal quantifier \(\forall x\, P(x)\) — "for all x".

    "All swans are white." One black swan makes it false.

  • Existential quantifier \(\exists x\, P(x)\) — "there exists an x".

    "Someone in the room is left-handed." One person is enough.

  • Unique existence \(\exists! x\, P(x)\) — "exactly one x".

    There is exactly one number that adds nothing: 0.

  • Free vs. bound variable — bound = captured by a quantifier.

    In \(\exists x\,(x + y = 3)\): \(x\) is bound (spoken for), \(y\) is free — the truth depends on what \(y\) is.

  • Sentence — a formula with no free variables.

    "\(\forall x,\ x \ge 0\)" over the naturals: nothing left to fill in — it's simply true.

Theorems — Quantifier Laws

  • Negating \(\forall\): \(\neg\forall x\, P(x) \equiv \exists x\, \neg P(x)\).

    "Not all swans are white" = "some swan is not white."

    (\(\Rightarrow\)) Suppose \(\neg\forall x\,P(x)\), and suppose for contradiction no \(x\) satisfied \(\neg P\). Then every \(x\) would satisfy \(P\) — contradicting the supposition. So \(\exists x\,\neg P(x)\). (\(\Leftarrow\)) A witness with \(\neg P(x)\) directly refutes \(\forall x\,P(x)\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Negating \(\exists\): \(\neg\exists x\, P(x) \equiv \forall x\, \neg P(x)\).

    "No one called" = "everyone didn't call."

    Apply the previous law to the predicate \(\neg P\): \(\neg\forall x\,\neg P(x) \equiv \exists x\,\neg\neg P(x) \equiv \exists x\,P(x)\). Negate both sides and cancel the double negation on the left: \(\forall x\,\neg P(x) \equiv \neg\exists x\,P(x)\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Order matters across types: \(\exists y\,\forall x\, P(x,y) \to \forall x\,\exists y\, P(x,y)\), but not the reverse.

    "Everyone has a mother" (\(\forall\) person \(\exists\) mother) is true. "There's one mother of everyone" (\(\exists\) mother \(\forall\) person) is not.

    Assume \(\exists y\,\forall x\,P(x,y)\); name a witness \(b\), so \(\forall x\,P(x,b)\). Given any \(x\), the choice \(y = b\) shows \(\exists y\,P(x,y)\); as \(x\) was arbitrary, \(\forall x\,\exists y\,P(x,y)\). The converse fails: over \(\mathbb{N}\) let \(P(x,y)\) be "\(y = x\)" — \(\forall x\,\exists y\) is true, \(\exists y\,\forall x\) is false. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Same-type quantifiers commute: \(\forall x\,\forall y \equiv \forall y\,\forall x\).

    "Every person likes every food" — either order of saying it, same claim.

    Each side asserts exactly that \(P(x,y)\) holds for every pair of values \((x,y)\) from the domain — the order of announcing the two \(\forall\)'s doesn't change which pairs are covered. (Dually, \(\exists x\,\exists y \equiv \exists y\,\exists x\): some pair works.) \(\blacksquare\)

Inference Rules

  • Universal instantiation — from \(\forall x\, P(x)\), conclude \(P(c)\) for any specific \(c\).

    "All humans are mortal" → "Socrates is mortal."

  • Universal generalization — proven for an arbitrary \(c\) → true for all.

    Prove the angle sum is 180° for a completely generic triangle — you never used its size — so it's true for all triangles.

  • Existential instantiation — from \(\exists x\, P(x)\), name a witness.

    "Someone stole the cookie. Call that person T, and reason about T."

  • Existential generalization — from \(P(c)\), conclude \(\exists x\, P(x)\).

    "7 is prime, therefore primes exist."

Methods

  • Proving \(\forall\) — take an arbitrary \(x\), prove \(P(x)\).

    "Every even number squared is even": take a generic even \(2k\), then \((2k)^2 = 4k^2 = 2(2k^2)\). ✓

  • Proving \(\exists\) — exhibit one concrete witness.

    "There is a prime above 100": here's 101. Done.

  • Counterexample — one failure kills a \(\forall\) claim.

    "All primes are odd" — meet 2.

Layer 3 · First objects

Sets — Basic Notions

Collections, membership, and containment.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Set & element — a collection of distinct objects; \(x \in A\) means \(x\) belongs to \(A\).

    \(\{2,3,5\}\) is a set; \(3 \in \{2,3,5\}\), but \(4 \notin \{2,3,5\}\). Order and repeats don't matter.

  • Roster vs. set-builder notation — list the members, or state their rule: \(\{x : P(x)\}\).

    \(\{2, 4, 6, \dots\}\) and \(\{x : x \text{ is even}\}\) describe the same set.

  • Empty set \(\varnothing\) — the set with nothing in it.

    The set of unicorns you own.

  • Set equality — same elements ⇒ same set.

    \(\{1,2\} = \{2,1\} = \{1,1,2\}\). Only membership counts.

  • Subset \(A \subseteq B\) — every element of \(A\) is in \(B\).

    Cats ⊆ animals: every cat is an animal.

  • Proper subset \(A \subsetneq B\) — subset, but not everything.

    Cats ⊊ animals: dogs exist, so "animals" has more.

  • Power set \(\mathcal{P}(A)\) — the set of all subsets of \(A\).

    \(\mathcal{P}(\{a,b\}) = \{\varnothing, \{a\}, \{b\}, \{a,b\}\}\) — every possible "selection", including picking nothing.

  • Cardinality \(|A|\) — the number of elements (finite case).

    \(|\{\text{red},\text{green},\text{blue}\}| = 3\).

  • Disjoint sets — \(A \cap B = \varnothing\).

    Dogs and cats: no animal is both.

  • Number sets: \(\mathbb{N} \subseteq \mathbb{Z} \subseteq \mathbb{Q} \subseteq \mathbb{R} \subseteq \mathbb{C}\).

    Counting numbers → add negatives → add fractions → add all decimals (like \(\pi\)) → add imaginary numbers.

Propositions

  • \(\varnothing \subseteq A\) for every set \(A\).

    "Every unicorn I own is also in your set" — true for free, since I own none. (Vacuous truth.)

    We must show \(\forall x\,(x \in \varnothing \to x \in A)\). The antecedent \(x \in \varnothing\) is false for every \(x\), and an implication with a false antecedent is true. So the claim holds vacuously. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Reflexivity: \(A \subseteq A\).

    Every cat is a cat.

    \(x \in A \to x \in A\) is an instance of the tautology \(p \to p\), true for every \(x\). That is the definition of \(A \subseteq A\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Transitivity: \(A \subseteq B \subseteq C \implies A \subseteq C\).

    Cats ⊆ mammals ⊆ animals, so cats ⊆ animals.

    Take any \(x \in A\). Since \(A \subseteq B\), \(x \in B\); since \(B \subseteq C\), \(x \in C\). (Hypothetical syllogism applied to the two membership implications.) As \(x\) was arbitrary, \(A \subseteq C\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Equality via double inclusion: \(A = B \iff A \subseteq B \text{ and } B \subseteq A\).

    Two clubs are identical exactly when each member of one belongs to the other — checked in both directions.

    By Extensionality (Layer 4), \(A = B \iff \forall x\,(x \in A \leftrightarrow x \in B)\). A biconditional is exactly two implications, \(x \in A \to x \in B\) and \(x \in B \to x \in A\) — which say precisely \(A \subseteq B\) and \(B \subseteq A\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • \(\varnothing\) is unique.

    Two empty boxes hold exactly the same contents (nothing) — so as sets, they're one and the same.

    Suppose \(\varnothing_1\) and \(\varnothing_2\) are both empty. Then \(\varnothing_1 \subseteq \varnothing_2\) and \(\varnothing_2 \subseteq \varnothing_1\), both vacuously (the previous proposition). By double inclusion, \(\varnothing_1 = \varnothing_2\). \(\blacksquare\)

Theorem

  • Power set size: if \(|A| = n\), then \(|\mathcal{P}(A)| = 2^n\).

    3 pizza toppings → \(2^3 = 8\) possible pizzas (each topping is a yes/no, including the plain one).

    Induction on \(n\). Base: \(|A| = 0\) means \(A = \varnothing\), whose only subset is \(\varnothing\) itself: \(2^0 = 1\). ✓ Step: let \(|A| = n + 1\) and pick \(a \in A\). Every subset either omits \(a\) — a subset of \(A \setminus \{a\}\), and by hypothesis there are \(2^n\) of those — or contains \(a\) — one of those same \(2^n\) with \(a\) added. Total: \(2^n + 2^n = 2^{n+1}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Layer 4 · The rulebook

The ZFC Axioms

Nine-ish rules that make set theory paradox-free. All of modern math fits inside them.

Axioms

  • Extensionality — sets with the same elements are equal.

    Two shopping lists with exactly the same items are the same list, whatever paper they're on.

  • Empty set — \(\varnothing\) exists.

    There is an empty box. Math needs at least that to start.

  • Pairing — for any \(a, b\), the set \(\{a, b\}\) exists.

    Given any two things, you may put them in one box together.

  • Union — for any set of sets, their union exists.

    A box of boxes: you may dump all inner contents into one big box.

  • Power set — \(\mathcal{P}(A)\) exists.

    Given a menu, the set of all possible orders from it exists.

  • Infinity — an infinite set exists.

    The counting numbers 0, 1, 2, … never stop — this axiom guarantees the whole endless collection is a legal set.

  • Separation — from set \(A\) and property \(P\), the subset \(\{x \in A : P(x)\}\) exists. Only from an existing set!

    From "all animals" you may carve out "animals that fly". You may not conjure "all things that fly" from thin air — that restriction is what blocks the paradoxes.

  • Replacement — the image of a set under a definable rule is a set.

    Apply "double it" to \(\{1,2,3\}\): the result \(\{2,4,6\}\) is guaranteed to be a set.

  • Foundation — no set contains itself; no infinite downward \(\in\)-chains.

    No box may contain itself, and Russian dolls can't nest downward forever.

  • Choice (AC) — from any family of nonempty sets, you can pick one element from each.

    Infinitely many pairs of identical socks: no rule tells you which sock to take — Choice says a full selection exists anyway.

Theorem — The Crisis Behind the Rules

  • Russell's paradox — \(R = \{x : x \notin x\}\) cannot be a set.

    The barber shaves exactly those who don't shave themselves. Who shaves the barber? Either answer contradicts itself — so no such "set" can exist.

    Suppose \(R\) were a set, and ask whether \(R \in R\). By \(R\)'s defining condition, \(R \in R \iff R \notin R\) — a contradiction either way. So no set satisfies the description. (ZFC's Separation only builds \(\{x \in A : x \notin x\}\) from an existing \(A\); the same argument then merely shows that set lies outside \(A\) — no paradox.) \(\blacksquare\)

  • Corollary — there is no set of all sets.

    A "set of everything" would let you carve out Russell's monster with Separation. So the universe of sets is not itself a set.

    Suppose a set \(V\) contained every set. Separation would then legally form \(R = \{x \in V : x \notin x\}\). Since every set is in \(V\), this \(R\) is exactly Russell's \(R\) — which we just proved cannot exist. Contradiction; no such \(V\). \(\blacksquare\)

Layer 5 · The toolkit

Set Operations & Their Algebra

Combining sets — a perfect mirror of Layer 1's logic.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Union \(A \cup B\) — in \(A\) or \(B\) (or both).

    Tea-likers ∪ coffee-likers = everyone who likes at least one.

  • Intersection \(A \cap B\) — in both.

    People who like both tea and coffee.

  • Difference \(A \setminus B\) — in \(A\), not in \(B\).

    Tea-likers \ coffee-likers = the tea-only crowd.

  • Complement \(A^{c} = U \setminus A\) — everything else.

    Within the office (the universe \(U\)), the complement of tea-likers is everyone who doesn't like tea.

  • Symmetric difference \(A \,\triangle\, B\) — in exactly one of the two.

    People who like tea or coffee, but not both.

  • Indexed families \(\bigcup_i A_i,\ \bigcap_i A_i\) — union/intersection over many sets at once.

    Union over all days of "who visited the gym that day" = everyone who came at least once. Intersection = the every-single-day regulars.

Theorems — The Algebra of Sets

  • Commutative: \(A \cup B = B \cup A\).

    "Tea or coffee people" = "coffee or tea people."

    Element-chasing: \(x \in A \cup B \iff x \in A \lor x \in B \iff x \in B \lor x \in A \iff x \in B \cup A\), using commutativity of \(\lor\) (Layer 1). Extensionality turns "same members" into equality. Same for \(\cap\) with \(\land\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Associative: \((A \cup B) \cup C = A \cup (B \cup C)\).

    Merging three guest lists: merge in any grouping, same crowd shows up.

    An \(x\) belongs to either side iff \(x \in A \lor x \in B \lor x \in C\); by associativity of \(\lor\) (Layer 1) the grouping is irrelevant. Extensionality finishes. (Dually for \(\cap\).) \(\blacksquare\)

  • Distributive: \(A \cap (B \cup C) = (A \cap B) \cup (A \cap C)\).

    "Students who also play (piano or violin)" = "students-who-play-piano, plus students-who-play-violin."

    \(x \in A \cap (B \cup C) \iff x \in A \land (x \in B \lor x \in C)\). By Layer 1's distributive law this is \((x \in A \land x \in B) \lor (x \in A \land x \in C)\), i.e. \(x \in (A \cap B) \cup (A \cap C)\). Extensionality. \(\blacksquare\)

  • De Morgan (sets): \((A \cup B)^{c} = A^{c} \cap B^{c}\).

    "Likes neither tea nor coffee" = "doesn't like tea AND doesn't like coffee."

    \(x \in (A \cup B)^c \iff \neg(x \in A \lor x \in B) \iff \neg(x \in A) \land \neg(x \in B)\) — Layer 1's De Morgan — \(\iff x \in A^c \cap B^c\). The dual law \((A \cap B)^c = A^c \cup B^c\) is the same chase with the other De Morgan law. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Identity: \(A \cup \varnothing = A\); \(A \cap U = A\).

    Adding an empty guest list changes nothing; intersecting with "everyone" changes nothing.

    \(x \in A \cup \varnothing \iff x \in A \lor \text{F} \iff x \in A\) (a false disjunct drops out). \(x \in A \cap U \iff x \in A \land \text{T} \iff x \in A\) (a true conjunct drops out). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Complement laws: \(A \cup A^{c} = U\); \(A \cap A^{c} = \varnothing\); \((A^{c})^{c} = A\).

    Tea-likers plus non-tea-likers = everyone; nobody is in both camps; "not not tea-liker" = tea-liker.

    For any \(x \in U\): excluded middle gives \(x \in A \lor x \notin A\), so \(A \cup A^c = U\). Non-contradiction forbids both at once, so \(A \cap A^c = \varnothing\). And \(x \in (A^c)^c \iff \neg\neg(x \in A) \iff x \in A\) by double negation. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Absorption: \(A \cup (A \cap B) = A\).

    Tea-likers, plus the both-drinks people — but they were already tea-likers. Nothing new.

    \(x \in A \cup (A \cap B) \iff (x \in A) \lor (x \in A \land x \in B)\), which by Layer 1's absorption law is just \(x \in A\). Extensionality. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Generalized De Morgan: \(\left(\bigcup_i A_i\right)^{c} = \bigcap_i A_i^{c}\).

    "Missed every meeting" = "not (attended at least one)".

    \(x \in \left(\bigcup_i A_i\right)^c \iff \neg\exists i\,(x \in A_i) \iff \forall i\,(x \notin A_i)\) — the quantifier negation law from Layer 2 — \(\iff x \in \bigcap_i A_i^c\). \(\blacksquare\)

Theorem — Counting

  • Inclusion–Exclusion: \(|A \cup B| = |A| + |B| - |A \cap B|\).

    30 like tea, 20 like coffee, 10 like both → \(30 + 20 - 10 = 40\) people, not 50. The overlap was counted twice.

    \(A \cup B\) splits into three disjoint pieces: \(A \setminus B\), \(A \cap B\), \(B \setminus A\), so \(|A \cup B|\) is the sum of their sizes. Also \(|A| = |A \setminus B| + |A \cap B|\) and \(|B| = |B \setminus A| + |A \cap B|\). Then \(|A| + |B| - |A \cap B| = |A \setminus B| + |A \cap B| + |B \setminus A|\) — the same three-piece sum. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Corollary (3 sets): add singles, subtract pairs, add back the triple.

    Counting students in football, chess, or drama: overlaps get double-counted, the triple-overlap gets over-corrected — the formula fixes both.

    Apply the two-set formula twice: \(|A \cup B \cup C| = |A \cup B| + |C| - |(A \cup B) \cap C|\). Expand \(|A \cup B|\) by the formula, distribute \((A \cup B) \cap C = (A \cap C) \cup (B \cap C)\), and apply the formula once more to that union (its overlap is \(A \cap B \cap C\)). Collecting terms: singles − pairs + the triple. \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Element-chasing — prove \(A = B\) by showing \(x \in A \iff x \in B\).

    Take a generic member \(x\) of the left side, use logic to show it must be in the right side — and back.

  • Double inclusion — prove \(A \subseteq B\), then \(B \subseteq A\).

    Two half-proofs, one equality. The standard exam move.

  • Venn diagrams — intuition, not proof.

    Sketch three overlapping circles to see De Morgan — then prove it properly with elements.

  • Algebraic manipulation — rewrite with the laws.

    Simplify \((A \cup B) \cap A = A\) by absorption, no diagram needed.

Layer 6 · Structure appears

Ordered Pairs, Products & Relations

Order, connections between things, and ways of comparing.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Ordered pair \((a, b)\) — order matters.

    Map coordinates: \((3, 5)\) and \((5, 3)\) are different places — unlike the set \(\{3, 5\}\).

  • Kuratowski's trick: \((a,b) = \{\{a\},\{a,b\}\}\).

    A clever way to fake "order" using only orderless sets: the element appearing in both inner sets is the first one.

  • Cartesian product \(A \times B\) — all pairs.

    \(\{S, M, L\} \times \{\text{red}, \text{blue}\}\) = the 6 possible t-shirts.

  • Relation — any set of pairs \(R \subseteq A \times B\).

    "Is a friend of" is just the set of all (person, person) pairs where friendship holds.

  • Reflexive: \(\forall a,\ aRa\).

    "≤": every number is ≤ itself. "Is taller than" is not reflexive.

  • Symmetric: \(aRb \to bRa\).

    "Is a sibling of" — goes both ways. "Is a parent of" — doesn't.

  • Antisymmetric: \(aRb \land bRa \to a = b\).

    If \(x \le y\) and \(y \le x\), they're the same number.

  • Transitive: \(aRb \land bRc \to aRc\).

    Ali is taller than Ben, Ben taller than Cy → Ali is taller than Cy.

  • Equivalence relation — reflexive + symmetric + transitive.

    "Has the same birthday as." Everyone matches themselves, matching is mutual, and it chains.

  • Equivalence class \([a]\) — everything related to \(a\).

    [you] = the set of all people sharing your birthday.

  • Partition — nonempty, disjoint pieces covering all of \(A\).

    366 birthday-groups slice up all of humanity: everyone in exactly one group.

  • Quotient set \(A/R\) — the set of the classes themselves.

    Zoom out: treat each birthday-group as a single object. That collection of 366 groups is the quotient.

  • Partial order — reflexive + antisymmetric + transitive; some pairs incomparable.

    Folders under "contains": your Photos and Music folders — neither contains the other.

  • Total order — a partial order where everything is comparable.

    ≤ on numbers: pick any two, one is smaller (or equal). No "incomparable" ties.

Propositions

  • Pair equality: \((a,b) = (c,d) \iff a = c \land b = d\).

    Two map points coincide exactly when both coordinates match, slot by slot — proving Kuratowski's trick really works.

    (\(\Leftarrow\)) Immediate. (\(\Rightarrow\)) Let \(\{\{a\},\{a,b\}\} = \{\{c\},\{c,d\}\}\). Case \(a = b\): the left side is \(\{\{a\}\}\), forcing \(\{c\} = \{c,d\} = \{a\}\), so \(c = d = a\), giving \(a = c\) and \(b = d\). Case \(a \neq b\): the singleton \(\{a\}\) can only equal the singleton \(\{c\}\), so \(a = c\); then \(\{a,b\} = \{c,d\}\) with \(b \neq a = c\) forces \(b = d\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Product size: \(|A \times B| = |A| \cdot |B|\).

    3 sizes × 2 colors = 6 shirts.

    For each fixed \(a \in A\), the pairs with first coordinate \(a\) are \((a,b)\) for \(b \in B\): exactly \(|B|\) of them. Distinct first coordinates give disjoint groups (pair equality), and there are \(|A|\) groups — so \(|A| \cdot |B|\) pairs in all. \(\blacksquare\)

Theorem

  • Equivalence relations ⟺ partitions — two views of the same thing.

    "Same birthday" (a way of relating people) and "the 366 birthday groups" (a way of slicing people) carry identical information.

    Equivalence → partition: reflexivity puts \(a \in [a]\), so classes cover \(A\); and if some \(x \in [a] \cap [b]\), then for any \(y \sim a\), symmetry and transitivity chain \(y \sim a \sim x \sim b\), so \([a] = [b]\) — classes are identical or disjoint. Partition → equivalence: define \(a \sim b\) iff \(a, b\) share a piece; this is reflexive (own piece), symmetric (sharing is mutual), transitive (pieces are disjoint, so a shared piece is the same piece). The two constructions undo each other. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Corollary — classes are identical or disjoint, and they cover everything.

    Nobody belongs to two birthday groups, and nobody belongs to none.

    Both halves sit inside the theorem's proof: \(a \in [a]\) (reflexivity) shows every element is covered, and the argument that a common member forces \([a] = [b]\) shows two classes are identical or disjoint. \(\blacksquare\)

Layer 7 · The workhorse

Functions

Reliable input → output machines.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Function \(f : A \to B\) — every input gets exactly one output.

    "Person → their birthday" is a function. "Person → their sibling" is not: some have none, some have three.

  • Domain, codomain, range — inputs, allowed outputs, outputs actually hit.

    People → months: domain = people, codomain = 12 months, range = the months someone was actually born in.

  • Image & preimage: \(f(S)\) and \(f^{-1}(T)\).

    \(f^{-1}(\text{May})\) = everyone born in May — "who lands there?"

  • Injective (one-to-one) — different inputs, different outputs.

    Fingerprint → person: no two people share one.

  • Surjective (onto) — every output is hit.

    In a big crowd, every month is somebody's birth month.

  • Bijective — a perfect one-to-one matching.

    A sold-out show: each seat ↔ exactly one ticket-holder.

  • Composition: \((g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x))\).

    "Mother of" after "father of" = paternal grandmother.

  • Identity: \(\mathrm{id}(x) = x\).

    The do-nothing machine: whatever goes in comes straight out.

  • Inverse \(f^{-1}\) — undoes \(f\); exists iff \(f\) is a bijection.

    Encrypt ↔ decrypt: each message locks to one unique cipher, so it can be unlocked.

Propositions & Lemmas

  • Composition is associative: \(h \circ (g \circ f) = (h \circ g) \circ f\).

    Do \(f\), then \(g\), then \(h\). However you mentally group the steps, the assembly line is the same.

    Both sides are functions on the same domain. Feed either one an \(x\): each computes \(h(g(f(x)))\). Two functions with the same domain and identical outputs at every input are equal (functions are sets of pairs — Extensionality). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Composition preserves inj / surj / bij.

    Two lossless steps in a row → lossless overall. Chain two perfect matchings → a perfect matching.

    Injective: if \(g(f(x)) = g(f(y))\), injectivity of \(g\) gives \(f(x) = f(y)\), then injectivity of \(f\) gives \(x = y\). Surjective: given \(z\), surjectivity of \(g\) yields \(y\) with \(g(y) = z\), then surjectivity of \(f\) yields \(x\) with \(f(x) = y\), so \(g(f(x)) = z\). Bijective = both at once. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Inverse ⟺ bijection, and the inverse is unique.

    Only a perfect pairing can be undone: if two inputs shared an output, "undo" wouldn't know which to return.

    If \(f\) is a bijection, each \(b \in B\) has exactly one preimage (at least one by surjectivity, at most one by injectivity) — define \(f^{-1}(b)\) to be it; both compositions are then identities. Conversely, if \(g\) undoes \(f\) both ways: \(f(x) = f(y) \implies x = g(f(x)) = g(f(y)) = y\) (injective), and every \(b = f(g(b))\) is hit (surjective). Uniqueness: if \(h\) also works, \(g = g \circ \mathrm{id} = g \circ (f \circ h) = (g \circ f) \circ h = \mathrm{id} \circ h = h\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Preimages behave better than images — \(f^{-1}\) preserves \(\cup\), \(\cap\), complements.

    "Born in May or June" = "born in May" ∪ "born in June" — preimages split cleanly along set operations. Images sometimes don't.

    \(x \in f^{-1}(S \cup T) \iff f(x) \in S \cup T \iff f(x) \in S \lor f(x) \in T \iff x \in f^{-1}(S) \cup f^{-1}(T)\). The \(\cap\) and complement cases are the same chase with \(\land\) and \(\neg\): preimage membership is a single condition on \(f(x)\), so it distributes over every connective. (Images fail for \(\cap\): \(f(S \cap T) \subseteq f(S) \cap f(T)\) can be strict when \(f\) merges points.) \(\blacksquare\)

  • Pigeonhole principle: \(|A| > |B|\) ⟹ no injection \(A \to B\).

    13 people, 12 months: two must share a birth month.

    An injection sends distinct inputs to distinct outputs, so its range contains \(|A|\) distinct elements sitting inside \(B\) — forcing \(|A| \le |B|\). Contrapositive: if \(|A| > |B|\), no injection exists, i.e. some two inputs collide. \(\blacksquare\)

Layer 8 · Numbers are born

Natural Numbers & Induction

\(\mathbb{N}\) built out of pure sets — and the domino principle.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Von Neumann naturals: \(0 = \varnothing,\ 1 = \{0\},\ 2 = \{0,1\}, \dots\)

    Each number is the set of all smaller ones: \(3 = \{0, 1, 2\}\). Numbers are made of nothing but nested emptiness.

  • Successor: \(S(n) = n \cup \{n\}\).

    "+1" = toss the number itself into its own bag: \(2 \cup \{2\} = \{0,1,2\} = 3\).

Axioms — Peano (derivable inside ZFC)

  • The five rules: 0 exists; every \(n\) has a successor; 0 is no one's successor; successors of different numbers differ; induction holds.

    The setup rules for an infinite domino line: there's a first domino, each has a next, no loops, no merges.

Theorems & Principles

  • Mathematical induction: \(P(0)\), and \(P(n) \to P(n+1)\), gives \(\forall n\, P(n)\).

    Dominoes: the first falls, and each falling domino knocks the next → all infinitely many fall.

    In ZFC, \(\mathbb{N}\) is defined as the smallest set containing 0 and closed under successor (the intersection of all such sets, which exist by Infinity). Let \(S = \{n \in \mathbb{N} : P(n)\}\). The hypotheses say exactly that \(S\) contains 0 and is closed under successor — so \(\mathbb{N} \subseteq S\) by minimality: \(P\) holds for every natural. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Strong induction — the step may lean on all earlier cases.

    To knock down domino \(n\), you may use the combined weight of every domino before it — handy for facts like "every number ≥ 2 factors into primes."

    Let \(Q(n)\) = "\(P(k)\) holds for all \(k \le n\)". Then \(Q(0)\) is just \(P(0)\), and \(Q(n) \to Q(n+1)\) is exactly the strong-induction step. Ordinary induction gives \(\forall n\,Q(n)\), and \(Q(n)\) contains \(P(n)\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Well-ordering principle — every nonempty set of naturals has a least element.

    Any bag of counting numbers has a smallest one. (Any bag of fractions doesn't: what's the smallest positive fraction?)

    Suppose \(S \subseteq \mathbb{N}\) has no least element; we show \(S\) is empty. By strong induction: assume every \(k < n\) lies outside \(S\). If \(n\) were in \(S\), it would be its least element — impossible — so \(n \notin S\) too. Hence no natural is in \(S\). Contrapositive: nonempty \(\Rightarrow\) least element exists. \(\blacksquare\)

  • All three are equivalent.

    Induction, strong induction, well-ordering: three costumes, one actor. Prove any one, get the others free.

    The proofs above already chain: induction ⟹ strong induction ⟹ well-ordering. Close the cycle (well-ordering ⟹ induction): given \(P(0)\) and \(P(n) \to P(n+1)\), suppose some natural failed \(P\); the set of failures would have a least element \(m\). Then \(m \neq 0\) (since \(P(0)\)), and \(P(m-1)\) holds (below the least failure), so the step forces \(P(m)\) — contradiction. A cycle of implications makes all three equivalent. \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Proof by induction — base case + inductive step.

    \(1 + 2 + \dots + n = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}\): check \(n = 1\) ✓; then assume it for \(n\) and add \(n+1\) to both sides ✓.

  • Recursive definition — define \(f(0)\), then \(f(n+1)\) from \(f(n)\).

    Factorial: \(0! = 1\), and \((n+1)! = (n+1) \cdot n!\). The definition climbs the same ladder induction does.

Layer 9 · Sizes of infinity

Cardinality

Comparing sets — including infinite ones — without counting.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Equinumerous \(A \approx B\) — a bijection exists.

    Are there as many chairs as guests? Don't count — seat everyone. If no one stands and no chair is empty, the sets match.

  • \(|A| \le |B|\) — an injection \(A \to B\) exists.

    Everyone finds a chair, maybe with chairs left over.

  • Finite vs. infinite — finite = matches \(\{0, \dots, n-1\}\) for some \(n\).

    Your fingers: match them to \(\{0,\dots,9\}\) and you're done. \(\mathbb{N}\) never runs out of matching to do.

  • Countably infinite \((\aleph_0)\) — matches \(\mathbb{N}\).

    You can list the members: 1st, 2nd, 3rd, … Every member eventually gets a position.

  • Countable / uncountable — listable, or too big to list.

    The evens: listable (2, 4, 6, …). The reals: provably not — see the diagonal below.

  • Continuum: \(|\mathbb{R}| = \mathfrak{c} = 2^{\aleph_0}\).

    The size of the number line — a strictly bigger infinity than the counting numbers.

Theorems

  • Cantor–Schröder–Bernstein: injections both ways ⟹ a bijection exists.

    Every guest fits a chair, and every chair fits a guest → a perfect seating must exist, even if neither of your two fittings was it.

    Sketch: given injections \(f : A \to B\) and \(g : B \to A\), trace each element's ancestry backwards (\(g^{-1}\), then \(f^{-1}\), then \(g^{-1}\), …). Every element falls into one of three kinds: the trail dies out in \(A\), dies out in \(B\), or runs forever. Define \(h = f\) on the first and third kinds and \(h = g^{-1}\) on the second. On each kind \(h\) matches perfectly, and the kinds partition both sets — so \(h : A \to B\) is a bijection. \(\blacksquare\)

  • \(\mathbb{Z}\) and \(\mathbb{Q}\) are countable.

    List \(\mathbb{Z}\) as 0, 1, −1, 2, −2, … For fractions, zigzag through a grid of numerators and denominators — every fraction gets a turn.

    \(\mathbb{Z}\): the list 0, 1, −1, 2, −2, … is a bijection with \(\mathbb{N}\) — evens map to \(0, -1, -2, \dots\) and odds to \(1, 2, 3, \dots\), every integer appearing exactly once. \(\mathbb{Q}\): put \(p/q\) (lowest terms) in a grid by numerator and denominator, walk the diagonals, skip repeats — an injection \(\mathbb{Q} \to \mathbb{N}\). With the obvious injection \(\mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{Q}\), Cantor–Schröder–Bernstein gives a bijection. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Countable union of countable sets is countable.

    Infinitely many buses arrive, each with infinitely many passengers — Hilbert's hotel still checks in everyone with a numbered room.

    List the \(i\)-th set's elements as \(a_{i0}, a_{i1}, a_{i2}, \dots\) (picking one enumeration per set uses countable Choice). Walk the grid \(a_{ij}\) diagonal by diagonal (\(i + j = 0, 1, 2, \dots\)): every element receives a finite position, skipping any already seen. That is an enumeration of the union. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Cantor's theorem: \(|A| < |\mathcal{P}(A)|\) — always strictly bigger.

    The collection of all subsets outgrows the set — even when the set is already infinite.

    The map \(x \mapsto \{x\}\) injects \(A\) into \(\mathcal{P}(A)\), so \(|A| \le |\mathcal{P}(A)|\). No \(f : A \to \mathcal{P}(A)\) is onto: let \(D = \{x \in A : x \notin f(x)\}\) (legal by Separation). If \(f(d) = D\) for some \(d\), then \(d \in D \iff d \notin f(d) = D\) — contradiction. So \(D\) is missed by \(f\), and the inequality is strict. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Corollary: infinitely many sizes of infinity.

    \(\mathbb{N}\), then its power set, then its power set, … Each strictly bigger. The ladder never tops out.

    Iterate Cantor's theorem: \(|\mathbb{N}| < |\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})| < |\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N}))| < \cdots\). Each step is strict, and \(<\) chains transitively (compose the injections; a bijection between distant rungs would contradict some step's strictness). So the sizes are pairwise distinct: infinitely many infinities. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Diagonal argument: \(\mathbb{R}\) is uncountable.

    Suppose you listed every decimal. Build a new number differing from list-entry \(n\) at digit \(n\). It disagrees with every entry — your "complete" list missed one. No list can work.

    Suppose \(r_1, r_2, r_3, \dots\) listed every real in \((0,1)\). Build \(x\): let its \(n\)-th decimal digit be 5 if \(r_n\)'s \(n\)-th digit isn't 5, else 6 (avoiding 0 and 9 dodges double representations like \(0.4999\ldots = 0.5\)). Then \(x \in (0,1)\) but \(x\) differs from each \(r_n\) at digit \(n\), so \(x\) is not on the list — contradicting completeness. No list of \((0,1)\) exists, so \(\mathbb{R}\) is uncountable. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Corollary: irrationals are uncountable.

    Rationals are listable, reals aren't — so the overflow must come from the irrationals. "Most" numbers are irrational.

    Suppose the irrationals were countable. Then \(\mathbb{R} = \mathbb{Q} \cup (\mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{Q})\) would be a union of two countable sets — countable by the union theorem — contradicting the diagonal argument. \(\blacksquare\)

Method

  • Diagonalization — build an object that dodges every item on a list.

    The same trick later proves Gödel's theorems and that no program can decide whether all programs halt.

Layer 10 · The controversial axiom

The Axiom of Choice & Friends

One axiom, three famous faces.

Theorem — Equivalences (provable from each other)

  • Axiom of Choice — every family of nonempty sets has a choice function.

    Infinitely many pairs of shoes: easy, "always take the left." Infinitely many pairs of identical socks: no describable rule exists — AC says a full selection exists anyway.

    Here: Well-ordering ⟹ AC (closing the cycle below). Well-order the union \(\bigcup_i A_i\); define the choice function by sending each nonempty \(A_i\) to its least element under that order — a fully explicit rule once the order exists. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Zorn's lemma — if every chain has an upper bound, a maximal element exists.

    A mountain range where every upward path has a top → there must be at least one peak with nothing above it.

    Sketch (AC ⟹ Zorn): suppose every chain has an upper bound but no maximal element exists. Then every element has something strictly above it; a choice function picks one such "next step" for each. Starting anywhere, keep stepping upward — at limit stages, continue from an upper bound of the chain built so far. Transfinite recursion then builds a strictly increasing chain longer than any set can be — contradiction. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Well-ordering theorem — every set can be well-ordered.

    Even \(\mathbb{R}\) can be rearranged so every subset has a "first element" — though provably no one can write that arrangement down.

    Sketch (Zorn ⟹ WO): partially order the well-orderings of subsets of \(A\) by "is an initial segment of". A chain's union is again a well-ordering — an upper bound — so Zorn provides a maximal one. If it missed some \(a \in A\), placing \(a\) on top would extend it, contradicting maximality. So the maximal well-ordering covers all of \(A\). (With the AC ⟹ Zorn and WO ⟹ AC arguments, the cycle closes: all three are equivalent.) \(\blacksquare\)

  • Corollaries across math — every vector space has a basis; Tychonoff's theorem; maximal ideals exist.

    Deep "something exists" facts all over algebra and analysis secretly run on this one axiom.

    Sample (every vector space has a basis): order the linearly independent subsets by inclusion. A chain's union is independent — any finite combination already lives in one link of the chain — so every chain is bounded, and Zorn yields a maximal independent set \(B\). If some vector \(v\) escaped \(\mathrm{span}(B)\), then \(B \cup \{v\}\) would be independent and strictly bigger — contradiction. So \(B\) spans: a basis. The other corollaries follow the same Zorn pattern. \(\blacksquare\)

Layer 11 · The ceiling

Limits of the System

What the tower provably cannot do.

Theorems — Metamathematics

  • Gödel's 1st incompleteness theorem — any consistent system containing arithmetic has true statements it cannot prove.

    A sentence engineered to say "I am not provable in this system." If false, the system proves a falsehood; so it's true — and unprovable.

    Sketch: assign every formula and proof a number (Gödel numbering), so "is provable" becomes an arithmetic predicate the system itself can express. The fixed-point (diagonal) lemma then produces a sentence \(G\) provably equivalent to "\(G\) is not provable". If the system proved \(G\), it would prove a false arithmetic statement — breaking consistency. So \(G\) is unprovable — which is exactly what \(G\) asserts: \(G\) is true but unprovable. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Gödel's 2nd incompleteness theorem — such a system cannot prove its own consistency.

    A witness vouching "I never lie" proves nothing. Math can't certify its own sanity from the inside.

    Sketch: the entire argument of the 1st theorem can be formalized inside the system, yielding a proof of "\(\mathrm{Con} \to G\)" (consistency implies the Gödel sentence). If the system also proved \(\mathrm{Con}\), modus ponens would prove \(G\) — which the 1st theorem forbids. So \(\mathrm{Con}\) is unprovable. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Continuum Hypothesis is independent of ZFC (Gödel 1940 + Cohen 1963).

    "Is there a size of infinity strictly between \(\mathbb{N}\) and \(\mathbb{R}\)?" ZFC shrugs: assuming yes is consistent; assuming no is consistent. The axioms simply don't decide.

    Sketch: Gödel (1940) built the constructible universe \(L\) — a minimal inner model satisfying all of ZFC in which CH is true — so ZFC cannot refute CH. Cohen (1963) invented forcing, enlarging a model with carefully controlled new reals until CH is false — so ZFC cannot prove CH either. Both halves assume only that ZFC itself is consistent. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Corollary — multiple consistent mathematical universes exist.

    Like geometry after the parallel postulate: change the undecided axiom, get a different — equally legal — mathematical world.

    Immediate from independence: starting from any model of ZFC, Gödel's construction yields a model where CH holds and Cohen's yields one where it fails. Both satisfy every ZFC axiom, yet they disagree on CH — two equally legal mathematical universes. \(\blacksquare\)
