The Derivative Is a Matrix

In one variable the derivative is a number. In \(n\) variables it is a linear map — the best flat approximation to the function near a point.

Its matrix is the Jacobian, and \(|\det J|\) is the local volume-scaling factor. That single sentence connects Chapter 5 to the change of variables formula in Chapter 12.

The chapter ends with the inverse and implicit function theorems, which is where Chapter 15's curved surfaces come from.

Layer 1 · One direction at a time

Partial & Directional Derivatives

The obvious first attempt — and why it is not enough.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Partial derivative \(\partial f/\partial x_i\) — differentiate in the \(i\)-th coordinate, holding the rest fixed.

    Walk due east and measure the slope. The other directions are simply ignored.

  • Directional derivative \(D_vf(a) = \lim_{t\to0}\frac{f(a+tv)-f(a)}{t}\).

    The slope along any chosen compass bearing, not just the axes.

  • Gradient \(\nabla f = (\partial_1f,\dots,\partial_nf)\).

    The arrow pointing straight uphill, whose length is the steepest slope.

  • Level set \(\{x : f(x) = c\}\).

    Contour lines on a map. The gradient always crosses them at right angles.

  • Smoothness classes \(C^{0}, C^{1}, C^{k}, C^{\infty}\).

    Continuous; continuously differentiable; \(k\) times; forever. Chapters 15–17 assume \(C^{\infty}\) throughout.

  • Jacobian matrix \(J_f = \bigl(\partial f_i/\partial x_j\bigr)\).

    One row per output, one column per input. For \(m = 1\) it is the gradient, written as a row.

  • Divergence & curl — \(\nabla\cdot F\) and \(\nabla\times F\) in \(\mathbb{R}^{3}\).

    How much a flow spreads out, and how much it spins. Chapter 16 shows both are the exterior derivative in disguise.

  • Laplacian \(\Delta f = \sum_i \partial^2 f/\partial x_i^{2}\).

    How much a value exceeds the average of its neighbours. Heat, waves and gravity all run on it.

Theorems

  • Partial derivatives can all exist while \(f\) is discontinuous.

    \(f(x,y)=\frac{xy}{x^{2}+y^{2}}\) (and 0 at the origin) has both partials at the origin, yet no limit there. Partials alone say almost nothing.

    Along either axis \(f \equiv 0\), so \(\partial_1f(0,0) = \partial_2f(0,0) = 0\) exist. But the path \(y = x\) gives \(f = \tfrac12\), so the limit at the origin fails to exist (Chapter 9, Layer 1) and \(f\) is not continuous there. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Even all directional derivatives existing is not enough.

    Every bearing can have a well-defined slope and the surface still be torn.

    Take \(f(x,y) = \frac{x^{2}y}{x^{4}+y^{2}}\), \(f(0,0)=0\). For \(v = (a,b)\ne0\), \(\frac{f(tv)}{t} = \frac{ta^{2}b}{t^{2}a^{4}+b^{2}}\to 0\) if \(b \ne 0\), and \(\equiv 0\) if \(b=0\); so every directional derivative at the origin is 0. Yet \(f = \tfrac12\) along \(y=x^{2}\), so \(f\) is not even continuous at the origin. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Gradient formula: if \(f\) is differentiable then \(D_vf(a) = \langle\nabla f(a),v\rangle\).

    One vector encodes the slope in every direction at once.

    Differentiability (Layer 2) gives \(f(a+tv) = f(a) + Df(a)(tv) + o(t)\). Dividing by \(t\) and letting \(t\to0\) yields \(D_vf(a) = Df(a)v\), and for \(m=1\) the matrix of \(Df(a)\) is the row \(\nabla f(a)\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Steepest ascent: \(\nabla f\) points in the direction of greatest increase, with rate \(\|\nabla f\|\).

    Water runs downhill along \(-\nabla f\). Every optimisation algorithm starts here.

    For unit \(v\), \(D_vf = \langle\nabla f,v\rangle \le \|\nabla f\|\) by Cauchy–Schwarz (Chapter 6), with equality exactly when \(v\) is the unit vector along \(\nabla f\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Gradients are orthogonal to level sets.

    Walk along a contour and your altitude doesn't change — so the uphill direction must be perpendicular to it.

    Let \(\gamma\) be a differentiable curve inside the level set, so \(f(\gamma(t)) = c\). Differentiating with the chain rule (Layer 2) gives \(\langle\nabla f(\gamma(t)),\gamma'(t)\rangle = 0\), i.e. \(\nabla f\) annihilates every tangent direction of the level set. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Clairaut–Schwarz: if the second partials are continuous, \(\partial_i\partial_jf = \partial_j\partial_if\).

    Mixed partials commute — provided you have continuity. Without it they can genuinely differ.

    Consider the second difference \(\Delta(h,k) = f(a+he_i+ke_j) - f(a+he_i) - f(a+ke_j) + f(a)\). Applying the one-variable mean value theorem twice, in each order, gives \(\Delta = hk\,\partial_j\partial_if(\xi) = hk\,\partial_i\partial_jf(\eta)\) for points \(\xi,\eta\) in the small rectangle. Dividing by \(hk\), letting \(h,k\to0\) and using continuity of both second partials at \(a\) forces the two values to agree. \(\blacksquare\)

  • The hypothesis is necessary: there is an \(f\) with \(\partial_1\partial_2f(0)\ne\partial_2\partial_1f(0)\).

    \(f(x,y)=xy\frac{x^{2}-y^{2}}{x^{2}+y^{2}}\) gives \(+1\) one way and \(-1\) the other.

    With \(f(0,0)=0\), direct computation gives \(\partial_2 f(x,0) = x\) for \(x \ne 0\) and \(\partial_1f(0,y) = -y\) for \(y \ne 0\). Differentiating these once more at the origin yields \(\partial_1\partial_2f(0,0) = 1\) and \(\partial_2\partial_1f(0,0) = -1\). The second partials are not continuous at the origin. \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Differentiate one variable at a time — treat the others as constants.

    Mechanical, and all that partial differentiation ever is.

  • Compute the gradient, then dot with a direction.

    One computation answers every directional-derivative question at that point.

  • Use \(\nabla f \perp\) level set — to find normals and tangent planes.

    The tangent plane to \(f = c\) at \(a\) is \(\langle\nabla f(a), x - a\rangle = 0\).

  • Check continuity of the partials before commuting them.

    Cheap insurance against a genuinely wrong answer.

Layer 2 · The right definition

The Total Derivative

Differentiability as best linear approximation.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Differentiability at \(a\) — there is a linear \(L\) with \(f(a+h) = f(a) + Lh + o(\|h\|)\).

    Zoom in far enough and the graph looks flat. This is the definition that works in every dimension.

  • The derivative \(Df(a)\) — that linear map; its matrix is the Jacobian.

    Not a number, not a vector: a map. Everything else in this chapter follows from taking that seriously.

  • Tangent plane / affine approximation \(x \mapsto f(a) + Df(a)(x-a)\).

    The flat sheet that hugs the surface at \(a\).

  • Jacobian determinant \(\det Df(a)\) — the local volume factor.

    How much a tiny box is stretched near \(a\). Chapter 12's change of variables is exactly this.

  • Continuously differentiable (\(C^{1}\)) — the partials exist and are continuous.

    The condition you actually check in practice, because it implies differentiability.

  • Diffeomorphism — a bijection, differentiable both ways.

    A smooth relabelling of space. Chapters 12 and 15 are written in terms of these.

  • Mean value inequality — the vector replacement for the mean value theorem.

    The exact equality fails for vector-valued functions, so an inequality is used instead.

Theorems

  • The derivative is unique, and differentiable \(\Rightarrow\) continuous.

    Only one linear map can be the best approximation.

    If \(L_1,L_2\) both work, subtracting gives \((L_1-L_2)h = o(\|h\|)\). Taking \(h = tv\) and letting \(t\to0\) gives \((L_1-L_2)v = 0\) for every \(v\). Continuity: \(\|f(a+h)-f(a)\| \le \|Df(a)\|\|h\| + o(\|h\|)\to0\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Differentiable \(\Rightarrow\) all directional derivatives exist, with \(D_vf(a) = Df(a)v\); the converse fails.

    Layer 1 gave the counterexample. Differentiability is genuinely stronger.

    Substituting \(h = tv\) into the definition gives \(\frac{f(a+tv)-f(a)}{t} = Df(a)v + \frac{o(|t|)}{t} \to Df(a)v\). The converse fails by Layer 1's second theorem. \(\blacksquare\)

  • \(C^{1}\Rightarrow\) differentiable.

    The practical criterion: continuous partials are all you ever need to check.

    Move from \(a\) to \(a+h\) one coordinate at a time. On the \(i\)-th step the one-variable mean value theorem gives an increment \(\partial_if(\xi_i)h_i\) for some intermediate \(\xi_i\). Summing, \(f(a+h)-f(a) - \sum_i\partial_if(a)h_i = \sum_i\bigl(\partial_if(\xi_i)-\partial_if(a)\bigr)h_i\), which is \(o(\|h\|)\) by continuity of the partials, since each \(\xi_i \to a\) as \(h\to0\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Linearity: \(D(f+g) = Df + Dg\), \(D(cf) = c\,Df\); and linear maps are their own derivative.

    A flat function is already its own best flat approximation.

    Add the two defining expansions; the error terms are each \(o(\|h\|)\). For a linear \(A\), \(A(a+h) = Aa + Ah\) exactly, with zero remainder, so \(DA(a) = A\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Chain rule: \(D(g\circ f)(a) = Dg(f(a))\circ Df(a)\) — the Jacobians multiply.

    Best flat approximations compose. All those partial-derivative chain formulas are one matrix product.

    Write \(f(a+h) = f(a) + Ah + \varepsilon_1(h)\) with \(A = Df(a)\) and \(\varepsilon_1 = o(\|h\|)\), and \(g(b+k) = g(b)+Bk+\varepsilon_2(k)\) with \(B = Dg(f(a))\). Substituting \(k = Ah+\varepsilon_1(h)\) gives \(g(f(a+h)) = g(f(a)) + BAh + B\varepsilon_1(h) + \varepsilon_2(k)\). Since \(\|k\| = O(\|h\|)\), both error terms are \(o(\|h\|)\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Product and quotient rules follow from the chain rule.

    Multiplication is a bilinear map; differentiate through it and Leibniz appears.

    The map \(m(u,v) = uv\) is bilinear, so \(Dm(u,v)(h,k) = hv + uk\). Applying the chain rule to \(x \mapsto (f(x),g(x)) \mapsto f(x)g(x)\) gives \(D(fg)(a)h = (Df(a)h)g(a) + f(a)(Dg(a)h)\). The quotient rule follows by composing with \(t\mapsto1/t\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Mean value inequality: \(\|f(b)-f(a)\| \le \sup_{[a,b]}\|Df\|\cdot\|b-a\|\) on a segment inside the domain.

    A bound on the derivative is a speed limit: a bounded \(\|Df\|\) makes \(f\) Lipschitz.

    Let \(\gamma(t) = a + t(b-a)\) and \(\varphi(t) = f(\gamma(t))\). Then \(\varphi'(t) = Df(\gamma(t))(b-a)\), so \(\|\varphi'(t)\| \le M\|b-a\|\) with \(M\) the supremum. Hence \(\|f(b)-f(a)\| = \|\int_0^{1}\varphi'(t)\,dt\| \le \int_0^{1}\|\varphi'(t)\|dt \le M\|b-a\|\). (The exact equality of the one-variable mean value theorem fails here: for \(f(t) = (\cos t,\sin t)\) on \([0,2\pi]\) the left side is 0 while \(\|f'\|\equiv1\).) \(\blacksquare\)

  • Zero derivative on a domain \(\Rightarrow\) constant.

    Needs connectedness: on two separate blobs each may take its own value.

    The mean value inequality gives \(f(b) = f(a)\) along any segment inside the domain, and any two points of an open connected set are joined by a polygonal path (Chapter 8, Layer 4). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Jacobian of a composition multiplies determinants: \(\det D(g\circ f) = \det Dg \cdot \det Df\).

    Local volume factors compound — exactly as Chapter 5 said they must.

    Take determinants in the chain rule and apply multiplicativity of the determinant (Chapter 5, Layer 3). \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Check \(C^{1}\), then quote differentiability.

    Computing the \(o(\|h\|)\) limit by hand is almost never necessary.

  • Assemble the Jacobian — one row per component, one column per variable.

    Then every chain rule question becomes matrix multiplication.

  • Use the mean value inequality for estimates.

    The standard way to get Lipschitz bounds — and thus contractions for Layer 4.

  • Disprove differentiability with the definition — show the remainder is not \(o(\|h\|)\).

    Approach along a curve where the candidate approximation fails.

Layer 3 · Finding the best point

Taylor & Optimisation

Second-order information, and constrained extrema.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Hessian \(H_f = (\partial_i\partial_jf)\) — the matrix of second partials.

    The curvature of the graph. Symmetric whenever Clairaut applies.

  • Critical point — where \(\nabla f = \mathbf 0\) (or \(f\) is not differentiable).

    The only interior candidates for a maximum or minimum.

  • Local vs global extremum.

    The top of a hill vs the top of the mountain range. Second derivatives see only the hill.

  • Saddle point — critical, but neither a max nor a min.

    A mountain pass: up in one direction, down in another.

  • Second-order Taylor polynomial \(f(a) + \nabla f(a)\cdot h + \tfrac12 h^{\mathsf T}H_f(a)h\).

    Value, slope and curvature. Everything local optimisation uses.

  • Constraint set \(\{g = 0\}\).

    Optimise the profit, but stay on the budget line.

  • Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda\) — the exchange rate between objective and constraint.

    How much extra profit one more unit of budget would buy.

  • Convex function — \(f(tx+(1-t)y)\le tf(x)+(1-t)f(y)\).

    A bowl. For convex functions every critical point is a global minimum — the reason convex optimisation is easy.

Theorems

  • Fermat's condition: at an interior local extremum of a differentiable \(f\), \(\nabla f = \mathbf 0\).

    At the top of the hill the ground is level in every direction.

    For each direction \(v\), \(t\mapsto f(a+tv)\) has an interior local extremum at \(t=0\), so its derivative \(D_vf(a) = \langle\nabla f(a),v\rangle\) vanishes (Chapter 3, Layer 6). Holding for every \(v\), this forces \(\nabla f(a) = \mathbf 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Taylor's theorem with remainder: \(f(a+h) = f(a)+\nabla f(a)\cdot h + \tfrac12h^{\mathsf T}H_f(\xi)h\) for some \(\xi\) on the segment.

    An exact second-order statement, with an honest error term.

    Apply the one-variable Taylor theorem (Chapter 3, Layer 6) to \(\varphi(t) = f(a+th)\) on \([0,1]\). The chain rule gives \(\varphi'(t) = \nabla f(a+th)\cdot h\) and \(\varphi''(t) = h^{\mathsf T}H_f(a+th)h\), so \(\varphi(1) = \varphi(0)+\varphi'(0)+\tfrac12\varphi''(\theta)\) is exactly the stated formula with \(\xi = a+\theta h\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Second derivative test: at a critical point, \(H_f\) positive definite \(\Rightarrow\) local minimum; negative definite \(\Rightarrow\) local maximum; indefinite \(\Rightarrow\) saddle.

    Chapter 6's classification of quadratic forms, applied to the curvature.

    With \(\nabla f(a)=\mathbf 0\), Taylor gives \(f(a+h)-f(a) = \tfrac12h^{\mathsf T}H_f(\xi)h\). If \(H_f(a)\) is positive definite, its smallest eigenvalue \(\lambda_{\min} > 0\) (Chapter 6, Layer 3), and continuity keeps \(h^{\mathsf T}H_f(\xi)h \ge \tfrac{\lambda_{\min}}{2}\|h\|^{2} > 0\) for small \(h\) — a strict local minimum. The negative definite case is symmetric; if indefinite, two directions give opposite signs, so \(a\) is a saddle. \(\blacksquare\)

  • The test is inconclusive when \(H_f\) is semidefinite.

    \(x^{4}\), \(-x^{4}\) and \(x^{3}\) all have vanishing second derivative at 0 and behave completely differently.

    For \(f(x,y)=x^{4}+y^{4}\), \(g = -(x^{4}+y^{4})\), \(h = x^{4}-y^{4}\), all three have zero gradient and zero Hessian at the origin, yet the origin is a strict minimum, a strict maximum and a saddle respectively. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Convexity criteria: \(f\) is convex iff \(f(y)\ge f(x)+\nabla f(x)\cdot(y-x)\) for all \(x,y\), iff \(H_f \succeq 0\) everywhere.

    The graph lies above every tangent plane.

    Restrict to a segment: \(\varphi(t)=f(x+t(y-x))\) is convex in \(t\) exactly when \(f\) is convex along that segment, and one-variable convexity is equivalent to \(\varphi'' \ge0\) (Chapter 3, Layer 6), i.e. \((y-x)^{\mathsf T}H_f(y-x)\ge0\). The tangent inequality is the one-variable statement that a convex function lies above its tangent line, applied along the segment. \(\blacksquare\)

  • For convex \(f\), every critical point is a global minimum.

    No local traps. This is why convex problems are the ones that get solved reliably.

    The tangent inequality with \(\nabla f(x)=\mathbf 0\) gives \(f(y)\ge f(x)\) for every \(y\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Lagrange multipliers: at a constrained extremum of \(f\) on \(\{g=0\}\) with \(\nabla g \ne \mathbf 0\), there is \(\lambda\) with \(\nabla f = \lambda\nabla g\).

    At the best point on the contour, the objective's contour is tangent to the constraint's — otherwise you could slide along and improve.

    Near such a point the implicit function theorem (Layer 4) parametrises the constraint set by a \(C^{1}\) curve \(\gamma\) through it, for every tangent direction \(v\) with \(\langle\nabla g,v\rangle = 0\). Since \(t\mapsto f(\gamma(t))\) has an extremum at 0, \(\langle\nabla f,v\rangle = 0\). So \(\nabla f\) annihilates the whole tangent hyperplane \(\nabla g^{\perp}\), which forces \(\nabla f\) to be a multiple of \(\nabla g\). With several constraints, the same argument gives \(\nabla f = \sum_i\lambda_i\nabla g_i\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Existence of constrained extrema: a continuous \(f\) on a compact constraint set attains them.

    Check compactness first; the multiplier equations only find candidates.

    Chapter 9, Layer 3's extreme value theorem: the constraint set \(\{g=0\}\) is closed as the preimage of a closed set under a continuous map, so if it is also bounded it is compact. \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Solve \(\nabla f = \mathbf 0\), then classify with the Hessian.

    Eigenvalue signs, or Sylvester's leading-minor test (Chapter 6).

  • Compare interior candidates with the boundary.

    The maximum may well sit on the edge, where \(\nabla f\) need not vanish.

  • Lagrange: solve \(\nabla f = \lambda\nabla g\) together with \(g = 0\).

    \(n+1\) equations in \(n+1\) unknowns. Then compare the values at all solutions.

  • Look for convexity — it turns a local answer into a global one.

    Check \(H_f \succeq 0\) and the problem is essentially solved.

Layer 4 · Solving for the unknown

Inverse & Implicit Function Theorems

When a nonlinear system can be untangled — and why surfaces exist.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Local inverse — an inverse defined only near a point.

    \(\sin\) has no global inverse, but near \(0\) it has \(\arcsin\).

  • Local diffeomorphism — invertible with differentiable inverse, near a point.

    Local, not global: \((r,\theta)\mapsto(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)\) is a local diffeomorphism everywhere off the origin, yet wraps around.

  • Implicit function — \(y = \varphi(x)\) extracted from an equation \(F(x,y)=0\).

    The circle \(x^{2}+y^{2}=1\) locally gives \(y = \pm\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\) — except at the two points where the tangent is vertical.

  • Regular value — a \(c\) at which \(DF\) has full rank on all of \(\{F = c\}\).

    The hypothesis that makes a level set a genuine smooth surface. Chapter 15 builds manifolds from it.

  • Immersion & submersion — \(Df\) injective; \(Df\) surjective.

    Threading a curve into space; projecting space onto a plane.

  • Rank of a map at a point — the rank of its Jacobian there.

    How many independent directions the map actually moves in.

Theorems

  • Inverse function theorem: if \(f\) is \(C^{1}\) near \(a\) and \(\det Df(a) \ne 0\), then \(f\) is a diffeomorphism of a neighbourhood of \(a\) onto a neighbourhood of \(f(a)\), with \(D(f^{-1})(f(a)) = Df(a)^{-1}\).

    If the linear approximation is invertible, so is the function — locally. Non-zero Jacobian determinant is the whole hypothesis.

    Sketch. Normalise so \(a = 0\), \(f(0)=0\) and \(Df(0)=I\) (compose with \(Df(a)^{-1}\)). For fixed \(y\), define \(T_y(x) = x - f(x) + y\); then fixed points of \(T_y\) are exactly solutions of \(f(x)=y\). Since \(DT_y(x) = I - Df(x)\) is small near 0 by continuity of \(Df\), the mean value inequality makes \(T_y\) a contraction of a small closed ball into itself for \(y\) small. Banach's fixed point theorem (Chapter 7, Layer 4) gives a unique solution \(x = g(y)\), so \(f\) is locally bijective. Continuity and then differentiability of \(g\) follow from the same estimates, and differentiating \(f(g(y)) = y\) with the chain rule gives \(Dg = (Df)^{-1}\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Corollary — open mapping: a \(C^{1}\) map with everywhere-invertible derivative sends open sets to open sets.

    Such a map cannot squash a blob onto a curve.

    Each point of an open \(U\) has a neighbourhood mapped onto a neighbourhood of its image, by the inverse function theorem; the union of those images is open and equals \(f(U)\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Implicit function theorem: if \(F(a,b)=0\) and \(\det \partial F/\partial y\,(a,b)\ne0\), then near \(a\) the equation \(F(x,y)=0\) has a unique \(C^{1}\) solution \(y=\varphi(x)\), with \(D\varphi = -\bigl(\partial F/\partial y\bigr)^{-1}\partial F/\partial x\).

    You may solve for \(y\) exactly when the equation is "non-degenerate in \(y\)" — and you get the derivative without ever solving.

    Apply the inverse function theorem to \(\Phi(x,y) = (x, F(x,y))\), whose Jacobian is block triangular with determinant \(\det(\partial F/\partial y) \ne 0\). Its local inverse has the form \((x,z)\mapsto(x,\psi(x,z))\), and \(\varphi(x) := \psi(x,0)\) satisfies \(F(x,\varphi(x))=0\). Differentiating that identity with the chain rule gives \(\partial F/\partial x + (\partial F/\partial y)D\varphi = 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Regular value theorem: if \(c\) is a regular value of \(F : \mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}\), then \(F^{-1}(c)\) is locally the graph of a \(C^{1}\) function — an \((n-k)\)-dimensional surface.

    The sphere \(x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}=1\) is a surface because 1 is a regular value. This is where Chapter 15 begins.

    Full rank \(k\) means some \(k\) columns of \(DF\) are independent; call those variables \(y\) and the rest \(x\). Then \(\partial F/\partial y\) is invertible, and the implicit function theorem writes the level set locally as \(y = \varphi(x)\) with \(x\) ranging over an open subset of \(\mathbb{R}^{n-k}\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Rank theorem: a \(C^{1}\) map of constant rank \(r\) looks, in suitable local coordinates, like \((x_1,\dots,x_n)\mapsto(x_1,\dots,x_r,0,\dots,0)\).

    Choose the right coordinates at both ends and every smooth map becomes a projection. Chapter 4's rank normal form, made local.

    Sketch: after permuting coordinates, the top-left \(r\times r\) block of \(Df\) is invertible. The inverse function theorem straightens the source so that the first \(r\) components of \(f\) become coordinates. Constancy of the rank then forces the remaining components to be independent of the last \(n-r\) variables, and a second change of coordinates in the target kills them. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Local immersion / submersion forms.

    Immersions locally look like \(x \mapsto (x,0)\); submersions like \((x,y)\mapsto x\).

    Both are the rank theorem with \(r = n\) (immersion, so \(Df\) injective) or \(r = m\) (submersion, so \(Df\) surjective); in each case the rank is automatically locally constant since maximal rank is an open condition. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Non-zero Jacobian is not enough for global injectivity.

    \((r,\theta)\mapsto(e^{r}\cos\theta,e^{r}\sin\theta)\) has non-vanishing Jacobian everywhere and wraps the plane around infinitely often.

    Its Jacobian determinant is \(e^{2r} \ne 0\), yet the map takes the same value at \(\theta\) and \(\theta+2\pi\). So local invertibility never upgrades to global invertibility on its own. \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Check \(\det Df \ne 0\) — then quote local invertibility.

    One determinant replaces any attempt to solve the system explicitly.

  • Differentiate the constraint implicitly — get \(D\varphi\) without solving for \(\varphi\).

    From \(x^{2}+y^{2}=1\): \(2x + 2yy' = 0\), so \(y' = -x/y\). No square roots needed.

  • Verify a regular value — to know a level set is a smooth surface.

    Check \(\nabla F \ne \mathbf 0\) at every point of the level set, not merely at one.

  • Set up a contraction — the proof technique behind all of these theorems.

    Existence of solutions to nonlinear equations, every time, from Chapter 7's fixed point theorem.
