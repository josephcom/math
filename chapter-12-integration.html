Adding Up Over a Volume

Riemann chops the domain into strips. Lebesgue chops the range into layers and measures the set where each layer lives.

That one change buys the convergence theorems — limits may be taken inside the integral — and makes integration over \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\) work as smoothly as over an interval.

It also delivers the formula this book has been heading for: \(\int_{T(E)}f = \int_E (f\circ T)\,|\det DT|\).

Layer 1 · Built in three steps

The Lebesgue Integral

Simple, then non-negative, then general.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Integral of a simple function \(\int\sum_ic_i\chi_{E_i} = \sum_ic_i\lambda(E_i)\).

    Height times the measure of the set at that height. Just as with a staircase.

  • Integral of a non-negative function \(\int f = \sup\bigl\{\int s : 0\le s\le f,\ s \text{ simple}\bigr\}\).

    Approach from below with staircases. Always defined, possibly \(+\infty\).

  • Positive and negative parts \(f^{+} = \max(f,0)\), \(f^{-} = \max(-f,0)\).

    Profits and losses, kept separately, then netted.

  • Integrable function — measurable with \(\int|f| < \infty\); then \(\int f = \int f^{+}-\int f^{-}\).

    The Lebesgue integral is absolute by design: \(f\) is integrable exactly when \(|f|\) is.

  • \(L^{1}\) — the integrable functions, with \(\|f\|_1 = \int|f|\).

    Functions identified when they agree a.e., so that \(\|f\|_1 = 0\) really means \(f = 0\).

  • Integral over a set \(\int_E f = \int f\chi_E\).

    Restrict by multiplying by the indicator. No separate theory needed.

  • Improper vs Lebesgue.

    \(\int_0^{\infty}\frac{\sin x}{x}dx\) converges as an improper Riemann integral but not as a Lebesgue one — the positive and negative parts are each infinite.

Theorems

  • The integral of a simple function is well defined and linear on simple functions.

    Different ways of writing the same staircase must give the same area.

    Any two representations have a common refinement into disjoint measurable pieces; on each piece the coefficient is the same in both, and finite additivity of \(\lambda\) makes the sums agree. Linearity follows by refining the partitions of \(s\) and \(t\) to a common one. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Monotonicity: \(f \le g\) implies \(\int f \le \int g\); and \(\bigl|\int f\bigr| \le \int|f|\).

    The bound used in almost every estimate in the rest of the book.

    Every simple \(s \le f\) also satisfies \(s\le g\), so the supremum defining \(\int f\) is over a smaller set. For the second, \(\pm f \le |f|\) gives \(\pm\int f \le \int|f|\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Linearity: \(\int(af+bg) = a\int f + b\int g\) for integrable \(f,g\).

    Obvious for staircases, and everything else is a limit of staircases — but the limit needs the monotone convergence theorem.

    For non-negative \(f, g\), take increasing simple \(s_k\uparrow f\), \(t_k\uparrow g\) (Chapter 11, Layer 5). Then \(s_k+t_k\uparrow f+g\), and monotone convergence (Layer 2) plus linearity on simple functions gives \(\int(f+g) = \lim\int(s_k+t_k) = \int f+\int g\). The general case follows by splitting into positive and negative parts and rearranging. \(\blacksquare\)

  • \(\int f = 0\) for \(f\ge0\) iff \(f = 0\) a.e.; and changing \(f\) on a null set changes nothing.

    Null sets are invisible to the integral — which is exactly why they were worth defining.

    If \(\lambda(\{f>1/k\}) > 0\) for some \(k\), the simple function \(\tfrac1k\chi_{\{f>1/k\}}\) lies under \(f\) and has positive integral. Conversely if \(f = 0\) a.e., every simple \(s \le f\) is 0 a.e., so \(\int s = 0\). The second claim follows by applying this to \(|f-g|\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Chebyshev–Markov inequality: \(\lambda(\{|f| \ge t\}) \le \frac1t\int|f|\).

    A function with a small integral cannot be large on a large set.

    \(t\chi_{\{|f|\ge t\}} \le |f|\) pointwise; integrate and use monotonicity. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Integrable \(\Rightarrow\) finite a.e.

    A function can be infinite somewhere and still integrable — but only on a null set.

    By Chebyshev, \(\lambda(\{|f|\ge t\}) \le \|f\|_1/t \to 0\), and \(\{|f| = \infty\} \subseteq \{|f|\ge t\}\) for every \(t\), so it is null. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Riemann \(\Rightarrow\) Lebesgue: a Riemann-integrable function on \([a,b]\) is Lebesgue integrable with the same value.

    Nothing is lost; a great deal is gained. \(\chi_{\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]}\) now has integral 0.

    Upper and lower Darboux sums are integrals of simple step functions \(u_k \ge f \ge l_k\). Riemann integrability makes \(\int(u_k - l_k)\to0\), so the monotone limits \(u = \lim u_k\) and \(l = \lim l_k\) satisfy \(\int(u-l) = 0\), giving \(u = l = f\) a.e. Hence \(f\) is measurable (Chapter 11's completeness) and its Lebesgue integral is the common limit — the Riemann integral. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Absolute continuity of the integral: for integrable \(f\) and \(\varepsilon > 0\) there is \(\delta\) with \(\int_E|f| < \varepsilon\) whenever \(\lambda(E)<\delta\).

    A little bit of domain contributes only a little bit of integral — however tall the function is.

    Let \(f_k = \min(|f|,k)\uparrow|f|\). By monotone convergence choose \(k\) with \(\int(|f|-f_k)<\varepsilon/2\), then take \(\delta = \varepsilon/(2k)\): on any \(E\) with \(\lambda(E)<\delta\), \(\int_E|f| \le \int(|f|-f_k) + k\lambda(E) < \varepsilon\). \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Prove it for simple functions first, then take monotone limits.

    The standard three-step architecture: simple, non-negative, general.

  • Split into \(f^{+}\) and \(f^{-}\).

    Reduces every general statement to the non-negative case.

  • Chebyshev to convert an integral bound into a measure bound.

    The bridge between "small integral" and "small set".

  • Modify on a null set whenever it makes life easier.

    The integral cannot tell.

Layer 2 · Why Lebesgue won

The Convergence Theorems

When you may move a limit inside an integral.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Monotone sequence of functions \(0\le f_1\le f_2\le\cdots\).

    A rising tide. The simplest situation in which limits behave.

  • Dominating function \(g\) with \(|f_k|\le g\) and \(\int g<\infty\).

    A single integrable ceiling for the whole sequence. This is the hypothesis that does the work.

  • Uniform integrability — the tails of \(\int|f_k|\) are small uniformly in \(k\).

    The sharp condition: it is what dominated convergence really needs.

  • Escape of mass — the failure mode when no dominating function exists.

    A bump of area 1 that slides off to infinity, or gets taller and thinner. The pointwise limit is 0; the integrals are not.

Theorems

  • Monotone convergence theorem: \(0\le f_k\uparrow f\) pointwise implies \(\int f_k \uparrow \int f\).

    A rising tide raises the integral to the limit. The foundation of everything else here.

    Monotonicity gives \(\lim\int f_k \le \int f\). For the reverse, fix a simple \(s \le f\) and \(c\in(0,1)\), and set \(E_k = \{f_k \ge cs\}\), which increase to the whole space. Then \(\int f_k \ge c\int_{E_k}s\), and continuity from below of the measure (Chapter 11, Layer 2) gives \(\int_{E_k}s \to \int s\). So \(\lim\int f_k \ge c\int s\) for every \(c < 1\) and every such \(s\); take suprema. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Corollary — series: \(\int\sum_kf_k = \sum_k\int f_k\) for non-negative \(f_k\) (Beppo Levi).

    Infinite sums of non-negative functions may always be integrated term by term.

    The partial sums increase to the total sum, so monotone convergence applies, and linearity handles each partial sum. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Fatou's lemma: \(\int\liminf f_k \le \liminf\int f_k\) for \(f_k\ge0\).

    Mass can be lost in the limit but never gained. The sliding bump loses all of it.

    Let \(g_N = \inf_{k\ge N}f_k\), so \(g_N \uparrow \liminf f_k\) and \(g_N \le f_k\) for all \(k \ge N\). Monotone convergence gives \(\int\liminf f_k = \lim_N\int g_N\), and \(\int g_N \le \inf_{k\ge N}\int f_k\), whose limit is \(\liminf\int f_k\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Dominated convergence theorem: \(f_k\to f\) a.e. with \(|f_k|\le g\) integrable implies \(\int f_k\to\int f\) and \(\|f_k - f\|_1\to0\).

    The workhorse. With a ceiling in place, limits pass through the integral without comment.

    Apply Fatou to the non-negative sequences \(g + f_k\) and \(g - f_k\): the first gives \(\int g + \int f \le \int g + \liminf\int f_k\), the second \(\int g - \int f \le \int g - \limsup\int f_k\). Cancelling the finite \(\int g\) yields \(\limsup\int f_k \le \int f \le \liminf\int f_k\). Applying the same to \(|f_k - f| \le 2g\) gives \(\|f_k-f\|_1\to0\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Domination is necessary: without it the conclusion fails.

    \(f_k = k\chi_{(0,1/k)}\) tends to 0 pointwise, yet \(\int f_k = 1\) forever.

    Each \(f_k\) is simple with integral \(k\cdot\frac1k = 1\), and \(f_k(x)\to0\) for every \(x > 0\). Any dominating \(g\) would satisfy \(g \ge k\) on \((0,1/k)\), forcing \(g \ge 1/x\), which is not integrable near 0. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Differentiation under the integral sign: if \(|\partial_t f(x,t)| \le g(x)\) integrable, then \(\frac{d}{dt}\int f(x,t)dx = \int\partial_tf(x,t)dx\).

    The everyday application of dominated convergence — and the reason it appears in every physics calculation.

    Write the difference quotient \(\frac{f(x,t+h)-f(x,t)}{h}\), which by the mean value theorem is bounded by \(g(x)\) and converges pointwise to \(\partial_tf(x,t)\) as \(h\to0\). Dominated convergence, applied along any sequence \(h_k\to0\), gives the claim. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Continuity of parameter integrals: if \(f(x,\cdot)\) is continuous and dominated, then \(t\mapsto\int f(x,t)dx\) is continuous.

    Same proof, one derivative less.

    For \(t_k \to t\), \(f(x,t_k)\to f(x,t)\) pointwise with the common bound \(g\); dominated convergence gives convergence of the integrals, which is sequential continuity. \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Find a dominating function — then take the limit inside without further thought.

    Look for a bound independent of \(k\); that is the entire skill.

  • Use monotone convergence for non-negative increasing sequences.

    No domination needed — monotonicity is enough.

  • Fatou when you only need an inequality.

    Often all a proof requires, and it has almost no hypotheses.

  • Integrate a series term by term — legitimate if the terms are non-negative, or if \(\sum\int|f_k|<\infty\).

    The integral version of absolute convergence from Chapter 3.

Layer 3 · One dimension at a time

Product Measures & Fubini

Turning an \(n\)-dimensional integral into \(n\) one-dimensional ones.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Product \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal A\otimes\mathcal B\) — generated by the measurable rectangles.

    Built from bricks, exactly as \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\) is built from boxes.

  • Product measure \(\mu\otimes\nu\) — the unique measure with \((\mu\otimes\nu)(A\times B) = \mu(A)\nu(B)\).

    Area = length \(\times\) length. Lebesgue measure on \(\mathbb{R}^{m+n}\) is the product of those on \(\mathbb{R}^{m}\) and \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\).

  • Section (slice) \(E_x = \{y : (x,y)\in E\}\).

    Cut the solid at height \(x\) and look at the cross-section.

  • Iterated integral \(\int\!\!\left(\int f(x,y)dy\right)dx\).

    Slice, measure each slice, then add up the slices.

  • Cavalieri's principle — equal cross-sections, equal volume.

    A leaning stack of coins has the same volume as a straight one. It falls out of Fubini in one line.

  • Region under a graph \(\{(x,t) : 0 \le t \le f(x)\}\).

    The integral literally is the measure of this set — the picture, made into a theorem.

Theorems

  • Existence and uniqueness of the product measure for \(\sigma\)-finite \(\mu,\nu\).

    Area is determined by what it does to rectangles.

    Define \((\mu\otimes\nu)(E) = \int\nu(E_x)\,d\mu(x)\), after checking that \(x\mapsto\nu(E_x)\) is measurable — true for rectangles, and preserved under the monotone-class operations. Countable additivity follows from monotone convergence. Uniqueness: two such measures agree on the rectangles, which form a \(\pi\)-system generating the product \(\sigma\)-algebra, and \(\sigma\)-finiteness lets the uniqueness theorem of Chapter 11 apply. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Sections are measurable: if \(E\) is product-measurable, every \(E_x\) is measurable.

    You may always slice a measurable solid and get measurable cross-sections.

    The family of sets all of whose sections are measurable contains the rectangles and is closed under complements and countable unions (sections commute with both), hence contains the generated \(\sigma\)-algebra. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Tonelli's theorem: for \(f\ge0\) measurable, both iterated integrals equal \(\int f\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)\).

    For non-negative functions you may slice in either order with no hypotheses at all.

    For \(f = \chi_E\) the statement is the definition of the product measure together with the symmetric computation in the other order (both agree by uniqueness). Linearity extends it to simple functions, and monotone convergence — applied to \(s_k \uparrow f\) from Chapter 11, Layer 5 — extends it to all non-negative measurable \(f\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Fubini's theorem: if \(f\) is integrable, the iterated integrals exist a.e., are integrable, and both equal \(\int f\).

    Integrability is the licence. Check it with Tonelli on \(|f|\) first — always.

    Apply Tonelli to \(|f|\) to get \(\int\!\!\int|f| < \infty\), so for a.e. \(x\) the section \(f(x,\cdot)\) is integrable. Split \(f = f^{+}-f^{-}\), apply Tonelli to each, and subtract — legitimate since both iterated integrals are finite. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Fubini fails without integrability.

    With \(f(x,y) = \frac{x^{2}-y^{2}}{(x^{2}+y^{2})^{2}}\) on \((0,1)^{2}\), the two iterated integrals are \(\pi/4\) and \(-\pi/4\).

    Direct computation: \(\int_0^{1}\!\!\int_0^{1}f\,dy\,dx = \pi/4\) while reversing the order gives \(-\pi/4\), by the antisymmetry \(f(x,y) = -f(y,x)\). Tonelli applied to \(|f|\) gives \(+\infty\), so the hypothesis of Fubini genuinely fails. \(\blacksquare\)

  • The integral is the area under the graph: \(\int f\,d\lambda = (\lambda\otimes\lambda_1)\bigl(\{(x,t) : 0\le t\le f(x)\}\bigr)\).

    The schoolroom picture, promoted to a theorem in \(n\) dimensions.

    The region is measurable (it is \(\{(x,t) : t \ge 0\}\cap\{(x,t) : f(x)-t\ge0\}\), a preimage of closed sets under measurable maps). Tonelli, slicing in \(t\), gives \(\int\lambda_1([0,f(x)])\,dx = \int f(x)\,dx\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Layer-cake formula: \(\int|f| = \int_0^{\infty}\lambda(\{|f|>t\})\,dt\).

    Slice horizontally instead of vertically. This is Lebesgue's idea in a single equation.

    Apply Tonelli to \(\chi_{\{(x,t) : 0<t<|f(x)|\}}\): slicing in \(x\) gives \(\int|f|\), slicing in \(t\) gives \(\int_0^{\infty}\lambda(\{|f|>t\})dt\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Cavalieri's principle: if all parallel cross-sections have equal measure, the solids have equal volume.

    Shearing a stack of coins doesn't change its volume — the same fact Chapter 5 read off the determinant.

    By Tonelli applied to the indicator of each solid, \(\lambda_{n}(E) = \int\lambda_{n-1}(E_x)\,dx\). If the integrands agree for a.e. \(x\), the integrals agree. \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Tonelli on \(|f|\), then Fubini on \(f\) — the standard two-step.

    Never swap the order of integration before checking absolute integrability.

  • Slice in the easy direction — one order is usually far simpler.

    Draw the region first; the right order is then visible.

  • Layer-cake — turn an integral into a measure of level sets.

    Especially powerful for computing \(\int|f|^{p}\) and for probability tails.

  • Count by slicing — Cavalieri for volumes, and the same idea for surface area in Chapter 14.

    Archimedes computed the sphere's volume this way, two millennia early.

Layer 4 · The formula this book was built for

Change of Variables

Bending the coordinates, and paying \(|\det DT|\) for it.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Change of variables — a \(C^{1}\) diffeomorphism \(T : U \to V\).

    A smooth, invertible relabelling of the region. Chapter 10's inverse function theorem guarantees these exist locally.

  • Jacobian factor \(|\det DT(x)|\).

    How much a tiny box at \(x\) is stretched. It varies from point to point, which is the whole difficulty.

  • Polar coordinates \((r,\theta)\), with \(dA = r\,dr\,d\theta\).

    The extra \(r\) is the Jacobian: far from the origin, the same angle sweeps a wider arc.

  • Cylindrical and spherical coordinates, with factors \(r\) and \(\rho^{2}\sin\varphi\).

    The \(\rho^{2}\) is why the surface of a sphere grows as the square of the radius.

  • Generalised spherical coordinates in \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\), with factor \(\rho^{n-1}\).

    One radial direction, \(n-1\) angular ones. Chapter 13 computes the entire volume of the ball from this.

  • Pushforward measure \(T_{*}\mu(E) = \mu(T^{-1}(E))\).

    Carry the measure along with the map. Change of variables says how the density transforms.

Theorems

  • Linear change of variables: \(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(Tx)|\det T|\,dx = \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(y)\,dy\).

    Chapter 11's \(\lambda(T(E)) = |\det T|\lambda(E)\), read as a statement about integrals.

    For \(f = \chi_E\) the statement is exactly \(\lambda(T^{-1}(E))|\det T| = \lambda(E)\), i.e. Chapter 11, Layer 3. Linearity extends it to simple functions, and monotone convergence to all non-negative measurable \(f\); splitting into positive and negative parts finishes. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Change of variables theorem: for a \(C^{1}\) diffeomorphism \(T : U\to V\) and measurable \(f \ge 0\), \[\int_V f(y)\,dy = \int_U f(T(x))\,|\det DT(x)|\,dx.\]

    Every substitution you have ever made, in any number of dimensions, is this one formula.

    Sketch. It suffices to prove \(\lambda(T(E)) = \int_E|\det DT|\) for measurable \(E\), since the integral statement then follows by the simple-function argument above. Cover \(U\) by small cubes. On a cube of side \(\delta\) around \(x_0\), differentiability gives \(T(x) = T(x_0)+DT(x_0)(x-x_0)+o(\delta)\), so \(T\) maps the cube onto a set squeezed between two parallelepipeds whose volumes are \(|\det DT(x_0)|\delta^{n}(1\pm\varepsilon)\), by the linear case. Summing over the cubes, and using uniform continuity of \(DT\) on compact pieces to control \(\varepsilon\), gives the identity in the limit \(\delta\to0\). A covering argument transfers it from cubes to arbitrary measurable sets. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Corollary — polar coordinates: \(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f = \int_0^{\infty}\!\!\int_0^{2\pi}f(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)\,r\,d\theta\,dr\).

    Forget the \(r\) and every answer is wrong — it is the Jacobian, not a decoration.

    \(T(r,\theta) = (r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)\) has \(DT = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-r\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&r\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}\), so \(\det DT = r(\cos^{2}\theta+\sin^{2}\theta) = r\). It is a diffeomorphism from \((0,\infty)\times(0,2\pi)\) onto the plane minus a null set (a ray), which the integral cannot see. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Corollary — spherical coordinates in \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\): \(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f = \int_0^{\infty}\!\!\int_{S^{n-1}}f(\rho\omega)\rho^{\,n-1}\,d\sigma(\omega)\,d\rho\).

    Integrate over shells. The \(\rho^{n-1}\) is the surface area factor — and Chapter 13 lives on this identity.

    Apply the theorem to \(T(\rho,\omega) = \rho\omega\) on \((0,\infty)\times S^{n-1}\), whose Jacobian factorises as \(\rho^{n-1}\) times the surface element, because scaling by \(\rho\) multiplies \((n-1)\)-dimensional area by \(\rho^{n-1}\) and radial displacement contributes the remaining factor 1. Defining \(\sigma\) as the resulting measure on \(S^{n-1}\) makes the identity precise. \(\blacksquare\)

  • The Gaussian integral: \(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-x^{2}}dx = \sqrt\pi\).

    Computed by squaring it and switching to polar — the classic demonstration that two dimensions can be easier than one.

    Let \(I = \int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-x^{2}}dx\). By Tonelli, \(I^{2} = \int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{-(x^{2}+y^{2})}dxdy\), and polar coordinates turn this into \(\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\infty}e^{-r^{2}}r\,dr\,d\theta = 2\pi\cdot\tfrac12 = \pi\). Hence \(I = \sqrt\pi\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Corollary: \(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-\|x\|^{2}}dx = \pi^{n/2}\).

    The identity Chapter 13 uses to extract the volume of the \(n\)-ball.

    The integrand factorises as \(\prod_ie^{-x_i^{2}}\), so Tonelli turns the integral into the product of \(n\) copies of the one-dimensional Gaussian integral. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Substitution is a special case: the one-variable rule \(\int_{g(a)}^{g(b)}f = \int_a^b f(g(x))g'(x)dx\).

    The \(n=1\) case, where \(|\det DT|\) is simply \(|g'|\).

    For a monotone \(C^{1}\) \(g\), the theorem gives \(\int_{g(I)}f = \int_I f(g(x))|g'(x)|dx\). The absolute value disappears once the orientation of the limits is taken into account: reversing the endpoints flips the sign of both sides. \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Choose coordinates matching the symmetry — polar for discs, spherical for balls.

    A circularly symmetric problem in Cartesian coordinates is a self-inflicted wound.

  • Compute the Jacobian, take \(|\det|\) — and never forget it.

    The single most common error in multivariable integration.

  • Ignore null sets when checking bijectivity — a ray or a seam doesn't matter.

    Polar coordinates fail on one ray; the integral cannot tell.

  • Square it and go polar — the Gaussian trick.

    Sometimes the way to do a hard integral is to do a harder-looking one.

Layer 5 · Spaces of functions

\(L^{p}\) Spaces

Chapter 6's norms, applied to functions instead of vectors.

Concepts & Definitions

  • \(L^{p}\) norm \(\|f\|_p = \bigl(\int|f|^{p}\bigr)^{1/p}\), \(1\le p<\infty\).

    The \(p\)-norm of Chapter 6, with the sum replaced by an integral.

  • \(L^{\infty}\) — the essentially bounded functions, with \(\|f\|_\infty = \operatorname{ess\,sup}|f|\).

    The worst value, ignoring null sets.

  • Identification a.e. — \(L^{p}\) consists of equivalence classes.

    Without this, \(\|f\|_p = 0\) wouldn't imply \(f = 0\), and it wouldn't be a norm.

  • Conjugate exponents \(\frac1p+\frac1q=1\).

    2 with 2; 1 with \(\infty\). The same pairing as Chapter 6.

  • Convergence in \(L^{p}\) — \(\|f_k - f\|_p \to 0\).

    Neither implies nor is implied by pointwise convergence — the two are genuinely different.

  • Dense subspaces — simple functions, continuous compactly supported functions, smooth ones.

    You may always assume the function is as nice as you like, then pass to the limit.

  • Convolution \((f*g)(x) = \int f(y)g(x-y)dy\).

    Smearing one function by another. It smooths, and it turns differentiation into multiplication.

Theorems

  • Hölder's inequality: \(\|fg\|_1 \le \|f\|_p\|g\|_q\).

    Cauchy–Schwarz is \(p=q=2\). This is the estimate that appears in every proof below.

    Assume \(\|f\|_p = \|g\|_q = 1\) by scaling. Young's inequality (Chapter 6, Layer 4) gives \(|fg| \le \frac{|f|^{p}}{p}+\frac{|g|^{q}}{q}\) pointwise; integrating gives \(\|fg\|_1 \le \frac1p+\frac1q = 1\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Minkowski's inequality: \(\|f+g\|_p \le \|f\|_p+\|g\|_p\), so \(\|\cdot\|_p\) is a norm.

    The triangle inequality for functions.

    \(\int|f+g|^{p} \le \int|f||f+g|^{p-1}+\int|g||f+g|^{p-1}\), and Hölder with exponents \(p, q = p/(p-1)\) bounds each term by \(\|f\|_p\|f+g\|_p^{p-1}\) and \(\|g\|_p\|f+g\|_p^{p-1}\). Dividing by \(\|f+g\|_p^{p-1}\) gives the result. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Riesz–Fischer: \(L^{p}\) is complete — a Banach space; \(L^{2}\) is a Hilbert space.

    Chapter 7's completeness, for functions. It is what makes Fourier analysis possible.

    Given a Cauchy sequence, extract \(f_{k_j}\) with \(\|f_{k_{j+1}}-f_{k_j}\|_p<2^{-j}\). Then \(g = \sum_j|f_{k_{j+1}}-f_{k_j}|\) has \(\|g\|_p \le 1\) by Minkowski and monotone convergence, so \(g\) is finite a.e. and the telescoping series converges a.e. to some \(f\). Fatou gives \(\|f - f_{k_j}\|_p \to 0\), and a Cauchy sequence with a convergent subsequence converges (Chapter 7, Layer 3). For \(p=2\), \(\langle f,g\rangle = \int fg\) is an inner product inducing \(\|\cdot\|_2\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Corollary: an \(L^{p}\)-convergent sequence has an a.e. convergent subsequence.

    The two notions of convergence are related, but only through a subsequence.

    This is exactly the subsequence constructed in the proof above. The full sequence need not converge a.e. — the sliding-indicator "typewriter" sequence of Chapter 11, Layer 5, converges in every \(L^{p}\) to 0 but nowhere pointwise. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Density: simple functions, and continuous compactly supported functions, are dense in \(L^{p}\) for \(p<\infty\).

    Prove your theorem for nice functions and extend by continuity — the standard manoeuvre.

    Simple functions: approximate \(f^{\pm}\) by increasing simple functions (Chapter 11, Layer 5) and apply dominated convergence to \(|f - s_k|^{p} \le |f|^{p}\). Continuous functions: an indicator \(\chi_E\) with \(\lambda(E)<\infty\) is approximated using regularity — take \(K \subseteq E \subseteq U\) with \(\lambda(U\setminus K)<\varepsilon\) and a continuous function that is 1 on \(K\), 0 outside \(U\) (built from \(x\mapsto d(x,U^{c})\), Chapter 7). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Inclusions on finite measure spaces: \(p < q\) implies \(L^{q}\subseteq L^{p}\); on \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\) neither inclusion holds.

    On \([0,1]\), being square-integrable is stronger than being integrable. On the whole line, decay at infinity and blow-up at a point pull in opposite directions.

    Hölder with exponents \(q/p\) and its conjugate gives \(\int|f|^{p} \le \bigl(\int|f|^{q}\bigr)^{p/q}\lambda(X)^{1-p/q}\), finite when \(\lambda(X)<\infty\). On \(\mathbb{R}\), \(x^{-1/2}\chi_{(0,1)}\) is in \(L^{1}\) but not \(L^{2}\), while \((1+|x|)^{-1}\) is in \(L^{2}\) but not \(L^{1}\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Young's convolution inequality: \(\|f*g\|_r \le \|f\|_p\|g\|_q\) when \(\frac1p+\frac1q = 1+\frac1r\).

    Convolution never makes a function worse — it usually makes it better behaved.

    Sketch: for \(r=\infty\) it is Hölder applied pointwise. For \(r = p\) and \(q=1\), Minkowski's integral inequality gives \(\|f*g\|_p \le \int|g(y)|\|f(\cdot-y)\|_p\,dy = \|f\|_p\|g\|_1\) using translation invariance of \(\lambda\). The general case follows by interpolating between these endpoints. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Riesz representation on \(L^{2}\): every continuous linear functional is \(f\mapsto\int fg\) for a unique \(g\in L^{2}\).

    Chapter 6's finite-dimensional theorem, surviving intact into infinite dimensions — because \(L^{2}\) is complete.

    Sketch: let \(\varphi\) be a continuous functional with kernel \(N\), a closed subspace. If \(N = L^{2}\) take \(g=0\); otherwise pick a unit \(u \in N^{\perp}\) (which exists by the orthogonal decomposition, valid in Hilbert spaces by the best-approximation argument of Chapter 6 plus completeness). Then \(g = \overline{\varphi(u)}\,u\) works, since every \(f\) splits as a multiple of \(u\) plus an element of \(N\). \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Hölder to split a product.

    Choose the exponents to make both factors finite; the choice is the whole art.

  • Prove it on a dense class — then extend by continuity of the norm.

    Continuous compactly supported functions are almost always the class to use.

  • Pass to an a.e. convergent subsequence when \(L^{p}\) convergence isn't enough.

    Then apply Fatou or dominated convergence.

  • Convolve to smooth — mollify a rough function into a smooth one.

    How Chapter 15's partitions of unity and bump functions are manufactured.
