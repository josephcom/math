Length and Angle

A vector space knows about direction and scale. It does not know how long a vector is, or whether two vectors are perpendicular.

One extra piece of structure — the inner product — supplies both, and with them distance: \(d(x,y) = \|x-y\|\).

This is where \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\) becomes Euclidean space. Chapter 7 then throws the inner product away and keeps only the distance.

Layer 1 · The extra structure

Inner Products

One bilinear form, and geometry appears.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Dot product \(\langle x,y\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_iy_i\).

    Quantities times prices, summed: the total bill. That's all the formula is.

  • Inner product — a symmetric, bilinear, positive-definite form on a real vector space.

    Three demands, and length, angle and perpendicularity all follow.

  • Positive definite — \(\langle x,x\rangle > 0\) for \(x \ne \mathbf{0}\).

    Only the zero vector has zero length. Without this you get a "metric" that can't tell points apart.

  • Induced norm \(\|x\| = \sqrt{\langle x,x\rangle}\).

    In \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\): \(\sqrt{x_1^2+\cdots+x_n^2}\) — Pythagoras in \(n\) dimensions.

  • Euclidean distance \(d(x,y) = \|x-y\|\).

    Straight-line distance. Chapter 7 shows it is only one distance among many.

  • Angle \(\cos\theta = \frac{\langle x,y\rangle}{\|x\|\,\|y\|}\).

    The definition is legal only because Cauchy–Schwarz keeps that ratio inside \([-1,1]\).

  • Orthogonal \(x \perp y\) — \(\langle x,y\rangle = 0\).

    At right angles. In \(n\) dimensions "perpendicular" means nothing else.

  • Unit vector — \(\|x\| = 1\).

    Pure direction, no magnitude. Normalise by dividing by the length.

  • Inner product space — a vector space with a chosen inner product.

    \(C[a,b]\) with \(\langle f,g\rangle = \int_a^b fg\): functions can be perpendicular too. That is the whole of Fourier analysis.

  • Gram matrix \(G_{ij} = \langle v_i,v_j\rangle\).

    All the lengths and angles of a list of vectors, in one table.

Axioms — An Inner Product

  • Symmetry — \(\langle x,y\rangle = \langle y,x\rangle\).

    The angle from \(x\) to \(y\) is the angle from \(y\) to \(x\).

  • Bilinearity — linear in each argument.

    Double the quantities and the bill doubles.

  • Positive definiteness — \(\langle x,x\rangle \ge 0\), with equality only at \(\mathbf{0}\).

    Nothing has negative length, and only nothing has zero length.

Theorems

  • Cauchy–Schwarz inequality: \(|\langle x,y\rangle| \le \|x\|\,\|y\|\), with equality iff \(x, y\) are parallel.

    The single most useful inequality in analysis. It is what makes "angle" definable at all.

    If \(y = \mathbf 0\) both sides vanish. Otherwise, for every \(t \in \mathbb{R}\), positive definiteness gives \(0 \le \|x - ty\|^{2} = \|x\|^{2} - 2t\langle x,y\rangle + t^{2}\|y\|^{2}\). This quadratic in \(t\) is never negative, so its discriminant satisfies \(4\langle x,y\rangle^{2} - 4\|x\|^{2}\|y\|^{2} \le 0\). Equality forces a double root \(t_0\), i.e. \(\|x - t_0y\| = 0\), so \(x = t_0y\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Triangle inequality: \(\|x+y\| \le \|x\| + \|y\|\).

    Going via a third point never saves distance. Chapter 7 promotes this to an axiom.

    \(\|x+y\|^{2} = \|x\|^{2} + 2\langle x,y\rangle + \|y\|^{2} \le \|x\|^{2} + 2\|x\|\|y\| + \|y\|^{2} = (\|x\|+\|y\|)^{2}\), using Cauchy–Schwarz. Taking square roots preserves the inequality. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Pythagoras: \(x \perp y \Rightarrow \|x+y\|^{2} = \|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2}\).

    The oldest theorem in mathematics, now a one-line consequence of bilinearity.

    \(\|x+y\|^{2} = \langle x+y,x+y\rangle = \|x\|^{2} + 2\langle x,y\rangle + \|y\|^{2}\), and the middle term vanishes by orthogonality. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Parallelogram law: \(\|x+y\|^{2}+\|x-y\|^{2} = 2\|x\|^{2}+2\|y\|^{2}\).

    The diagonals of a parallelogram know the sides. This identity is exactly what distinguishes norms that come from an inner product.

    Expand both squares by bilinearity: the cross terms \(\pm2\langle x,y\rangle\) cancel, leaving \(2\|x\|^2 + 2\|y\|^2\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Polarisation identity: \(\langle x,y\rangle = \tfrac14\bigl(\|x+y\|^{2}-\|x-y\|^{2}\bigr)\).

    The norm remembers the inner product — so lengths alone determine all the angles.

    Expanding, \(\|x+y\|^2 = \|x\|^2+2\langle x,y\rangle+\|y\|^2\) and \(\|x-y\|^2 = \|x\|^2-2\langle x,y\rangle+\|y\|^2\); subtracting leaves \(4\langle x,y\rangle\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Jordan–von Neumann: a norm comes from an inner product iff it satisfies the parallelogram law.

    The taxicab norm fails it, so no inner product produces taxicab geometry — it has lengths but no angles.

    Sketch: necessity is the parallelogram law above. For sufficiency, define \(\langle x,y\rangle\) by the polarisation formula. Symmetry and \(\langle x,x\rangle = \|x\|^{2}\) are immediate; additivity in the first argument follows from repeated use of the parallelogram law, and homogeneity then follows for integers, rationals, and — by continuity of the norm — all reals. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Gram matrices are positive semidefinite, and \(\det G > 0\) iff the vectors are independent.

    The link to Chapter 5: \(\sqrt{\det G}\) is the volume of the box the vectors span.

    For any coefficient vector \(c\), \(c^{\mathsf T}Gc = \bigl\langle \sum_ic_iv_i, \sum_jc_jv_j\bigr\rangle = \|\sum_i c_iv_i\|^{2}\ge0\). It vanishes for some \(c \ne 0\) exactly when a non-trivial combination is \(\mathbf 0\), i.e. when the \(v_i\) are dependent — which is also when \(G\) is singular. \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Expand \(\|x \pm y\|^{2}\) by bilinearity — the standard opening move.

    Almost every identity in this layer is one expansion away.

  • Apply Cauchy–Schwarz to bound a sum.

    \(\bigl(\sum a_ib_i\bigr)^2 \le \bigl(\sum a_i^2\bigr)\bigl(\sum b_i^2\bigr)\) — used constantly in estimates.

  • Normalise — divide by the length to get a unit vector.

    Separates "which way" from "how far", which usually simplifies the algebra.

Layer 2 · Right angles

Orthogonality & Projection

Splitting a vector into the part you want and the part you don't.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Orthogonal set — pairwise perpendicular non-zero vectors.

    Axes that don't lean on each other. Coordinates in such a system are read off by dot products.

  • Orthonormal basis — an orthogonal basis of unit vectors.

    The standard basis of \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\), and every rotation of it.

  • Orthogonal complement \(U^{\perp} = \{v : v \perp u \ \forall u \in U\}\).

    Everything perpendicular to the floor is the vertical axis.

  • Orthogonal projection \(\operatorname{proj}_U(v)\) — the nearest point of \(U\) to \(v\).

    The shadow cast straight down onto the floor.

  • Gram–Schmidt process — turning any basis into an orthonormal one.

    Straighten each new axis by subtracting off its shadow on the ones already straightened.

  • QR factorisation \(A = QR\) — Gram–Schmidt, written as matrices.

    \(Q\) holds the straightened directions, \(R\) records the bookkeeping.

  • Least squares solution — minimises \(\|Ax - b\|\) when \(Ax=b\) has no solution.

    Fitting a line through scattered data: no line hits every point, so take the closest.

  • Fourier coefficients \(\langle v, e_i\rangle\) with respect to an orthonormal set.

    How much of each pure tone is in the chord.

Theorems

  • Orthogonal sets are independent.

    Perpendicular directions can never be mixtures of one another.

    If \(\sum_i c_iv_i = \mathbf 0\), take the inner product with \(v_j\): all cross terms vanish, leaving \(c_j\|v_j\|^{2} = 0\), and \(v_j \ne \mathbf 0\) forces \(c_j = 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Coordinates in an orthonormal basis: \(v = \sum_i \langle v,e_i\rangle e_i\), and \(\|v\|^{2} = \sum_i\langle v,e_i\rangle^{2}\) (Parseval).

    No linear system to solve — each coordinate is a single dot product.

    Write \(v = \sum_j c_je_j\) (possible since the \(e_j\) form a basis) and pair with \(e_i\): orthonormality leaves \(\langle v,e_i\rangle = c_i\). Then \(\|v\|^{2} = \langle\sum_ic_ie_i,\sum_jc_je_j\rangle = \sum_i c_i^{2}\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Gram–Schmidt works: every finite-dimensional inner product space has an orthonormal basis.

    Perpendicular axes always exist — you never have to work in a skewed frame.

    Given a basis \(v_1,\dots,v_n\), set \(u_1 = v_1\) and inductively \(u_k = v_k - \sum_{i<k}\frac{\langle v_k,u_i\rangle}{\|u_i\|^{2}}u_i\). Pairing with \(u_j\) for \(j < k\) gives 0, so the \(u_k\) are orthogonal; \(u_k \ne \mathbf 0\) because \(v_k\) is not in the span of the earlier vectors. At each stage \(\operatorname{span}(u_1,\dots,u_k) = \operatorname{span}(v_1,\dots,v_k)\), so normalising gives an orthonormal basis. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Orthogonal decomposition: \(V = U \oplus U^{\perp}\) for any subspace \(U\).

    Every vector is uniquely "in the floor" plus "straight up".

    Take an orthonormal basis \(e_1,\dots,e_k\) of \(U\) and set \(p = \sum_i\langle v,e_i\rangle e_i\). Then \(v - p \perp e_j\) for each \(j\) — check by pairing — so \(v - p \in U^{\perp}\) and \(v = p + (v-p)\). If \(x \in U\cap U^{\perp}\) then \(\langle x,x\rangle = 0\), so \(x = \mathbf 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Projection formula and idempotence: \(\operatorname{proj}_U(v) = \sum_i\langle v,e_i\rangle e_i\), and \(\operatorname{proj}_U^{2} = \operatorname{proj}_U\).

    Casting a shadow twice changes nothing — a shadow is already flat.

    The formula is the \(p\) constructed above. For a vector already in \(U\), expanding in the orthonormal basis returns it unchanged, so applying the projection twice equals applying it once. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Best approximation: \(\operatorname{proj}_U(v)\) is the unique closest point of \(U\) to \(v\).

    The shortest way to the floor is straight down — the reason least squares works.

    For any \(u \in U\), write \(v - u = (v - p) + (p - u)\), where the first term is in \(U^{\perp}\) and the second in \(U\). Pythagoras gives \(\|v-u\|^{2} = \|v-p\|^{2} + \|p-u\|^{2} \ge \|v-p\|^{2}\), with equality only when \(u = p\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Bessel's inequality: \(\sum_i\langle v,e_i\rangle^{2} \le \|v\|^{2}\) for any orthonormal set.

    The parts you can see never outweigh the whole. Equality exactly when the set is complete.

    Let \(p\) be the projection onto the span of the \(e_i\). Then \(\|v\|^{2} = \|p\|^{2} + \|v-p\|^{2} \ge \|p\|^{2} = \sum_i\langle v,e_i\rangle^{2}\) by Parseval applied inside that span. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Normal equations: \(x\) minimises \(\|Ax-b\|\) iff \(A^{\mathsf T}Ax = A^{\mathsf T}b\).

    Line of best fit, in one formula. The residual must be perpendicular to every column.

    By best approximation, \(Ax\) must be the projection of \(b\) onto the column space, i.e. \(b - Ax \perp \operatorname{im}A\). That says \(\langle a^{(j)}, b - Ax\rangle = 0\) for every column, which in matrix form is \(A^{\mathsf T}(b-Ax) = \mathbf 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • \(\dim U + \dim U^{\perp} = \dim V\), and \((U^{\perp})^{\perp} = U\).

    The perpendicular of the perpendicular is where you started.

    The dimension count is immediate from \(V = U\oplus U^{\perp}\) (Chapter 4, Layer 2). Clearly \(U \subseteq (U^{\perp})^{\perp}\), and both have dimension \(\dim V - \dim U^{\perp} = \dim U\), so they coincide. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Riesz representation (finite-dimensional): every linear functional is \(v\mapsto\langle v,w\rangle\) for a unique \(w\).

    Chapter 4's dual space becomes a copy of \(V\) itself — but only after you choose an inner product.

    Given \(f \in V^{*}\), fix an orthonormal basis and put \(w = \sum_i f(e_i)e_i\). Then \(\langle e_j, w\rangle = f(e_j)\) for each \(j\), so \(f\) and \(\langle\cdot,w\rangle\) agree on a basis, hence everywhere. Uniqueness: if \(\langle v,w\rangle = \langle v,w'\rangle\) for all \(v\), take \(v = w - w'\) to get \(\|w-w'\|^{2}=0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Gram–Schmidt — subtract shadows to straighten a basis.

    Do it in order, normalise at the end, and check orthogonality as you go.

  • Project to approximate — replace an unsolvable equation by the nearest solvable one.

    Data fitting, signal compression and regression are all this one move.

  • Split \(v = p + (v-p)\) — the standard orthogonal decomposition trick.

    Then apply Pythagoras. Half the proofs above are exactly this.

  • Use orthonormal coordinates — they turn every computation into dot products.

    No matrix inversion, no linear systems.

Layer 3 · Maps that respect length

Orthogonal & Symmetric Maps

Rigid motions, quadratic forms, and the spectral theorem.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Orthogonal matrix — \(Q^{\mathsf T}Q = I\); its columns are orthonormal.

    Rotations and reflections: the maps that don't distort anything.

  • Isometry — a map preserving distances.

    Picking up a rigid object and putting it down somewhere else.

  • Orthogonal group \(O(n)\), and \(SO(n)\) with \(\det = 1\).

    \(SO(3)\) is exactly the set of physical rotations of an object in space.

  • Adjoint \(T^{*}\) — defined by \(\langle Tx,y\rangle = \langle x, T^{*}y\rangle\); in coordinates, the transpose.

    Move the map to the other side of the dot product and it becomes its transpose.

  • Symmetric (self-adjoint) map — \(T = T^{*}\), i.e. \(A = A^{\mathsf T}\).

    Covariance matrices, Hessians, and inertia tensors are all symmetric. They behave impeccably.

  • Quadratic form \(q(x) = \langle Ax,x\rangle = x^{\mathsf T}Ax\).

    Energy, variance, squared distance — every "sum of squares with cross terms" is one.

  • Positive definite / semidefinite / indefinite.

    A bowl, a trough, or a saddle. Chapter 10's second-derivative test is this classification.

  • Principal axes — the eigenvector directions of a symmetric matrix.

    The natural axes of an ellipse. Turn your head to them and the cross terms vanish.

  • Singular value decomposition \(A = U\Sigma V^{\mathsf T}\).

    Every linear map is: rotate, stretch along axes, rotate again. No exceptions.

Theorems

  • Isometry \(\iff\) orthogonal: a linear map preserves norms iff it preserves inner products iff its matrix is orthogonal.

    Preserve all lengths and you automatically preserve all angles — polarisation does the work.

    If \(\langle Qx,Qy\rangle = \langle x,y\rangle\) then taking \(y = x\) preserves norms. Conversely, preserving norms plus the polarisation identity (Layer 1) recovers the inner product. And \(\langle Qx,Qy\rangle = \langle Q^{\mathsf T}Qx, y\rangle\) equals \(\langle x,y\rangle\) for all \(x,y\) exactly when \(Q^{\mathsf T}Q = I\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • \(\det Q = \pm1\) for orthogonal \(Q\); \(+1\) rotations, \(-1\) reflections.

    Rigid motions preserve volume — of course they do. The sign records handedness.

    \(1 = \det I = \det(Q^{\mathsf T}Q) = \det(Q^{\mathsf T})\det Q = (\det Q)^{2}\) by Chapter 5, so \(\det Q = \pm1\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Symmetric matrices have real eigenvalues.

    No rotation hides inside a symmetric matrix; every direction is either stretched or squashed.

    Let \(Av = \lambda v\) with \(v \ne \mathbf 0\), allowing complex entries, and let \(\bar v\) denote the conjugate. Then \(\bar v^{\mathsf T}Av = \lambda\,\bar v^{\mathsf T}v\), while transposing and conjugating the same scalar gives \(\bar\lambda\,\bar v^{\mathsf T}v\) using \(A = A^{\mathsf T}\) with real entries. Since \(\bar v^{\mathsf T}v = \sum|v_i|^{2} > 0\), we get \(\lambda = \bar\lambda\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Eigenvectors of a symmetric matrix for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.

    The principal axes of an ellipse meet at right angles — always.

    If \(Av = \lambda v\) and \(Aw = \mu w\) with \(\lambda \ne \mu\), then \(\lambda\langle v,w\rangle = \langle Av,w\rangle = \langle v,Aw\rangle = \mu\langle v,w\rangle\), so \((\lambda-\mu)\langle v,w\rangle = 0\) and hence \(\langle v,w\rangle = 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Spectral theorem: every real symmetric matrix is \(Q D Q^{\mathsf T}\) with \(Q\) orthogonal and \(D\) diagonal.

    Turn your head to the principal axes and the map becomes pure stretching. The most useful theorem in applied mathematics.

    Induct on \(n\). A real eigenvalue \(\lambda_1\) exists with a unit eigenvector \(v_1\). The subspace \(v_1^{\perp}\) is invariant: for \(w \perp v_1\), \(\langle Aw, v_1\rangle = \langle w, Av_1\rangle = \lambda_1\langle w,v_1\rangle = 0\). The restriction of \(A\) to \(v_1^{\perp}\) is again symmetric, so the induction hypothesis provides an orthonormal eigenbasis there; adjoining \(v_1\) gives one for \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\), and its matrix is diagonal. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Principal axis theorem: every quadratic form is \(\sum_i\lambda_i y_i^{2}\) in suitable orthonormal coordinates.

    Every conic is an ellipse, hyperbola or parabola once you rotate the axes properly.

    Write \(q(x) = x^{\mathsf T}Ax\) with \(A\) symmetric (replace \(A\) by \(\tfrac12(A+A^{\mathsf T})\), which gives the same form). By the spectral theorem \(A = QDQ^{\mathsf T}\); substituting \(y = Q^{\mathsf T}x\) gives \(q = y^{\mathsf T}Dy = \sum_i\lambda_iy_i^{2}\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Definiteness test: \(q\) is positive definite iff all eigenvalues are positive, iff all leading principal minors are positive (Sylvester).

    Two ways to check a bowl: look at the stretch factors, or at a chain of determinants.

    In principal axes \(q = \sum\lambda_iy_i^{2}\), which is positive for \(y\ne0\) exactly when all \(\lambda_i > 0\). For Sylvester's criterion, induct on \(n\): positive definiteness restricts to the leading \(k\times k\) block, whose determinant is a product of positive eigenvalues; conversely positive leading minors force, by induction and the determinant being the product of eigenvalues, that no eigenvalue can be \(\le 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Rayleigh quotient: \(\lambda_{\min} \le \frac{\langle Ax,x\rangle}{\|x\|^{2}} \le \lambda_{\max}\), both attained.

    The extreme stretch factors are the extreme values of the form on the unit sphere.

    In principal axes with \(\|y\| = 1\), \(\langle Ax,x\rangle = \sum\lambda_iy_i^{2}\), a weighted average of the \(\lambda_i\) with weights summing to 1. Such an average lies between the smallest and largest, and equals them at the corresponding eigenvectors. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Singular value decomposition: every \(m\times n\) real matrix factors as \(U\Sigma V^{\mathsf T}\) with \(U, V\) orthogonal and \(\Sigma\) diagonal with non-negative entries.

    Rotate, stretch, rotate. The singular values say how much each direction is stretched — and \(\prod\sigma_i = |\det A|\) for square \(A\).

    \(A^{\mathsf T}A\) is symmetric and positive semidefinite, so the spectral theorem gives an orthonormal eigenbasis \(v_i\) with eigenvalues \(\sigma_i^{2}\ge0\). For \(\sigma_i > 0\) put \(u_i = Av_i/\sigma_i\); these are orthonormal since \(\langle u_i,u_j\rangle = \langle Av_i,Av_j\rangle/(\sigma_i\sigma_j) = \langle A^{\mathsf T}Av_i, v_j\rangle/(\sigma_i\sigma_j) = \delta_{ij}\). Extend to an orthonormal basis of \(\mathbb{R}^{m}\); then \(Av_i = \sigma_iu_i\) reads exactly as \(A = U\Sigma V^{\mathsf T}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Diagonalise a symmetric matrix orthogonally — find eigenvalues, then orthonormal eigenvectors.

    Within one eigenspace, run Gram–Schmidt; across eigenspaces, orthogonality is automatic.

  • Complete the square / rotate to principal axes — remove cross terms from a quadratic form.

    What "the equation of an ellipse" looks like once you stop fighting the coordinates.

  • Test definiteness — eigenvalue signs or leading minors.

    Chapter 10 uses this on the Hessian to classify critical points.

  • Use the SVD — for rank, pseudo-inverse, condition number and low-rank approximation.

    Keeping the largest few singular values is exactly how image compression works.

Layer 4 · Other rulers

Norms & Unit Balls

Length without angle — and why, in finite dimensions, the choice hardly matters.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Norm — \(\|x\| \ge 0\) with equality only at \(\mathbf 0\); \(\|cx\| = |c|\|x\|\); \(\|x+y\|\le\|x\|+\|y\|\).

    Three rules for "size". No inner product required — so no angles come with it.

  • Euclidean norm \(\|x\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum x_i^{2}}\).

    As the crow flies.

  • Taxicab norm \(\|x\|_1 = \sum|x_i|\).

    Distance through a city grid: you can't cut across the blocks.

  • Maximum norm \(\|x\|_\infty = \max_i|x_i|\).

    The worst single error, ignoring the rest. A king's move on a chessboard.

  • \(p\)-norm \(\|x\|_p = \bigl(\sum|x_i|^{p}\bigr)^{1/p}\), \(p \ge 1\).

    A dial running from taxicab (\(p=1\)) through Euclidean (\(p=2\)) to maximum (\(p=\infty\)).

  • Unit ball \(B = \{x : \|x\|\le1\}\).

    A round disc for \(p=2\), a diamond for \(p=1\), a square for \(p=\infty\). The ball is the norm.

  • Equivalent norms — \(c\|x\|_a \le \|x\|_b \le C\|x\|_a\).

    Different rulers, same verdict on which sequences converge.

  • Operator norm \(\|A\| = \sup_{\|x\|=1}\|Ax\|\).

    The worst stretching the map can do. Chapters 9 and 10 use it constantly.

  • Conjugate exponents — \(\frac1p + \frac1q = 1\).

    2 pairs with 2; 1 pairs with \(\infty\). The pairing behind Hölder's inequality.

Theorems

  • Young's inequality: \(ab \le \frac{a^{p}}{p} + \frac{b^{q}}{q}\) for \(a,b\ge0\) and conjugate \(p,q\).

    The workhorse lemma. At \(p=q=2\) it is just \(ab \le \frac{a^2+b^2}{2}\).

    The logarithm is concave (its second derivative is \(-1/x^{2} < 0\)), so \(\ln\bigl(\tfrac1p a^{p} + \tfrac1q b^{q}\bigr) \ge \tfrac1p\ln a^{p} + \tfrac1q \ln b^{q} = \ln(ab)\). Exponentiating gives the inequality; equality holds iff \(a^{p} = b^{q}\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Hölder's inequality: \(\sum|x_iy_i| \le \|x\|_p\|y\|_q\).

    Cauchy–Schwarz is the case \(p=q=2\). This is its full family.

    Normalise so \(\|x\|_p = \|y\|_q = 1\). Apply Young to each pair: \(|x_iy_i| \le \frac{|x_i|^{p}}{p} + \frac{|y_i|^{q}}{q}\). Summing gives \(\sum|x_iy_i| \le \frac1p + \frac1q = 1 = \|x\|_p\|y\|_q\). Scaling restores the general case. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Minkowski's inequality: \(\|x+y\|_p \le \|x\|_p + \|y\|_p\) — so \(\|\cdot\|_p\) is a norm.

    The triangle inequality holds for every \(p \ge 1\). For \(p < 1\) it fails, which is why \(p\ge1\) is required.

    Write \(\sum|x_i+y_i|^{p} \le \sum|x_i||x_i+y_i|^{p-1} + \sum|y_i||x_i+y_i|^{p-1}\), and apply Hölder to each sum with exponents \(p\) and \(q = p/(p-1)\). This gives \(\|x+y\|_p^{p}\le(\|x\|_p+\|y\|_p)\|x+y\|_p^{p-1}\); dividing by the last factor finishes. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Norm comparisons in \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\): \(\|x\|_\infty \le \|x\|_2 \le \|x\|_1 \le n\|x\|_\infty\), and \(\|x\|_2 \le \sqrt n\,\|x\|_\infty\).

    The constants grow with \(n\) — a warning that "equivalent" is not "interchangeable" once \(n\) is large. Chapter 13 collects the consequences.

    \(\|x\|_\infty^{2} = \max_i x_i^{2}\le\sum_i x_i^{2}\) gives the first. \(\|x\|_2^{2} = \sum x_i^2 \le (\sum|x_i|)^{2}\) since cross terms are non-negative, giving the second. Each \(|x_i|\le\|x\|_\infty\) summed \(n\) times gives the third, and \(\sum x_i^{2}\le n\|x\|^2_\infty\) the fourth. \(\blacksquare\)

  • All norms on a finite-dimensional space are equivalent.

    In \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\), "converges", "bounded", "open" and "continuous" mean the same thing whichever ruler you pick. In infinite dimensions this collapses.

    Fix a basis and compare an arbitrary norm \(N\) with \(\|\cdot\|_\infty\). Writing \(x = \sum x_ie_i\), the triangle inequality gives \(N(x) \le (\sum_iN(e_i))\|x\|_\infty =: C\|x\|_\infty\), so \(N\) is continuous with respect to \(\|\cdot\|_\infty\). The \(\|\cdot\|_\infty\)-unit sphere is closed and bounded, hence compact (Chapter 8's Heine–Borel), so the continuous positive function \(N\) attains a minimum \(c > 0\) on it. Homogeneity extends \(c\|x\|_\infty \le N(x)\le C\|x\|_\infty\) to all \(x\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Unit balls are convex and symmetric, and every such body with non-empty interior is the unit ball of a norm.

    Norms and centrally symmetric convex bodies are the same thing seen twice. Chapter 19 exploits this constantly.

    Convexity: if \(\|x\|,\|y\|\le1\) and \(t\in[0,1]\), then \(\|tx+(1-t)y\| \le t\|x\|+(1-t)\|y\| \le 1\). Symmetry is \(\|-x\|=\|x\|\). Conversely, given such a body \(K\), define the Minkowski functional \(\|x\|_K = \inf\{t > 0 : x/t \in K\}\); homogeneity is immediate, the triangle inequality follows from convexity, and definiteness from \(K\) being bounded with \(\mathbf 0\) in its interior. \(\blacksquare\)

  • The operator norm is a norm, and \(\|AB\| \le \|A\|\|B\|\), \(\|Ax\| \le \|A\|\|x\|\).

    Submultiplicativity: worst-case stretches compound. It makes matrix estimates behave like number estimates.

    The norm axioms follow from those of the target norm applied pointwise. For \(x\ne\mathbf 0\), \(\|Ax\| = \|x\|\,\bigl\|A\tfrac{x}{\|x\|}\bigr\| \le \|A\|\|x\|\). Then \(\|ABx\| \le \|A\|\|Bx\| \le \|A\|\|B\|\|x\|\), and taking the supremum over unit \(x\) gives the product bound. \(\blacksquare\)

  • For the Euclidean norm, \(\|A\| = \sigma_{\max}\), the largest singular value.

    The SVD reads the answer straight off — the biggest stretch factor is the worst case, by construction.

    \(\|Ax\|^{2} = \langle A^{\mathsf T}Ax,x\rangle\), and by the Rayleigh quotient (Layer 3) this is maximised over unit \(x\) at the largest eigenvalue of \(A^{\mathsf T}A\), namely \(\sigma_{\max}^{2}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Choose the convenient norm — in finite dimensions they are equivalent, so pick the easiest.

    \(\|\cdot\|_\infty\) for coordinatewise arguments, \(\|\cdot\|_2\) for geometry, \(\|\cdot\|_1\) for sums.

  • Hölder to split a sum — separate two factors into two norms.

    The discrete rehearsal for the same inequality on integrals in Chapter 12.

  • Draw the unit ball — the fastest way to understand an unfamiliar norm.

    Diamond, disc, square: you can see immediately which vectors it considers small.

  • Bound with the operator norm — turn \(\|Ax\|\) into \(\|A\|\|x\|\).

    Used in every estimate of Chapters 9 and 10.
