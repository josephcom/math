Where \(n\) Dimensions Begin

\(\mathbb{R}^{n}\) is Chapter 2's Cartesian product with Chapter 3's arithmetic applied coordinate by coordinate. That is all a dimension is, to start with.

This layer supplies direction and scale — but not yet length, angle or volume. Those arrive in Chapters 5 and 6.

Prerequisites: Chapters 1–3. Fields and induction are used freely; the Axiom of Choice appears once, for infinite bases.

Layer 1 · The object

Vector Spaces & Subspaces

Things you can add together and scale — and nothing more.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Vector space over \(\mathbb{R}\) — a set \(V\) with addition and scalar multiplication obeying eight axioms.

    Arrows you can lay end to end and stretch. Nothing about length or angle is assumed yet.

  • \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\) — ordered \(n\)-tuples, added and scaled coordinate by coordinate.

    \((1,2,3) + (4,0,1) = (5,2,4)\). The whole of multi-dimensional space, defined in one line.

  • Vector & scalar — an element of \(V\); an element of the field.

    A displacement, and a number that stretches it.

  • Zero vector \(\mathbf{0}\) — the additive identity.

    Standing still. Every subspace must contain it — the fastest test for "not a subspace".

  • Subspace — a non-empty subset closed under addition and scalar multiplication.

    A line or plane through the origin inside \(\mathbb{R}^3\). Not through the origin? Not a subspace.

  • Linear combination \(c_1v_1 + \cdots + c_kv_k\).

    A recipe: two parts east, three parts north.

  • Affine subspace — a subspace shifted by a fixed vector.

    A road that doesn't pass through town centre. Solutions of \(Ax=b\) with \(b \ne 0\) look like this.

  • Function spaces — \(C[a,b]\), polynomials, sequences: all vector spaces.

    Add two temperature curves and you get a curve. The theory doesn't care that the "vectors" are functions.

  • Sum of subspaces \(U + W = \{u + w\}\).

    Two lines through the origin sum to the plane they span.

  • Direct sum \(U \oplus W\) — a sum in which \(U \cap W = \{\mathbf{0}\}\).

    Every vector splits into a horizontal and a vertical piece, in exactly one way.

Axioms — A Vector Space

  • Additive group — \(+\) is associative and commutative, with a zero and negatives.

    Walk the displacements in any order and you end up at the same place.

  • Compatibility of scalars — \(a(bv) = (ab)v\) and \(1v = v\).

    Doubling then tripling is sextupling; scaling by 1 does nothing.

  • Two distributive laws — \(a(u+v) = au+av\) and \((a+b)v = av+bv\).

    Scaling a whole journey scales each leg; adding two stretches is one bigger stretch.

Theorems

  • \(0v = \mathbf{0}\) and \((-1)v = -v\), and \(a\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}\).

    The obvious facts are consequences, not extra axioms.

    \(0v = (0+0)v = 0v + 0v\); adding \(-(0v)\) gives \(\mathbf{0} = 0v\). Then \(v + (-1)v = (1 + (-1))v = 0v = \mathbf{0}\), so \((-1)v\) is the additive inverse of \(v\). Likewise \(a\mathbf{0} = a(\mathbf{0}+\mathbf{0}) = a\mathbf{0} + a\mathbf{0}\) forces \(a\mathbf{0}=\mathbf{0}\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Subspace test: a non-empty \(U \subseteq V\) is a subspace iff \(au + bw \in U\) for all \(a,b\in\mathbb{R}\), \(u,w\in U\).

    One line to check instead of eight axioms — they're inherited from \(V\).

    A subspace clearly satisfies the condition. Conversely, taking \(a=b=0\) gives \(\mathbf{0}\in U\) (using non-emptiness), \(b=0\) gives closure under scaling, and \(a=b=1\) gives closure under addition. All other axioms hold in \(U\) because they hold in \(V\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Intersections of subspaces are subspaces — unions generally are not.

    Two lines through the origin: their union is not closed under addition, since a sum leaves both lines.

    If \(u, w \in \bigcap_i U_i\) then \(au+bw \in U_i\) for each \(i\), hence in the intersection, and \(\mathbf{0}\) lies in every \(U_i\). For unions, take \(u \in U_1\setminus U_2\) and \(w \in U_2 \setminus U_1\): if \(u + w\) lay in \(U_1\) then \(w = (u+w)-u \in U_1\), and symmetrically — so \(u+w\) is in neither. \(\blacksquare\)

  • \(U + W\) is the smallest subspace containing both.

    The union isn't a subspace, so you take the smallest one that repairs it.

    \(U+W\) is closed under linear combinations, since \(a(u_1+w_1)+b(u_2+w_2) = (au_1+bu_2)+(aw_1+bw_2)\), and it contains \(U\) (take \(w=\mathbf{0}\)) and \(W\). Any subspace containing both must contain every \(u+w\), hence contains \(U+W\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Direct sum criterion: \(V = U \oplus W\) iff every \(v\) is uniquely \(u + w\).

    Unique decomposition is exactly what "the two pieces don't overlap" means.

    If \(U\cap W = \{\mathbf{0}\}\) and \(u_1+w_1 = u_2+w_2\), then \(u_1 - u_2 = w_2 - w_1 \in U \cap W = \{\mathbf{0}\}\), forcing equality. Conversely, if some \(x \ne \mathbf{0}\) lay in \(U\cap W\), then \(\mathbf{0} = x + (-x)\) would give two different decompositions of \(\mathbf{0}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Check \(\mathbf{0}\) first — the quickest way to rule out a subspace.

    "Vectors whose coordinates sum to 1" fails immediately.

  • One-line closure test — verify \(au + bw \in U\).

    Do this instead of listing axioms; it is a complete proof.

  • Recognise the space — polynomials, matrices and functions are vector spaces too.

    Everything proved here applies to them verbatim.

Layer 2 · Counting directions

Span, Independence & Dimension

What "\(n\)-dimensional" actually means.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Span \(\operatorname{span}(S)\) — all linear combinations of vectors in \(S\).

    Two non-parallel arrows in the plane span the whole plane: any point is some mix of them.

  • Spanning set — a set whose span is all of \(V\).

    Enough directions to reach everywhere. Possibly with waste.

  • Linear independence — \(c_1v_1+\cdots+c_kv_k = \mathbf{0}\) forces every \(c_i = 0\).

    No arrow is a mix of the others; nothing is redundant.

  • Basis — an independent spanning set.

    Exactly enough directions: no gaps, no duplicates.

  • Standard basis \(e_1,\dots,e_n\) of \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\).

    East, north, up. \(e_i\) is 1 in slot \(i\) and 0 elsewhere.

  • Coordinates \([v]_B\) — the unique coefficient list of \(v\) in a basis \(B\).

    "3 east, 2 north" — the vector is the place, the coordinates are the description.

  • Dimension \(\dim V\) — the number of vectors in any basis.

    The number of independent choices you can make. This is the first honest definition of "\(n\)-dimensional".

  • Finite- vs infinite-dimensional.

    \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\) is finite-dimensional; the space of all polynomials is not. Chapter 13 shows how differently the two behave.

  • Codimension \(\dim V - \dim U\).

    A plane in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) has codimension 1 — one constraint. Hyperplanes are the codimension-1 subspaces.

Theorems

  • Span is the smallest subspace containing \(S\).

    Take your arrows and close up under mixing — you get exactly the span.

    A linear combination of linear combinations of \(S\) is again one, so \(\operatorname{span}(S)\) is a subspace containing \(S\). Any subspace containing \(S\) is closed under linear combinations, so it contains \(\operatorname{span}(S)\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Unique representation: \(B\) is a basis iff every \(v\) is a linear combination of \(B\) in exactly one way.

    Coordinates are well defined precisely because the basis is independent.

    Spanning gives at least one representation. If \(\sum c_iv_i = \sum d_iv_i\), subtracting gives \(\sum(c_i-d_i)v_i = \mathbf{0}\), and independence forces \(c_i = d_i\). Conversely, uniqueness applied to \(\mathbf{0}\) — which is the trivial combination — rules out any non-trivial dependency. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Steinitz exchange lemma: an independent set is never larger than a spanning set.

    You can't have more independent directions than you have directions in total. Everything about dimension follows from this.

    Let \(u_1,\dots,u_m\) be independent and \(w_1,\dots,w_n\) spanning. Write \(u_1\) in terms of the \(w\)'s; some coefficient is non-zero, so that \(w\) can be swapped out for \(u_1\) and the set still spans. Repeat: at step \(k\), \(u_k\) is a combination of \(u_1,\dots,u_{k-1}\) and the remaining \(w\)'s, and independence forbids all the \(w\)-coefficients vanishing, so another exchange is possible. If \(m > n\) the \(w\)'s run out, and then \(u_{n+1}\) would be a combination of \(u_1,\dots,u_n\) — contradicting independence. Hence \(m \le n\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Dimension is well defined: any two bases of \(V\) have the same size.

    The plane is 2-dimensional however you choose your axes.

    Let \(B, B'\) be bases. \(B\) is independent and \(B'\) spans, so \(|B| \le |B'|\) by exchange; swapping the roles gives \(|B'|\le|B|\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Extension and reduction: every independent set extends to a basis; every spanning set contains one.

    Add missing directions, or throw away redundant ones — either route reaches a basis.

    (Finite case.) If an independent set doesn't span, some \(v\) lies outside its span; adding \(v\) keeps independence, and dimension bounds the process, so it terminates at a basis. If a spanning set is dependent, some vector is a combination of the others and can be deleted without changing the span; repeat until independent. \(\blacksquare\)

  • In an \(n\)-dimensional space: any \(n\) independent vectors form a basis, and so do any \(n\) spanning vectors.

    With the right count, you only have to check one of the two conditions.

    If \(n\) independent vectors failed to span, they could be extended to a basis of size \(> n\), contradicting well-definedness of dimension. If \(n\) spanning vectors were dependent, one could be deleted, giving a spanning set of size \(n-1\) and hence — by exchange against a basis — \(n \le n-1\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Subspace dimension: \(U \subseteq V\) gives \(\dim U \le \dim V\), with equality iff \(U = V\).

    A plane inside 3-space is smaller in the only sense that matters here.

    A basis of \(U\) is an independent subset of \(V\), so exchange bounds its size by \(\dim V\). If the sizes agree, that basis of \(U\) is an independent set of full size in \(V\), hence a basis of \(V\), so \(U = \operatorname{span} = V\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Dimension formula: \(\dim(U+W) = \dim U + \dim W - \dim(U\cap W)\).

    Two planes in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) meeting in a line: \(2+2-1 = 3\), the whole space. Inclusion–exclusion for dimensions.

    Take a basis \(C\) of \(U\cap W\), extend it to a basis \(C\cup A\) of \(U\) and to \(C \cup B\) of \(W\). Then \(C\cup A\cup B\) spans \(U+W\). It is independent: a dependency puts a vector of \(\operatorname{span}(A)\) inside \(W\), hence inside \(U\cap W = \operatorname{span}(C)\), forcing the \(A\)-coefficients to vanish, and similarly for \(B\). Counting gives the formula. \(\blacksquare\)

  • \(\dim(U\oplus W) = \dim U + \dim W\).

    Independent pieces simply add their dimensions — the source of \(\dim \mathbb{R}^{n} = n\).

    Immediate from the dimension formula with \(\dim(U\cap W) = 0\). Concretely, the union of bases of \(U\) and \(W\) is a basis of the direct sum. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Every vector space has a basis (with the Axiom of Choice).

    Even \(\mathbb{R}\) as a \(\mathbb{Q}\)-vector space. No one has ever written such a basis down.

    Order the independent subsets of \(V\) by inclusion. The union of a chain of independent sets is independent, since any dependency involves finitely many vectors, all lying in a single link. Zorn's lemma (Chapter 2, Layer 9) supplies a maximal independent set \(B\); if some \(v \notin \operatorname{span}(B)\), then \(B\cup\{v\}\) would be independent and larger. So \(B\) spans. \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Test independence by solving \(\sum c_iv_i = \mathbf{0}\).

    Only the all-zeros solution means independent. This is a linear system — Layer 4 solves it mechanically.

  • Count first — compare the number of vectors with the dimension before working.

    Three vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) are dependent, no calculation needed.

  • Extend or trim — build a basis by adding missing directions or deleting redundant ones.

    Both routes are legitimate; pick whichever end you're nearer.

  • Use the dimension formula — to find the size of an intersection you can't see.

    Rearranged, it computes \(\dim(U\cap W)\) from three easier numbers.

Layer 3 · Maps that respect the structure

Linear Maps

The only functions that see a vector space as a vector space.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Linear map \(T(au+bv) = aT(u)+bT(v)\).

    Rotation, projection, scaling. Not translation — that moves the origin.

  • Kernel \(\ker T = \{v : T(v) = \mathbf{0}\}\).

    What gets crushed to nothing. A projection onto the floor has the vertical axis as kernel.

  • Image (range) \(\operatorname{im} T = \{T(v)\}\).

    Where the map can actually land.

  • Rank & nullity — \(\dim \operatorname{im} T\) and \(\dim\ker T\).

    How much survives, and how much is lost. They always add to the starting dimension.

  • Isomorphism — a bijective linear map.

    A perfect renaming: the two spaces are the same object in different clothes.

  • Endomorphism & automorphism — a linear map from \(V\) to itself; an invertible one.

    Rotating the room. Chapter 5's determinant measures what such a map does to volume.

  • Projection — \(P\) with \(P^{2} = P\).

    Shadow on the floor: casting it twice changes nothing.

  • Space of linear maps \(\mathcal{L}(V,W)\) — itself a vector space.

    You can add two rotations pointwise and get a linear map. Its dimension is \(\dim V \cdot \dim W\).

  • Linear functional — a linear map \(V \to \mathbb{R}\).

    "Total cost" as a function of quantities. Layer 6 is devoted to these.

  • Invariant subspace — \(U\) with \(T(U)\subseteq U\).

    A direction the map keeps to itself; the seed of eigenvector theory in Chapter 5.

Theorems

  • \(T(\mathbf{0}) = \mathbf{0}\), and kernel and image are subspaces.

    Linear maps always fix the origin — which is why translation isn't linear.

    \(T(\mathbf{0}) = T(0\cdot\mathbf{0}) = 0\,T(\mathbf{0}) = \mathbf{0}\). If \(u,v \in \ker T\) then \(T(au+bv) = aT(u)+bT(v) = \mathbf{0}\); if \(T(u), T(v)\in\operatorname{im}T\) then \(aT(u)+bT(v) = T(au+bv)\) is in the image. \(\blacksquare\)

  • A linear map is determined by its values on a basis, and those may be assigned freely.

    Say where east and north go, and the whole plane's fate is sealed.

    If \(v = \sum c_ib_i\) then linearity forces \(T(v) = \sum c_iT(b_i)\), so the values on \(B\) determine \(T\). Conversely, given arbitrary targets \(w_i\), define \(T(\sum c_ib_i) := \sum c_iw_i\); this is well defined because coordinates are unique, and it is linear by inspection. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Injective \(\iff\) trivial kernel.

    Nothing is lost exactly when nothing is crushed to zero.

    If \(T\) is injective and \(T(v) = \mathbf{0} = T(\mathbf{0})\), then \(v = \mathbf{0}\). Conversely, if \(\ker T = \{\mathbf{0}\}\) and \(T(u)=T(v)\), then \(T(u-v)=\mathbf{0}\), so \(u - v = \mathbf{0}\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Rank–nullity theorem: \(\dim V = \dim\ker T + \dim\operatorname{im}T\).

    Whatever a map crushes, it loses from its output. The single most used theorem in linear algebra.

    Take a basis \(k_1,\dots,k_p\) of \(\ker T\) and extend it to a basis \(k_1,\dots,k_p,u_1,\dots,u_q\) of \(V\). The vectors \(T(u_1),\dots,T(u_q)\) span \(\operatorname{im}T\), since the \(k_i\) contribute nothing. They are independent: \(\sum c_jT(u_j) = \mathbf{0}\) puts \(\sum c_ju_j\) in \(\ker T\), so it is a combination of the \(k_i\), and independence of the full basis forces all \(c_j = 0\). Hence \(\dim\operatorname{im}T = q\) and \(\dim V = p + q\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Corollary: for \(T : V \to V\) with \(V\) finite-dimensional, injective \(\iff\) surjective \(\iff\) bijective.

    In finite dimensions there is no room to lose or gain. In infinite dimensions this fails badly.

    Injective means nullity 0, so rank \(= \dim V\) by rank–nullity, so the image is a subspace of full dimension, i.e. all of \(V\). Conversely surjective gives rank \(=\dim V\), hence nullity 0. (The shift map on infinite sequences is injective but not surjective.) \(\blacksquare\)

  • Two spaces are isomorphic iff they have the same dimension.

    Every real \(n\)-dimensional space is \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\) once you choose a basis.

    An isomorphism carries a basis to a basis, so dimensions agree. Conversely, if \(\dim V = \dim W = n\), pick bases \((b_i)\) and \((c_i)\) and let \(T(b_i) = c_i\); by the determination theorem this defines a linear map, which is surjective (it hits a spanning set) and injective (rank–nullity), hence an isomorphism. \(\blacksquare\)

  • \(\mathcal{L}(V,W)\) has dimension \(\dim V\cdot\dim W\).

    Which is exactly the number of entries in an \(m\times n\) matrix — no coincidence.

    Fix bases \((b_j)\) of \(V\) and \((c_i)\) of \(W\), and let \(E_{ij}\) be the map sending \(b_j \mapsto c_i\) and every other basis vector to \(\mathbf{0}\). These span, since any \(T\) equals \(\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}E_{ij}\) where \(T(b_j) = \sum_i a_{ij}c_i\); and they are independent, since such a combination applied to \(b_j\) returns the \(j\)-th column of coefficients. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Solutions of \(T(x)=b\) form the affine set \(x_0 + \ker T\), when non-empty.

    One particular solution plus every way of going nowhere. This is "particular + homogeneous", proved.

    If \(T(x_0)=b\), then \(T(x) = b \iff T(x - x_0) = \mathbf{0} \iff x - x_0 \in \ker T\). \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Define a map on a basis — the cleanest way to build a linear map.

    Choose where the basis goes; linearity does the rest.

  • Compute the kernel by solving \(T(x)=\mathbf{0}\).

    Then rank–nullity gives the image dimension for free.

  • Rank–nullity as a counting argument.

    Two of the three numbers always determine the third — often the fastest route to an answer.

Layer 4 · The calculation

Matrices & Linear Systems

Linear maps in coordinates, and how to actually solve them.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Matrix — an \(m\times n\) array of scalars; the coordinate form of a map \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}\).

    Column \(j\) is where the \(j\)-th basis vector lands. That single sentence explains matrices.

  • Matrix–vector product \(Ax = \sum_j x_j (\text{column } j)\).

    A recipe applied to the columns: \(x\) says how much of each column to take.

  • Matrix product \(AB\) — the matrix of the composition \(T_A \circ T_B\).

    Do \(B\) then \(A\). The strange row-times-column rule is forced by that requirement.

  • Transpose \(A^{\mathsf T}\) — rows and columns swapped.

    Reflect across the diagonal. In Chapter 6 it becomes the adjoint.

  • Identity & inverse \(I\), \(A^{-1}\).

    Do nothing; undo. Only square matrices of full rank have inverses.

  • Row space, column space, null space.

    Column space = image; null space = kernel. The row space is the column space of the transpose.

  • Rank of a matrix — the dimension of its column space.

    How many genuinely different directions the columns point in.

  • Elementary row operations — swap, scale, add a multiple of one row to another.

    The three legal moves. Each is invertible, which is why they preserve the solution set.

  • Row echelon & reduced row echelon form.

    A staircase of leading entries. RREF is unique, so it is a genuine canonical form.

  • Pivot & free variables.

    Pivots are determined; free variables are the dials you may still turn — one per dimension of the kernel.

  • Homogeneous system \(Ax=\mathbf{0}\).

    Always has the zero solution. The question is only whether it has others.

  • Augmented matrix \([A\mid b]\).

    Carry the right-hand side along for the ride while you row-reduce.

  • Trace \(\operatorname{tr}A = \sum a_{ii}\).

    Sum of the diagonal. Basis-independent, and in Chapter 5 it turns out to measure infinitesimal volume change.

Theorems

  • Matrices \(\leftrightarrow\) linear maps: the correspondence is an isomorphism of vector spaces, turning composition into matrix product.

    Matrices aren't a separate subject — they're linear maps written in coordinates.

    Fix bases. Sending \(T\) to the matrix whose \(j\)-th column is \([T(b_j)]\) is linear and bijective (Layer 3's determination theorem). For composition, \([S\circ T](b_j) = S(\sum_i a_{ij}c_i) = \sum_k(\sum_i s_{ki}a_{ij})d_k\), whose coefficient is exactly the \((k,j)\) entry of the product \(SA\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Matrix multiplication is associative but not commutative.

    Rotate then reflect \(\ne\) reflect then rotate — as anyone who has assembled furniture knows.

    Associativity is inherited from composition of functions (Chapter 2, Layer 5), which is associative. Non-commutativity is shown by one example: a \(90^\circ\) rotation and a reflection in the \(x\)-axis compose differently in the two orders. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Row operations preserve the solution set and the row space.

    Why Gaussian elimination is legal rather than merely convenient.

    Each elementary operation is left multiplication by an invertible elementary matrix \(E\); then \(Ax = b \iff EAx = Eb\), because \(E\) can be undone. Each operation replaces rows by linear combinations of rows and is reversible, so the span of the rows is unchanged. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Every matrix has a unique reduced row echelon form.

    A canonical answer — so two matrices are row-equivalent exactly when their RREFs agree.

    Existence is the Gaussian elimination algorithm. Uniqueness: the pivot columns are exactly those not in the span of the earlier columns, a property of the column relations, which row operations preserve; and once the pivot positions are fixed, the reduced entries are the unique coordinates of the non-pivot columns in terms of the pivot ones. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Row rank = column rank.

    The rows and the columns of any matrix carry the same amount of independence, however lopsided the shape.

    Row-reduce to echelon form \(R\). The non-zero rows of \(R\) are independent and span the row space, so the row rank is the number of pivots. The pivot columns of the original \(A\) are independent, and every non-pivot column is a combination of earlier pivot columns (read off from \(R\)), so the column rank is also the number of pivots. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Rouché–Capelli: \(Ax = b\) is solvable iff \(\operatorname{rank}A = \operatorname{rank}[A\mid b]\), and then the solutions form an affine space of dimension \(n - \operatorname{rank}A\).

    The system is consistent exactly when \(b\) adds no new direction to the columns.

    \(Ax=b\) is solvable iff \(b\) lies in the column space, i.e. iff appending \(b\) doesn't increase the rank. Given one solution \(x_0\), the solution set is \(x_0 + \ker A\) (Layer 3), and rank–nullity gives \(\dim\ker A = n - \operatorname{rank}A\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Invertibility criteria: for a square \(A\), the following are equivalent — invertible; \(\operatorname{rank} = n\); \(\ker = \{\mathbf 0\}\); RREF is \(I\); columns form a basis; \(Ax = b\) is uniquely solvable for every \(b\).

    Six ways of saying one thing. Chapter 5 adds a seventh: \(\det A \neq 0\).

    A cyclic argument. Invertible \(\Rightarrow\) \(\ker = \{\mathbf 0\}\) (apply \(A^{-1}\)); trivial kernel \(\Rightarrow\) rank \(n\) by rank–nullity \(\Rightarrow\) the columns are \(n\) independent vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^n\), hence a basis (Layer 2) \(\Rightarrow\) every \(b\) is uniquely a combination of columns, i.e. unique solvability \(\Rightarrow\) the RREF has a pivot in every column and every row, so it is \(I\) \(\Rightarrow\) the product of the elementary matrices used is an inverse of \(A\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Inverse facts: \((AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}\), \((A^{\mathsf T})^{-1} = (A^{-1})^{\mathsf T}\), \((AB)^{\mathsf T} = B^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}\).

    Socks and shoes: to undo, reverse the order.

    \((AB)(B^{-1}A^{-1}) = A(BB^{-1})A^{-1} = AA^{-1} = I\), and similarly on the other side. For the transpose product, compare entries: \(((AB)^{\mathsf T})_{ij} = (AB)_{ji} = \sum_k a_{jk}b_{ki} = \sum_k (B^{\mathsf T})_{ik}(A^{\mathsf T})_{kj}\). Transposing \(AA^{-1}=I\) then gives the middle identity. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Trace is linear and \(\operatorname{tr}(AB) = \operatorname{tr}(BA)\), hence basis-independent.

    A number attached to the map itself, not to your choice of axes.

    \(\operatorname{tr}(AB) = \sum_i\sum_k a_{ik}b_{ki} = \sum_k\sum_i b_{ki}a_{ik} = \operatorname{tr}(BA)\). Hence \(\operatorname{tr}(P^{-1}AP) = \operatorname{tr}(APP^{-1}) = \operatorname{tr}(A)\). \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Gaussian elimination — row-reduce \([A\mid b]\) to echelon form and back-substitute.

    One algorithm answers solvability, uniqueness, rank, kernel and inverse.

  • Gauss–Jordan for the inverse — row-reduce \([A \mid I]\) to \([I \mid A^{-1}]\).

    The same moves that clean up \(A\) build its undo.

  • Read the kernel off the RREF — one basis vector per free variable.

    Set one free variable to 1 and the rest to 0; repeat.

  • Block multiplication — multiply matrices in chunks when the structure is clear.

    Saves work and reveals structure — used heavily for Jacobians in Chapter 10.

Layer 5 · Same map, new clothes

Change of Basis & Quotients

Telling the map apart from the coordinates you happened to choose.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Change of basis matrix \(P\) — columns are the new basis vectors in old coordinates.

    A dictionary between two descriptions of the same room.

  • Coordinate change \([v]_{B'} = P^{-1}[v]_B\).

    The vector never moves; only its description does. The inverse is the classic trap.

  • Similar matrices \(A' = P^{-1}AP\).

    Two matrices for the same map, seen from different axes.

  • Equivalent matrices \(A' = QAP\) — allowing different bases at each end.

    With that much freedom, rank is the only thing that survives.

  • Invariant — a quantity unchanged by a change of basis.

    Rank, trace, and (Chapter 5) the determinant. These describe the map, not the notation.

  • Quotient space \(V/U\) — vectors modulo a subspace.

    Ignore height: two points are "the same" if they differ only vertically. What remains is the floor plan.

  • Complement — \(W\) with \(V = U \oplus W\).

    Always exists, never unique: many different planes complement the same line.

  • Canonical form — a preferred representative of each equivalence class of matrices.

    RREF for row-equivalence; the rank normal form for full equivalence.

Theorems

  • Change of basis formula: if \(A\) represents \(T\) in \(B\), then \(P^{-1}AP\) represents it in \(B'\).

    Translate into the old language, act, translate back.

    Let \(P\) send \(B'\)-coordinates to \(B\)-coordinates, i.e. \([v]_B = P[v]_{B'}\). Then \([T(v)]_{B'} = P^{-1}[T(v)]_B = P^{-1}A[v]_B = P^{-1}AP[v]_{B'}\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Similarity is an equivalence relation, and rank and trace are similarity invariants.

    Whatever axes you choose, the map's rank and trace read the same.

    Reflexivity via \(P = I\); symmetry by inverting \(P\); transitivity by multiplying the two change matrices. Rank is invariant because \(P\) is invertible, so \(P^{-1}AP\) and \(A\) have images of the same dimension. Trace is invariant by \(\operatorname{tr}(AB)=\operatorname{tr}(BA)\) (Layer 4). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Rank normal form: every \(m\times n\) matrix of rank \(r\) is equivalent to \(\begin{pmatrix} I_r & 0\\ 0&0\end{pmatrix}\).

    Choose good bases at both ends and every linear map looks like "keep \(r\) coordinates, delete the rest".

    Take a basis \(k_1,\dots,k_{n-r}\) of \(\ker T\) and extend by \(u_1,\dots,u_r\) to a basis of \(V\). Then \(T(u_1),\dots,T(u_r)\) is a basis of the image (rank–nullity proof, Layer 3); extend it to a basis of \(W\). In these bases \(T(u_i)\) is the \(i\)-th basis vector and \(T(k_j)=\mathbf 0\), which is exactly the stated matrix. \(\blacksquare\)

  • \(V/U\) is a vector space with \(\dim(V/U) = \dim V - \dim U\).

    Forgetting a subspace costs exactly its dimension.

    Cosets \(v + U\) partition \(V\) (Chapter 2, Layer 4), and \((v+U)+(w+U) := (v+w)+U\), \(a(v+U) := av+U\) are well defined because \(U\) is closed under the operations. If \(u_1,\dots,u_k\) is a basis of \(U\), extend to \(u_1,\dots,u_k,v_1,\dots,v_m\) of \(V\); the classes \(v_j + U\) span \(V/U\) and are independent, since a vanishing combination puts \(\sum c_jv_j\) in \(U\), forcing all \(c_j = 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • First isomorphism theorem: \(V/\ker T \cong \operatorname{im}T\).

    Crush what the map crushes, and what remains is exactly the image. Rank–nullity, restated.

    Define \(\bar T(v + \ker T) = T(v)\). It is well defined (two representatives differ by a kernel element), linear, surjective onto \(\operatorname{im}T\), and injective: \(\bar T(v+\ker T) = \mathbf 0\) means \(v \in \ker T\), i.e. the class is zero. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Complements exist (finite dimensions; with AC in general) but are not unique.

    Any plane not containing the line will do — and there are infinitely many.

    Extend a basis of \(U\) to one of \(V\); the span of the added vectors is a complement, since together they span \(V\) and independence forces the intersection to be trivial. Non-uniqueness: in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) with \(U\) the \(x\)-axis, both the \(y\)-axis and the line \(y=x\) are complements. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Projections split the space: \(P^{2}=P\) gives \(V = \operatorname{im}P \oplus \ker P\).

    Shadow plus what was lost casting it — and each vector splits exactly one way.

    Write \(v = Pv + (v - Pv)\); the first term is in the image, and \(P(v-Pv) = Pv - P^2v = \mathbf 0\), so the second is in the kernel. If \(x\) lies in both, \(x = Py\) gives \(x = Py = P^{2}y = Px = \mathbf 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Choose a basis that suits the map — half of linear algebra is picking coordinates.

    Diagonalisation (Chapter 5) is exactly this idea taken to its conclusion.

  • Sandwich with \(P^{-1}\ldots P\) — translate, act, translate back.

    Get the direction of \(P\) right: columns are the new basis in old coordinates.

  • Quotient to forget — factor out what you don't want to see.

    The standard move for reducing a problem to its essential dimensions.

Layer 6 · The shadow space

Duality

Measuring devices instead of arrows — the language forms and integration will need.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Dual space \(V^{*} = \mathcal{L}(V,\mathbb{R})\) — all linear functionals on \(V\).

    Vectors are things; functionals are meters that read them. Chapter 16's differential forms are built from these.

  • Dual basis \(b^{*}_i\) with \(b_i^{*}(b_j) = \delta_{ij}\).

    The \(i\)-th meter reads off the \(i\)-th coordinate and ignores the rest.

  • Annihilator \(U^{0} = \{f \in V^{*} : f|_U = 0\}\).

    All the meters blind to a given subspace. It becomes the orthogonal complement once Chapter 6 supplies an inner product.

  • Transpose (dual) map \(T^{*}(f) = f\circ T\).

    Push the meter backwards through the map. Its matrix is the transpose — that's where the name comes from.

  • Double dual \(V^{**}\) — the dual of the dual.

    In finite dimensions it is \(V\) again, and canonically so: a vector is what a meter reads.

  • Hyperplane — the kernel of a non-zero functional.

    One linear equation, one dimension lost. Chapter 19's separating hyperplanes are exactly these.

  • Bilinear form — linear in each argument separately.

    The dot product is one. Chapter 5's determinant is multilinear in \(n\) arguments.

Theorems

  • The dual basis is a basis: \(\dim V^{*} = \dim V\) in finite dimensions.

    As many independent meters as there are independent directions.

    Independence: applying \(\sum_i c_ib^{*}_i = 0\) to \(b_j\) gives \(c_j = 0\). Spanning: for any \(f\), the functional \(\sum_i f(b_i)b^{*}_i\) agrees with \(f\) on every basis vector, hence equals \(f\) by Layer 3's determination theorem. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Separation by functionals: if \(v \ne \mathbf{0}\), some \(f \in V^{*}\) has \(f(v) \ne 0\).

    No non-zero vector is invisible to every meter.

    Extend \(\{v\}\) to a basis (Layer 2) and take the dual basis functional corresponding to \(v\); it sends \(v\) to 1. \(\blacksquare\)

  • \(\dim U^{0} = \dim V - \dim U\).

    Every dimension you ignore is a dimension of meters that ignore it.

    Extend a basis \(u_1,\dots,u_k\) of \(U\) to \(u_1,\dots,u_k,w_1,\dots,w_m\) of \(V\). A functional annihilates \(U\) exactly when its first \(k\) dual coordinates vanish, so \(U^{0} = \operatorname{span}(w_1^{*},\dots,w_m^{*})\), of dimension \(m = \dim V - \dim U\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Canonical embedding: \(V \to V^{**}\), \(v \mapsto (f \mapsto f(v))\), is an isomorphism in finite dimensions.

    No choice of basis is needed — unlike the isomorphism \(V \cong V^{*}\), which always requires one.

    The map is linear, and injective by the separation result. Since \(\dim V^{**} = \dim V^{*} = \dim V\), injectivity forces surjectivity (Layer 3's corollary). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Matrix of the dual map is the transpose, and \(\operatorname{rank}T^{*} = \operatorname{rank}T\).

    Row rank equals column rank, seen structurally rather than computationally.

    With \(T(b_j) = \sum_i a_{ij}c_i\), we get \(T^{*}(c^{*}_i)(b_j) = c^{*}_i(T(b_j)) = a_{ij}\), so \(T^{*}(c^{*}_i) = \sum_j a_{ij}b^{*}_j\) — the transpose. For rank, \(\ker T^{*} = (\operatorname{im}T)^{0}\), so \(\dim\ker T^{*} = \dim W - \operatorname{rank}T\), and rank–nullity in \(W^{*}\) gives \(\operatorname{rank}T^{*} = \operatorname{rank}T\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Hyperplanes are exactly the codimension-1 subspaces.

    "One linear equation" and "one dimension less" say the same thing.

    If \(f \ne 0\) then \(\operatorname{im}f = \mathbb{R}\), so rank–nullity gives \(\dim\ker f = \dim V - 1\). Conversely, if \(\dim U = \dim V - 1\), then \(\dim U^{0} = 1\), and any non-zero \(f \in U^{0}\) has \(\ker f \supseteq U\) of the same dimension, hence \(\ker f = U\). \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Describe a subspace by equations — list the functionals that kill it.

    Span form for building, equation form for testing membership. Both are useful; convert freely.

  • Dualise a statement — swap subspaces for annihilators and maps for their transposes.

    Half the theorems in this chapter come in matched pairs this way.

  • Evaluate on a basis — a functional is determined by \(n\) numbers.

    That's all a row vector ever was.
