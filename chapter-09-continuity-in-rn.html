Continuity in \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\)

❮ Chapter 8 — Topology of \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\) Start Learning ❯

Many Variables at Once

On the line there are two ways to approach a point. In the plane there are infinitely many — and that single fact is what makes multivariable limits delicate.

Once continuity is pinned down, Chapter 8's compactness and connectedness do the rest: values are attained, continuity becomes uniform, and intermediate values are hit.

The last layer moves up a level, to sequences of functions — the setting Chapters 12 and 15 need.

Layer 1 · Approaching from everywhere

Limits in Several Variables

Why one direction is never enough.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Limit \(\lim_{x\to a}f(x) = L\) — for every \(\varepsilon\) there is \(\delta\) with \(0<\|x-a\|<\delta \Rightarrow \|f(x)-L\|<\varepsilon\).

    Chapter 3's definition with \(|\cdot|\) replaced by \(\|\cdot\|\) — and now "close to \(a\)" means inside a ball, not an interval.

  • Cluster point of the domain — where a limit may be asked about.

    You can't ask what a function tends to at an isolated point; there is nothing to approach along.

  • Path (directional) limit — the limit along a chosen curve into \(a\).

    Approach the origin along the \(x\)-axis, the \(y\)-axis, or a parabola — three answers is entirely possible.

  • Iterated limit \(\lim_{x\to a}\lim_{y\to b}f(x,y)\).

    Take the limits one variable at a time. Both can exist, agree, and still not be the real limit.

  • Vector-valued function \(f : \mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}\), with components \(f_1,\dots,f_m\).

    A wind map: at each point of the ground, a velocity vector.

  • Scalar field & vector field — \(m=1\); \(m=n\).

    Temperature in a room; the airflow in it.

  • Little-o and big-O — \(g = o(h)\) means \(g/h \to 0\); \(g = O(h)\) means \(g/h\) stays bounded.

    "Negligible compared to" and "no worse than". Chapter 10's derivative is defined with the first.

  • Limit at infinity — \(\|x\|\to\infty\).

    One "point at infinity" in every direction at once, unlike \(\pm\infty\) on the line.

Theorems

  • Limits are unique, and the limit is componentwise.

    A vector limit is just \(m\) ordinary limits, computed separately.

    Uniqueness is Chapter 7, Layer 2, applied to the Euclidean metric. For components, \(|f_i(x)-L_i| \le \|f(x)-L\| \le \sum_j|f_j(x)-L_j|\); the left inequality gives one direction, the right (with \(\varepsilon/m\) for each component) the other. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Sequential criterion: \(\lim_{x\to a}f = L\) iff \(f(x_k)\to L\) for every sequence \(x_k \to a\) with \(x_k \ne a\).

    The practical tool: to disprove a limit, produce two sequences with different answers.

    Chapter 7, Layer 2's argument applies unchanged: the forward direction is immediate, and if the \(\varepsilon\)–\(\delta\) condition fails for some \(\varepsilon_0\), choosing \(x_k\) within \(1/k\) of \(a\) but with \(\|f(x_k)-L\|\ge\varepsilon_0\) gives a counterexample sequence. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Algebra of limits: sums, scalar multiples, products, quotients and dot products behave as expected.

    All inherited from \(\mathbb{R}\) via the componentwise theorem.

    By the sequential criterion each statement reduces to the corresponding one for sequences of real numbers (Chapter 3, Layer 3), applied component by component; for the dot product use bilinearity and boundedness of convergent sequences. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Path test: if two paths into \(a\) give different limits, the limit does not exist.

    \(f(x,y) = \frac{xy}{x^{2}+y^{2}}\) gives 0 along each axis and \(\tfrac12\) along \(y=x\). No limit at the origin.

    If the limit were \(L\), the sequential criterion would force every sequence approaching \(a\) — in particular sequences along each path — to have image tending to \(L\). Two different path limits therefore contradict existence. \(\blacksquare\)

  • All straight-line limits agreeing is not enough.

    \(f(x,y)=\frac{x^{2}y}{x^{4}+y^{2}}\) tends to 0 along every straight line through the origin, but to \(\tfrac12\) along \(y = x^{2}\).

    Along \(y = mx\), \(f = \frac{mx^{3}}{x^{4}+m^{2}x^{2}} = \frac{mx}{x^{2}+m^{2}} \to 0\); along \(x=0\), \(f \equiv 0\). But on \(y=x^{2}\), \(f = \frac{x^{4}}{2x^{4}} = \tfrac12\) for \(x \ne 0\). So no limit exists despite every line agreeing. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Iterated limits may exist and disagree with the limit.

    Order of limits matters — the same warning as Chapter 3's rearrangement theorem, in a new costume.

    For \(f(x,y) = \frac{x^{2}-y^{2}}{x^{2}+y^{2}}\), the inner limit as \(y\to0\) is 1, so the iterated limit is 1; taking \(x\to0\) first gives \(-1\). Since the two iterated limits differ, no joint limit exists (a joint limit would force both to equal it). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Squeeze theorem: \(\|f(x)-L\| \le g(x)\) with \(g(x)\to0\) forces \(f(x)\to L\).

    The positive tool: to prove a limit exists, bound the whole thing by something that clearly dies.

    Given \(\varepsilon\), take \(\delta\) making \(g(x) < \varepsilon\) on the punctured ball; then \(\|f(x)-L\| < \varepsilon\) there. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Polar coordinates test: if \(|f(r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta) - L| \le h(r)\) with \(h(r)\to0\) uniformly in \(\theta\), the limit is \(L\).

    The uniformity is the whole point — a bound depending on \(\theta\) proves nothing.

    Since \(\|(x,y)\| = r\), the punctured ball of radius \(\delta\) is exactly \(0 < r < \delta\). Choosing \(\delta\) with \(h(r)<\varepsilon\) for \(r < \delta\) bounds \(|f - L|\) by \(\varepsilon\) at every such point, whatever \(\theta\) is. \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Try two paths first — the fastest way to show a limit does not exist.

    Axes, then \(y = mx\), then \(y = x^{2}\). One disagreement ends the question.

  • Squeeze to prove existence — bound \(\|f - L\|\) by a function of \(\|x-a\|\) alone.

    \(\left|\frac{x^{2}y}{x^{2}+y^{2}}\right| \le |y| \le \|(x,y)\| \to 0\).

  • Switch to polar coordinates — and check the bound is uniform in the angle.

    Powers of \(r\) fall out immediately; the \(\theta\)-dependence is where the traps are.

  • Work componentwise — split a vector limit into \(m\) scalar ones.

    Nothing about the vector case is genuinely new once this is done.

Layer 2 · No jumps

Continuity

Three equivalent definitions, and which to use when.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Continuity at a point — \(\lim_{x\to a}f(x) = f(a)\).

    The value is where the surrounding values are heading.

  • Continuity on a set — continuous at each point; equivalently, preimages of open sets are open.

    The second version needs no \(\varepsilon\) and is what Chapter 8 uses.

  • Uniform continuity — one \(\delta\) serving every point.

    \(1/x\) on \((0,1)\) is continuous but not uniformly: no single \(\delta\) survives near 0.

  • Lipschitz map — \(\|f(x)-f(y)\| \le K\|x-y\|\).

    Every linear map is Lipschitz, with \(K = \|A\|\), the operator norm.

  • Separately continuous — continuous in each variable with the others fixed.

    Strictly weaker than continuity — the standard trap in several variables.

  • Coordinate functions & projections.

    \(\pi_i(x)=x_i\). Continuous, and the building block for showing polynomials are continuous.

  • Support of a function — the closure of \(\{f \ne 0\}\).

    Where the function actually does something. "Compact support" is a standing hypothesis from Chapter 12 onward.

  • Bump function — smooth, non-negative, with small compact support.

    A gentle hill that is exactly flat outside a small disc. Built from \(e^{-1/t}\), and essential in Chapter 15.

Theorems

  • Componentwise continuity: \(f\) is continuous iff every \(f_i\) is.

    A moving point traces a continuous path exactly when each coordinate does.

    Immediate from Layer 1's componentwise limit theorem applied at each point — or from Chapter 8's product characterisation of continuity into a product. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Algebra of continuity: sums, products, quotients (non-vanishing denominator), compositions and dot products of continuous maps are continuous.

    Every polynomial in several variables is continuous; every rational function is, off its zero set.

    By the sequential criterion each case reduces to the algebra of limits (Layer 1). For compositions use the preimage criterion: \((g\circ f)^{-1}(W) = f^{-1}(g^{-1}(W))\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Linear maps are continuous, indeed Lipschitz, with \(\|Ax - Ay\| \le \|A\|\|x-y\|\).

    In finite dimensions there is no such thing as a discontinuous linear map. In infinite dimensions there is.

    Writing \(x = \sum x_ie_i\), \(\|Ax\| \le \sum_i|x_i|\|Ae_i\| \le \bigl(\sum_i\|Ae_i\|\bigr)\|x\|_\infty\), so \(A\) is bounded, hence Lipschitz by linearity: \(\|Ax-Ay\| = \|A(x-y)\|\le\|A\|\|x-y\|\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Norms and the distance function are continuous.

    Length varies smoothly with the vector — used silently in almost every proof.

    The reverse triangle inequality gives \(\bigl|\|x\|-\|y\|\bigr| \le \|x-y\|\), so the norm is Lipschitz with constant 1; the same holds for \(x \mapsto d(x,A)\) (Chapter 7, Layer 2). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Separately continuous \(\not\Rightarrow\) continuous.

    \(f(x,y)=\frac{xy}{x^2+y^2}\) (with \(f(0,0)=0\)) is continuous in \(x\) for fixed \(y\) and vice versa, yet has no limit at the origin.

    With \(y\) fixed and non-zero, \(f\) is a rational function of \(x\) with non-vanishing denominator, hence continuous; the same with roles swapped, and along either axis \(f\equiv0\). But Layer 1 showed the joint limit at the origin does not exist, so \(f\) is not continuous there. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Continuity is a topological property — it depends only on the open sets, hence not on which equivalent norm is used.

    Taxicab or Euclidean, the continuous functions are the same.

    Continuity is characterised by open preimages (Chapter 8, Layer 2), and equivalent norms generate the same open sets (Chapter 6, Layer 4). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Gluing lemma: continuous functions agreeing on the overlap of two closed pieces glue to a continuous function.

    Define a function piecewise and, provided the pieces are closed and match on the seam, it stays continuous.

    Let \(X = A\cup B\) with \(A,B\) closed and \(f|_A, f|_B\) continuous. For closed \(C\) in the target, \(f^{-1}(C) = (f|_A)^{-1}(C)\cup(f|_B)^{-1}(C)\), each closed in a closed set, hence closed in \(X\); a finite union of closed sets is closed. So preimages of closed sets are closed. \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Build from known continuous pieces — projections, norms, polynomials, then compose.

    Almost never necessary to touch an \(\varepsilon\).

  • Use preimages of open sets.

    Especially for compositions and for proving sets open or closed.

  • Find a Lipschitz constant — the strongest cheap form of continuity.

    For a differentiable \(f\), a bound on \(\|Df\|\) gives one (Chapter 10).

  • Glue on closed pieces — the standard piecewise construction.

    On open pieces gluing also works; on half-open ones it fails.

Layer 3 · The great guarantees

Global Theorems

What compactness and connectedness buy you.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Attained maximum — a point where the function actually reaches its supremum.

    Not merely approached. On \((0,1)\), \(f(x)=x\) has a supremum it never reaches.

  • Coercive function — \(f(x)\to\infty\) as \(\|x\|\to\infty\).

    The trick for optimising on all of \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\): coercivity confines the search to a compact ball.

  • Proper map — preimages of compact sets are compact.

    Nothing runs off to infinity. The right hypothesis when the domain is unbounded.

  • Homeomorphism onto the image.

    A continuous injection on a compact set is automatically one — a small miracle worth remembering.

  • Uniform continuity on compact sets.

    The upgrade that makes integration work in Chapter 12.

Theorems

  • Extreme value theorem: a continuous real function on a compact \(K \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{n}\) attains its maximum and minimum.

    The warmest point of a closed room really exists.

    \(f(K)\) is compact (Chapter 8, Layer 3), hence closed and bounded in \(\mathbb{R}\); a non-empty closed bounded set contains its supremum and infimum, and their preimages are the extremisers. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Corollary — coercive functions attain a minimum on \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\).

    Every quadratic bowl has a lowest point, even though space is unbounded.

    Pick any \(x_0\) and let \(c = f(x_0)\). Coercivity gives \(R\) with \(f(x) > c\) whenever \(\|x\| > R\), so the infimum over \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\) equals the infimum over the closed ball \(\overline{B}(0,R)\), which is compact — and there the minimum is attained. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Heine–Cantor: continuity on a compact set is uniform.

    A local promise made everywhere becomes a single global promise.

    Chapter 8, Layer 3's Lebesgue number argument, applied to the cover by preimages of \(\varepsilon/2\)-balls. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Continuous maps on compact sets are bounded and their images are compact.

    Compactness is the hypothesis that stops anything running away.

    Chapter 8, Layer 3: continuous images of compact sets are compact, hence closed and bounded. \(\blacksquare\)

  • A continuous bijection from a compact space to a Hausdorff space is a homeomorphism.

    You get the continuity of the inverse for free — no need to prove it.

    It suffices that \(f\) is a closed map. A closed subset \(C\) of a compact \(K\) is compact, so \(f(C)\) is compact, hence closed in a Hausdorff space. Therefore \((f^{-1})^{-1}(C) = f(C)\) is closed for every closed \(C\), which is continuity of \(f^{-1}\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Intermediate value theorem: a continuous real function on a connected set attains every intermediate value.

    Somewhere on a connected map of the country, the temperature is exactly 15°C — if it is 10 somewhere and 20 elsewhere.

    Chapter 8, Layer 4: the image is a connected subset of \(\mathbb{R}\), hence an interval. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Borsuk–Ulam (stated): every continuous \(f : S^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}\) has a pair of antipodal points with equal values.

    At any moment there are two opposite points on Earth with identical temperature and pressure.

    Sketch: consider \(g(x) = f(x)-f(-x)\), which is odd. If \(g\) never vanished, \(x\mapsto g(x)/\|g(x)\|\) would be a continuous odd map \(S^{n}\to S^{n-1}\); a degree (or homology) argument shows no such map exists. Hence \(g(x_0)=\mathbf 0\) for some \(x_0\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Brouwer's fixed point theorem (stated): a continuous self-map of a compact convex set in \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\) has a fixed point.

    Stir the tea however you like: some point of the liquid ends where it began.

    Sketch: Chapter 7, Layer 4 gives the ball case via the impossibility of a retraction onto the sphere. A compact convex set with non-empty interior is homeomorphic to a closed ball, and fixed-point existence is preserved by homeomorphism; degenerate cases follow by working in the affine hull. \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Check compactness before optimising — closed and bounded, or coercive.

    Skip this and you may spend hours looking for a maximum that isn't there.

  • Restrict to a compact ball — using coercivity or decay.

    Turns a global problem into one the extreme value theorem can handle.

  • Use connectedness for existence — sign changes give solutions.

    The multivariable descendant of "the graph must cross the axis".

  • Compact + injective + continuous \(\Rightarrow\) homeomorphism.

    A standing shortcut in Chapters 14–15 when checking that a parametrisation is well behaved.

Layer 4 · Functions as points

Sequences of Functions

A whole space whose points are functions — and when it is compact.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Pointwise convergence — \(f_k(x)\to f(x)\) for each \(x\).

    Every point settles, but possibly at wildly different speeds.

  • Uniform convergence — \(\sup_x\|f_k(x)-f(x)\|\to0\).

    The whole graph settles together. This is convergence in the sup norm.

  • Sup norm \(\|f\|_\infty = \sup_x\|f(x)\|\).

    Turns the set of bounded functions into a normed vector space — complete, by Chapter 7.

  • Equicontinuity — one \(\delta\) works for every function in the family at once.

    A family with a common speed limit. The missing ingredient for compactness.

  • Uniformly bounded family — one bound for all of them.

    Bounded in the sup norm — necessary, but on its own not enough for compactness.

  • Algebra of functions — a family closed under \(+\), \(\times\) and scalars.

    Polynomials form one; so do trigonometric polynomials.

  • Separates points — for \(x\ne y\) some member of the family has \(f(x)\ne f(y)\).

    The family can tell any two points apart. Without this it can't approximate everything.

Theorems

  • Uniform limits of continuous functions are continuous.

    Pointwise is not enough: \(x^{k}\) on \([0,1]\) has a discontinuous limit.

    The \(\varepsilon/3\) argument of Chapter 3, Layer 5, works verbatim with \(|\cdot|\) replaced by \(\|\cdot\|\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • \(\bigl(C(K,\mathbb{R}^{m}),\|\cdot\|_\infty\bigr)\) is a Banach space for compact \(K\).

    Functions become points of a complete space, so the fixed point theorem applies to them.

    Continuous functions on a compact set are bounded (Layer 3), so the sup norm is finite; the norm axioms are pointwise. Completeness: a sup-Cauchy sequence is pointwise Cauchy, hence has a pointwise limit, and the Cauchy estimate passes to the limit to give uniform convergence — whose limit is continuous by the previous theorem. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Weierstrass M-test: if \(\|f_k\|_\infty \le M_k\) with \(\sum M_k < \infty\), then \(\sum f_k\) converges uniformly.

    The workhorse for showing a series of functions defines a continuous function.

    The partial sums satisfy \(\bigl\|\sum_{k=n}^{m}f_k\bigr\|_\infty \le \sum_{k=n}^{m}M_k\), which is small for large \(n\) by the Cauchy criterion for \(\sum M_k\). So the partial sums are Cauchy in the sup norm, and the previous theorem supplies the limit. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Dini's theorem: a monotone pointwise-convergent sequence of continuous functions on a compact set converges uniformly.

    Monotonicity plus compactness upgrade pointwise to uniform for free.

    Let \(g_k = f_k - f \downarrow 0\) pointwise, each continuous. Given \(\varepsilon\), the sets \(U_k = \{x : g_k(x)<\varepsilon\}\) are open and increasing, and cover \(K\) by pointwise convergence. A finite subcover is a single \(U_N\), so \(g_k < \varepsilon\) everywhere for \(k \ge N\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Arzelà–Ascoli: a family in \(C(K)\) is relatively compact iff it is uniformly bounded and equicontinuous.

    The Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem for functions — and the reason many differential equations have solutions.

    Sketch. (\(\Leftarrow\)) Take a countable dense \(\{x_j\}\subseteq K\). Uniform boundedness plus a diagonal argument extracts a subsequence converging at every \(x_j\). Equicontinuity then upgrades this to uniform convergence: split \(\|f_{k}(x)-f_{l}(x)\|\) into three pieces via a nearby \(x_j\), each under \(\varepsilon/3\). (\(\Rightarrow\)) A compact set in \(C(K)\) is bounded, and total boundedness gives a finite \(\varepsilon\)-net whose members' individual (uniform) continuity yields a common \(\delta\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Stone–Weierstrass: a subalgebra of \(C(K,\mathbb{R})\) containing the constants and separating points is dense.

    Every continuous function on a closed box is a uniform limit of polynomials — however jagged it looks.

    Sketch: first show the closure of such an algebra is closed under \(\max\) and \(\min\), using that \(|t|\) is a uniform limit of polynomials on a bounded interval. Then, given \(f\) and \(\varepsilon\), separation and the constants provide for each pair \(x,y\) a member agreeing with \(f\) at both; taking finitely many maxima and minima over a compactness argument produces a member within \(\varepsilon\) of \(f\) everywhere. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Uniform convergence permits swapping limits with integrals (on compact sets).

    Chapter 12 replaces this with far better theorems — but on a compact interval this one already does a lot.

    \(\bigl|\int_K f_k - \int_K f\bigr| \le \int_K\|f_k - f\| \le \|f_k-f\|_\infty\operatorname{vol}(K)\to0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Estimate the sup norm — to prove or disprove uniform convergence.

    Find the worst point: often where the derivative of \(f_k - f\) vanishes.

  • M-test — bound each term by a constant and sum the constants.

    Three lines, and a series of functions is continuous.

  • Diagonal extraction — converge at countably many points, then upgrade.

    The engine of Arzelà–Ascoli and of many compactness proofs.

  • Approximate by polynomials — prove the result for polynomials, then pass to the limit.

    Stone–Weierstrass turns a hard general statement into an algebraic one.
