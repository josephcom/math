Distance, and Nothing Else

Chapter 6 built distance out of an inner product. This chapter keeps the distance and throws everything else away.

Three axioms remain — and they are enough for convergence, continuity, completeness and a fixed point theorem that solves differential equations.

The payoff is reach: the same theorems now apply to \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\), to spaces of functions, to strings of text, and to graphs.

Layer 1 · Three axioms

Metrics & Balls

What a distance has to satisfy, and what it may otherwise be.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Metric space \((X,d)\) — a set with a distance function obeying three axioms.

    Points and the distances between them. No addition, no scaling, no angles.

  • Open ball \(B(x,r) = \{y : d(x,y) < r\}\).

    Everything within \(r\) of \(x\), edge excluded. The basic building block of every later chapter.

  • Closed ball & sphere — \(\le r\), and \(= r\).

    In \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\) the sphere is the skin; Chapter 13 measures it.

  • Bounded set — contained in some ball.

    Fits inside a big enough circle, wherever you centre it.

  • Diameter \(\operatorname{diam}(A) = \sup\{d(x,y) : x,y\in A\}\).

    The width of the set at its widest. Chapter 18 builds Hausdorff measure out of diameters.

  • Distance between sets \(d(A,B) = \inf\{d(a,b)\}\).

    Can be 0 for disjoint sets — the \(x\)-axis and the graph of \(1/x\) never meet, yet get arbitrarily close.

  • Subspace metric — restrict \(d\) to a subset.

    The surface of the Earth with straight-line distance through the rock. A legitimate metric, if not a useful one.

  • Product metric — e.g. \(d\bigl((x_1,x_2),(y_1,y_2)\bigr) = \max_i d_i(x_i,y_i)\).

    How \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\) is assembled from \(n\) copies of \(\mathbb{R}\).

  • Discrete metric — \(d(x,y) = 1\) for \(x \ne y\).

    Every point is an island. The standard source of counterexamples.

  • Isometry — a distance-preserving bijection.

    The same space with the points renamed.

  • Metrics from norms — \(d(x,y) = \|x-y\|\).

    Every norm gives a metric. Not every metric comes from a norm — the discrete one doesn't.

  • Equivalent metrics — metrics with the same convergent sequences and the same open sets.

    All the \(p\)-metrics on \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\) are equivalent (Chapter 6, Layer 4), so they define the same analysis.

Axioms — A Metric

  • Identity of indiscernibles — \(d(x,y) \ge 0\), and \(d(x,y) = 0\) iff \(x=y\).

    Zero distance means the same place. Drop this and you get a pseudometric, where distinct points can coincide.

  • Symmetry — \(d(x,y) = d(y,x)\).

    There and back are the same length — false for one-way streets, which is why traffic models drop this axiom.

  • Triangle inequality — \(d(x,z) \le d(x,y)+d(y,z)\).

    Detours never help. This is the only axiom with real content.

Theorems

  • Reverse triangle inequality: \(|d(x,z)-d(y,z)| \le d(x,y)\).

    Moving a small step changes your distance to anywhere by at most that step.

    From \(d(x,z)\le d(x,y)+d(y,z)\) we get \(d(x,z)-d(y,z)\le d(x,y)\); swapping \(x\) and \(y\) and using symmetry gives the other direction. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Balls are open, and every open set is a union of balls.

    The balls form a base: knowing them is knowing the whole topology (Chapter 8).

    Let \(y \in B(x,r)\) and put \(s = r - d(x,y) > 0\). If \(d(y,z) < s\) then \(d(x,z)\le d(x,y)+d(y,z) < r\), so \(B(y,s)\subseteq B(x,r)\). Hence every point of a ball is interior. Given an open set \(U\), each \(x \in U\) has a ball \(B(x,r_x)\subseteq U\), and \(U = \bigcup_{x\in U}B(x,r_x)\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Metrics separate points (Hausdorff): distinct points have disjoint balls around them.

    Two different places can always be told apart by a small enough neighbourhood. This is what makes limits unique.

    Let \(x \ne y\) and \(r = d(x,y)/2 > 0\). If \(z\) lay in both \(B(x,r)\) and \(B(y,r)\), the triangle inequality would give \(d(x,y)\le d(x,z)+d(z,y) < 2r = d(x,y)\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • \(d\) is continuous, in each variable and jointly.

    Distance itself varies smoothly — which is why "nearest point" arguments behave.

    By the reverse triangle inequality, \(|d(x,y) - d(x',y')| \le d(x,x') + d(y,y')\). So \(d\) is Lipschitz with constant 1 in the product metric, hence continuous. \(\blacksquare\)

  • The \(p\)-metrics on \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\) are metrics, and are all equivalent.

    Taxicab, Euclidean and maximum distance disagree on numbers but agree on every limit.

    Each \(\|\cdot\|_p\) is a norm by Minkowski's inequality (Chapter 6, Layer 4), and \(d_p(x,y) = \|x-y\|_p\) inherits the three axioms — the triangle inequality from the norm's. Equivalence follows from the norm comparison constants there, which sandwich each metric between multiples of another. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Any set carries a metric — the discrete one.

    So "metric space" is no restriction at all by itself; the content is always in which metric.

    Non-negativity and symmetry are clear. For the triangle inequality, if \(x = z\) the left side is 0; otherwise it is 1, and at least one of \(d(x,y), d(y,z)\) must be 1 since \(y\) cannot equal both \(x\) and \(z\). \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Verify the three axioms — and expect all the work to be in the triangle inequality.

    Non-negativity and symmetry are usually one line each.

  • Draw the unit ball — it tells you what the metric thinks "close" means.

    A diamond, a disc, a square — same points, three geometries.

  • Replace a metric by an equivalent one — pick whichever makes the estimate easy.

    In \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\), \(\max_i|x_i - y_i|\) is nearly always the easiest.

Layer 2 · Getting close

Convergence & Continuity

Chapter 3's definitions, with \(|x-y|\) replaced by \(d(x,y)\).

Concepts & Definitions

  • Convergence \(x_n \to x\) — \(d(x_n,x)\to 0\).

    Word for word Chapter 3's definition, with the distance abstracted.

  • Limit point (cluster point) of a set — every ball around it meets the set in another point.

    0 is a limit point of \(\{1/n\}\), though it isn't in the set.

  • Isolated point — a point with a ball containing nothing else of the set.

    In the discrete metric every point is isolated, so the only convergent sequences are eventually constant.

  • Closure \(\overline{A}\) — \(A\) together with its limit points.

    Sealing the edges: \(\overline{(0,1)} = [0,1]\).

  • Dense subset — closure is the whole space.

    \(\mathbb{Q}\) in \(\mathbb{R}\). Every point is approximable by the dense set — the basis of all numerical work.

  • Separable space — has a countable dense subset.

    \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\) is separable, thanks to points with rational coordinates.

  • Continuity at a point — for all \(\varepsilon\) there is \(\delta\) with \(d(x,a)<\delta \Rightarrow d(f(x),f(a))<\varepsilon\).

    Small input change, small output change — measured by the two metrics involved.

  • Uniform continuity — one \(\delta\) works everywhere.

    A single promise for the whole space, not one per point.

  • Lipschitz map — \(d(f(x),f(y)) \le K\,d(x,y)\).

    A hard speed limit. Lipschitz \(\Rightarrow\) uniformly continuous \(\Rightarrow\) continuous, and neither arrow reverses.

  • Contraction — Lipschitz with \(K < 1\).

    Every application shrinks distances. Layer 4 shows this forces a fixed point.

  • Homeomorphism — a continuous bijection with continuous inverse.

    Same shape, possibly different distances: a circle and a square are homeomorphic, not isometric.

Theorems

  • Limits are unique.

    A sequence cannot settle in two places — the Hausdorff property is what forbids it.

    If \(x_n \to x\) and \(x_n \to y\) with \(x \ne y\), take \(r = d(x,y)/2\). Beyond some index the terms lie in \(B(x,r)\) and beyond another in \(B(y,r)\), so beyond both they lie in the intersection — which is empty by Layer 1. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Convergent sequences are bounded, and so is any Cauchy sequence.

    Settling down means never having been far away for long.

    Take \(\varepsilon = 1\) and the corresponding index \(N\): the terms beyond \(N\) lie in \(B(x,1)\) (or within 1 of \(x_N\) in the Cauchy case), and the finitely many earlier terms lie in a large enough ball around \(x\). The larger radius bounds all of them. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Closure by sequences: \(x \in \overline{A}\) iff some sequence in \(A\) converges to \(x\); and \(A\) is closed iff it contains all its sequential limits.

    In metric spaces you may always argue with sequences instead of neighbourhoods — a luxury general topology loses.

    If every ball \(B(x,1/n)\) meets \(A\), choose \(a_n\) in the intersection to get \(a_n \to x\). Conversely, a sequence in \(A\) converging to \(x\) puts points of \(A\) in every ball around \(x\), so \(x\in\overline A\). The closed-set statement is this applied to \(A = \overline A\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Sequential characterisation of continuity: \(f\) is continuous at \(a\) iff \(x_n\to a \Rightarrow f(x_n)\to f(a)\).

    The quickest way to disprove continuity: find one bad sequence.

    (\(\Rightarrow\)) Given \(\varepsilon\), take \(\delta\) from continuity and then \(N\) with \(d(x_n,a)<\delta\). (\(\Leftarrow\)) If continuity fails for some \(\varepsilon_0\), then for each \(n\) there is \(x_n\) with \(d(x_n,a)<1/n\) but \(d(f(x_n),f(a))\ge\varepsilon_0\), giving a sequence that converges while its image does not. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Topological characterisation: \(f\) is continuous iff preimages of open sets are open, iff preimages of closed sets are closed.

    No \(\varepsilon\), no \(\delta\), no sequences. Chapter 8 adopts this as the definition.

    If \(f\) is continuous and \(V\) is open, take \(x \in f^{-1}(V)\) and \(\varepsilon\) with \(B(f(x),\varepsilon)\subseteq V\); continuity gives \(\delta\) with \(f(B(x,\delta))\subseteq B(f(x),\varepsilon)\subseteq V\), so \(B(x,\delta)\subseteq f^{-1}(V)\). Conversely, apply the hypothesis to \(V = B(f(a),\varepsilon)\): its preimage is open and contains \(a\), so it contains some \(B(a,\delta)\). The closed version follows by taking complements (Chapter 2, Layer 5). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Compositions of continuous maps are continuous; Lipschitz \(\Rightarrow\) uniformly continuous \(\Rightarrow\) continuous.

    A chain of well-behaved steps is well behaved.

    For composition, use the preimage criterion: \((g\circ f)^{-1}(W) = f^{-1}(g^{-1}(W))\), open as the preimage of an open set. For the implications, \(\delta = \varepsilon/K\) serves uniformly in the Lipschitz case, and a uniform \(\delta\) is in particular a \(\delta\) at each point. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Equivalent metrics give the same convergent sequences, open sets and continuous functions.

    Analysis on \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\) does not depend on which \(p\)-norm you like.

    If \(c\,d_1 \le d_2 \le C\,d_1\), then \(d_1(x_n,x)\to0 \iff d_2(x_n,x)\to0\) by the sandwich, and every \(d_1\)-ball contains a \(d_2\)-ball and vice versa, so the open sets agree. Continuity, characterised by open sets, follows. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Distance to a set is Lipschitz: \(x \mapsto d(x,A)\) has constant 1, and vanishes exactly on \(\overline A\).

    A ready-made continuous function attached to any set — the standard tool for building bump functions and partitions of unity (Chapter 15).

    For any \(a\in A\), \(d(x,A)\le d(x,a)\le d(x,y)+d(y,a)\); taking the infimum over \(a\) gives \(d(x,A)\le d(x,y)+d(y,A)\), and symmetrically, so \(|d(x,A)-d(y,A)|\le d(x,y)\). And \(d(x,A) = 0\) means points of \(A\) come arbitrarily close to \(x\), i.e. \(x\in\overline A\). \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Argue with sequences — in a metric space this is always legitimate.

    Usually shorter than \(\varepsilon\)–\(\delta\), and much easier to get right.

  • Use preimages for continuity — no estimates at all.

    The proof that compositions are continuous shrinks to one line.

  • Find a Lipschitz constant — the strongest easy form of continuity.

    A bounded derivative gives one immediately, by the mean value theorem.

  • Use \(d(x,A)\) — to turn a set into a continuous function.

    Separating two closed sets, cutting off a function smoothly, measuring nearness — all from this one formula.

Layer 3 · No holes

Completeness

Chapter 3's completeness axiom, restated so it travels.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Cauchy sequence — for every \(\varepsilon\) there is \(N\) with \(d(x_n,x_m)<\varepsilon\) for \(n,m > N\).

    The terms bunch together, without reference to any limit point.

  • Complete space — every Cauchy sequence converges.

    \(\mathbb{R}\) is complete; \(\mathbb{Q}\) is not — a sequence of rationals can bunch around \(\sqrt2\) and have nowhere to land.

  • Completion \(\hat X\) — the smallest complete space containing \(X\) densely.

    \(\mathbb{R}\) is the completion of \(\mathbb{Q}\). Filling the holes is always possible, and essentially in one way.

  • Banach space — a complete normed vector space.

    \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\) with any norm; \(C[a,b]\) with the sup norm. The natural home of analysis.

  • Hilbert space — a complete inner product space.

    Everything from Chapter 6 plus no holes. Where Fourier series live.

  • Nowhere dense set — its closure has empty interior.

    A line inside the plane: thin from every angle.

  • Meagre set — a countable union of nowhere dense sets.

    "Small" in the sense of category. Baire's theorem says a complete space is never meagre in itself.

  • Totally bounded — coverable by finitely many balls of any given radius.

    Stronger than bounded in general spaces; combined with completeness it gives compactness (Chapter 8).

Theorems

  • Convergent \(\Rightarrow\) Cauchy, but not conversely.

    Cauchy is what convergence looks like from the inside — and inside \(\mathbb{Q}\) it can be a mirage.

    If \(x_n\to x\), then \(d(x_n,x_m)\le d(x_n,x)+d(x,x_m)<\varepsilon\) for large \(n,m\). The sequence \(1, 1.4, 1.41, \dots\) in \(\mathbb{Q}\) is Cauchy with no rational limit, so the converse fails. \(\blacksquare\)

  • A Cauchy sequence with a convergent subsequence converges.

    If the crowd is bunching and part of it has settled, all of it has.

    Let \(x_{n_k}\to x\). Given \(\varepsilon\), choose \(N\) with \(d(x_n,x_m)<\varepsilon/2\) for \(n,m>N\), and \(k\) with \(n_k > N\) and \(d(x_{n_k},x)<\varepsilon/2\). Then \(d(x_n,x)\le d(x_n,x_{n_k})+d(x_{n_k},x)<\varepsilon\) for \(n > N\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\) is complete.

    The foundation for everything that follows: limits in \(n\) dimensions never escape.

    If \((x^{(k)})\) is Cauchy in \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\), then \(|x^{(k)}_i - x^{(m)}_i| \le \|x^{(k)}-x^{(m)}\|\), so each coordinate sequence is Cauchy in \(\mathbb{R}\) and converges (Chapter 3, Layer 3). Let \(x\) collect the limits; then \(\|x^{(k)}-x\|\le\sqrt n\max_i|x^{(k)}_i - x_i|\to0\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • A closed subset of a complete space is complete, and a complete subspace is closed.

    Completeness passes down to closed pieces, and only to those.

    A Cauchy sequence in a closed \(A\subseteq X\) converges in \(X\) by completeness, and its limit lies in \(A\) by closedness. Conversely, if \(A\) is complete and \(x \in \overline A\), take \(a_n \to x\) with \(a_n \in A\); the sequence is Cauchy, so it converges in \(A\), and uniqueness of limits puts \(x \in A\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • \(C[a,b]\) with the sup metric is complete.

    Uniform limits of continuous functions are continuous (Chapter 3), so nothing escapes the space. This is what makes Layer 4's applications possible.

    A sup-Cauchy sequence \((f_n)\) is pointwise Cauchy, so \(f(x) := \lim f_n(x)\) exists. Letting \(m\to\infty\) in \(\sup_x|f_n(x)-f_m(x)|\le\varepsilon\) gives \(\sup_x|f_n(x)-f(x)|\le\varepsilon\), i.e. uniform convergence; and a uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Cantor's intersection theorem: in a complete space, nested non-empty closed sets with diameters \(\to 0\) meet in exactly one point.

    The nested interval theorem, freed from \(\mathbb{R}\).

    Pick \(x_n \in F_n\). For \(m > n\), both points lie in \(F_n\), so \(d(x_n,x_m)\le\operatorname{diam}F_n\to0\): the sequence is Cauchy, hence convergent to some \(x\). Each \(F_n\) is closed and contains the tail, so \(x\in F_n\) for every \(n\). Two such points would force \(\operatorname{diam}F_n \ge d > 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Every metric space has a completion, unique up to isometry.

    The construction of \(\mathbb{R}\) from \(\mathbb{Q}\), performed in general.

    Sketch: take Cauchy sequences in \(X\) and declare two equivalent when \(d(x_n,y_n)\to0\) — an equivalence relation (Chapter 2, Layer 4). Define \(\hat d([x],[y]) = \lim d(x_n,y_n)\), which exists because the reals are complete and is well defined by the reverse triangle inequality. Embed \(X\) by constant sequences; the image is dense, and \(\hat X\) is complete since a Cauchy sequence of classes can be represented by a diagonal sequence. Uniqueness: a dense isometric embedding extends uniquely to the completions. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Baire category theorem: in a complete space, a countable intersection of dense open sets is dense.

    You cannot exhaust a complete space with countably many thin sets. It powers a surprising number of existence proofs.

    Let \(U_n\) be dense open and \(B_0\) any ball. Inductively choose closed balls \(\overline{B_n}\subseteq B_{n-1}\cap U_n\) with radius \(< 2^{-n}\) — possible since \(U_n\) is dense and open. Cantor's intersection theorem gives a point in every \(\overline{B_n}\), hence in \(B_0\cap\bigcap_nU_n\). So the intersection meets every ball. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Corollary: a complete space is not a countable union of nowhere dense sets; e.g. \(\mathbb{R}\) is uncountable.

    A second proof of uncountability, with no diagonal in sight.

    If \(X = \bigcup_nA_n\) with each \(A_n\) nowhere dense, the sets \(U_n = X\setminus\overline{A_n}\) are dense and open, so \(\bigcap_nU_n\) is dense — in particular non-empty — yet it misses every \(A_n\), contradicting the union being all of \(X\). Applying this with \(A_n\) the singletons of a supposed enumeration of \(\mathbb{R}\) shows \(\mathbb{R}\) is uncountable. \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Prove convergence via Cauchy — when the limit is the object being constructed.

    Solutions of equations, integrals and inverse functions are all built this way.

  • Work in a complete space on purpose — choose \(C[a,b]\) with the sup norm, not the \(L^1\) one.

    Completeness is a resource; pick the metric that has it.

  • Nested closed sets — shrink to a point to prove existence.

    Bisection, Baire, and Chapter 8's compactness proofs all run on this.

  • Baire category argument — show a "generic" object exists by intersecting dense open conditions.

    How one proves that most continuous functions are nowhere differentiable.

Layer 4 · The payoff

Fixed Points

One theorem that solves equations, inverts functions and integrates differential equations.

Concepts & Definitions

  • Fixed point — \(x\) with \(f(x) = x\).

    Stir a cup of tea and put it down: some molecule ends up exactly where it started.

  • Contraction constant \(K < 1\) with \(d(f(x),f(y))\le K\,d(x,y)\).

    Every application shrinks all distances by at least the factor \(K\).

  • Iteration \(x_{n+1} = f(x_n)\).

    Press the same calculator button repeatedly and watch the display settle.

  • Rate of convergence \(d(x_n,x^{*}) \le \frac{K^{n}}{1-K}d(x_0,x_1)\).

    Geometric — each step buys a fixed fraction of the remaining error, and the bound is computable in advance.

  • Picard iteration — solving \(y' = F(x,y)\) by repeatedly integrating.

    Guess a solution, plug it in, integrate, repeat. The contraction theorem says the guesses converge.

  • Newton's method — \(x_{n+1} = x_n - f(x_n)/f'(x_n)\).

    Follow the tangent line to the axis and repeat. Near a simple root it is a contraction, and a very fast one.

Theorems

  • Banach fixed point theorem: a contraction on a non-empty complete metric space has exactly one fixed point, and iteration from anywhere converges to it.

    Existence, uniqueness and an algorithm, from one hypothesis. Few theorems give so much.

    Let \(x_{n+1}=f(x_n)\). Then \(d(x_{n+1},x_n)\le K^{n}d(x_1,x_0)\), so for \(m > n\) the triangle inequality gives \(d(x_n,x_m)\le\sum_{k=n}^{m-1}K^{k}d(x_1,x_0)\le\frac{K^{n}}{1-K}d(x_1,x_0)\to0\): the sequence is Cauchy, hence convergent to some \(x^{*}\) by completeness. A contraction is continuous, so \(f(x^{*}) = \lim f(x_n) = \lim x_{n+1} = x^{*}\). Uniqueness: two fixed points give \(d(x^{*},y^{*}) = d(f(x^{*}),f(y^{*}))\le K\,d(x^{*},y^{*})\), forcing the distance to be 0. \(\blacksquare\)

  • A priori error bound: \(d(x_n,x^{*})\le\frac{K^{n}}{1-K}d(x_0,x_1)\).

    You can decide how many iterations you need before running a single one.

    Let \(m \to \infty\) in the estimate \(d(x_n,x_m)\le\frac{K^{n}}{1-K}d(x_0,x_1)\) obtained above, and use continuity of the metric (Layer 1). \(\blacksquare\)

  • Contraction on a closed ball: if \(f\) contracts \(\overline{B}(x_0,r)\) into itself, the fixed point lies there — and \(d(x_0,f(x_0))\le(1-K)r\) suffices.

    The practical form: you rarely contract the whole space, only a neighbourhood.

    The ball is closed in a complete space, hence complete (Layer 3). For invariance, if \(d(x,x_0)\le r\) then \(d(f(x),x_0)\le d(f(x),f(x_0))+d(f(x_0),x_0)\le Kr + (1-K)r = r\). Banach's theorem then applies to the ball. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Picard–Lindelöf theorem: \(y' = F(x,y)\), \(y(x_0)=y_0\) has a unique local solution when \(F\) is continuous and Lipschitz in \(y\).

    Why differential equations have predictable solutions at all. Drop the Lipschitz condition and uniqueness dies: \(y'=\sqrt{|y|}\) has many solutions through 0.

    The initial value problem is equivalent to the integral equation \(y(x) = y_0 + \int_{x_0}^{x}F(t,y(t))\,dt\). On \(C[x_0-h,x_0+h]\) with the sup metric — complete by Layer 3 — the right-hand side defines a map \(T\), and \(\|Ty_1 - Ty_2\|_\infty \le hL\|y_1-y_2\|_\infty\) using the Lipschitz constant \(L\) of \(F\). Choosing \(h < 1/L\) makes \(T\) a contraction on a suitable closed ball, so it has a unique fixed point — the solution. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Newton's method converges quadratically near a simple root.

    The number of correct digits roughly doubles each step — but only once you are close enough.

    Let \(g(x) = x - f(x)/f'(x)\). Then \(g'(x) = f(x)f''(x)/f'(x)^{2}\), which vanishes at a simple root \(x^{*}\) where \(f(x^{*})=0, f'(x^{*})\ne0\). By Taylor (Chapter 3, Layer 6), \(|g(x)-x^{*}| \le C|x-x^{*}|^{2}\) near \(x^{*}\), which is both a contraction on a small enough ball and the stated quadratic rate. \(\blacksquare\)

  • Brouwer's fixed point theorem (stated): a continuous self-map of a closed ball in \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\) has a fixed point.

    No contraction needed — but also no uniqueness, and no algorithm. Lay a map of the country on the ground anywhere in it and one point sits exactly above its own image.

    Sketch: suppose \(f\) had no fixed point. Then \(r(x)\), the point where the ray from \(f(x)\) through \(x\) meets the sphere, is a continuous retraction of the ball onto its boundary. Such a retraction is impossible — it would make the identity map of the sphere null-homotopic, contradicting the non-vanishing of a topological invariant (degree, or the top homology of \(S^{n-1}\)). \(\blacksquare\)

Methods

  • Rewrite an equation as a fixed point problem — turn \(g(x)=0\) into \(f(x)=x\).

    Many rewritings exist; choose one whose \(f\) contracts.

  • Check the contraction constant — usually a derivative bound.

    \(|f'|\le K < 1\) on an interval gives a contraction by the mean value theorem.

  • Contract a closed ball into itself — the practical form of the hypothesis.

    Verify invariance first; it is the step most often skipped and most often false.

  • Iterate and bound — use the a priori estimate to choose the number of steps.

    Numerical analysis in one line.
